BORDER ZONE

Three Accounts of the Ostnitz Affair


I. THE TRAIN
(as told by the businessman)

It is always a pleasure to leave Frobnia, and the ride is never over too soon. A plane would be
faster, but in that part of the world the rule is better late than never. I import goods from
Frobnia; I have been in worse countries, and I have learned not to complain about the border
crossings.

The train from Frzi to Vienna was in its sixth hour and nearing the border of neutral Litzenburg.
Night was falling, the lights of the villages were coming on, and I was drifting off in my
first-class compartment when someone knocked and a man staggered in, clutching his left arm. There
was blood on his sleeve.

"Don't be frightened," he said. "I'm all right." He was an American agent, he said, and he had
learned of a plot to assassinate a top-ranking American diplomat the next morning in Ostnitz, the
town we were coming into. He gave me a document to pass to his contact at the station. It was in
Frobnian, of which I know a handful of words; that's why I always carry my tourist guide and phrase
book. He pinned a rumpled white carnation to my jacket.

"He will bump into you and greet you with the words 'Forgive me,'" he said, "to which you should
reply 'It is my fault.' Then hand him the document and earn our country's thanks." He glanced over
his shoulder, out of instinct, perhaps, and then he was gone. He left a few drops of blood on my
floor.

I'm a businessman, not a spy. But I know what a border search is like.

My camera bag held an old roll of film and a camera. I put the document on the seat and
photographed it twice, frames 24 and 25. Then I rewound the film, took the colour roll out of the
camera, opened the window, and threw the camera into the dark, and the bag after it. I unpinned the
carnation.

There were guards with machine guns at both ends of the passageway, new since the start of the
trip. A man in a trench coat was working his way down the corridor, compartment by compartment,
talking to the passengers in rapid-fire Frobnian.

In the lavatory I took the last paper towel from the dispenser and wet it under the cold tap. I
left the film and the carnation there. Then I tore the document into a handful of shreds and, just
like in the movies, ate them. They did not go down easily. Back in my compartment I wiped up the
blood with the wet towel, then flushed the towel down the toilet. The man in the trench coat was
two compartments away.

He knocked twice and came in. He spoke beautiful, correct English with a thick accent, and his eyes
took in the whole compartment and then all of me. He took out a cigarette and a book of matches and
failed to strike a match four times, then smiled and put them away. "I should be giving these up
anyway." They were looking, he said, for a tall, slight man, a bit dishevelled, hurt perhaps, for
his own safety. Had I seen such a man?

"No," I said.

He nodded a few times, as if agreeing with me, and left.

We went into a tunnel. When we came out, I saw a figure fall from the roof of the train. I would
swear it was the American.

At the border station they searched everything and dumped the contents of my briefcase on the seat.
There was nothing for them to find. "You have been through worse, I am sure," said the man in the
trench coat, with that unnerving smile, and he told me I could leave the train if I wished.

I went back for the film and the carnation, pinned the flower on, and went down onto the platform.
It was crowded with people, and beyond it, back in Frobnia, the border defences rose in the dark:
guard towers, searchlights, patrols, dogs.

I walked slowly through the crowd. A boy of about twelve backed into me, bowed, and said, "Hartzi
dazi!"

Forgive me. I had looked it up.

"Popka izim," I said. It is my fault.

He made eye contact and looked away, nervously. I was clearly not who he'd expected. But he took
the roll of film and hid it in his clothes, and a moment later he was gone into the crowd.

Two days later, in my hotel in Vienna, I read about an attempted assassination of a high-ranking
American diplomat in the little border town of Ostnitz. Local police had been tipped off just in
time. Nobody gave me a medal. I didn't expect one.


II. THE BORDER
(as told by Topaz)

Cold. Pain.

I wasn't even active. I was just making one of my monthly trips to Vienna to keep up the front:
George Bonnard, Vice President, Eastern Europe, IC Cola. "Drink It Up, Frobnia!" What a laugh.

Then, past Yinsli, I'd spotted Viper on the train, my oldest enemy, dressed as a priest. I'd found
his compartment and his briefcase, and the cable inside it about an assassination. Then he came
back, and there was the fight, and a shot, and now my left arm. I'd given the document to the
businessman in the dining car, the real McCoy, knowing I'd be caught. And in the tunnel I'd jumped.

I came to on the railway tracks in the snow, a few miles short of the border. I tore a strip off my
clothes and tied it round my arm, and the worst of the bleeding stopped.

Northeast, through the forest, smoke was rising from a hut in a clearing. A car was coming. I
worked round to the back door and waited in the cold while the car pulled up, doors slammed, and
voices went to the front. When the occupant stepped outside to meet them, I opened the back door
(it squeaked like the end of the world), slipped into the storeroom, and closed it behind me.

There was a knapsack, a pair of knee boots, work shoes, a can of household oil and a white down
parka. I took everything, oiled that door so it wouldn't give me away again, and put on the boots
and the parka. White, for snow. A guard came round and looked through the back window, but not into
the room. Then the owner came back in, the car's engine started, and somewhere a pack of dogs began
to bay.

I left as the car drove away. The dogs were coming. I crossed a road to the edge of a swamp,
stinking of sulphur, and waded into it in the knee boots, along its edge, until the dogs lost my
scent and their noise faded.

In a woodshed in the forest I found a pair of binoculars hanging by the door, and among the tools a
bolt-cutter and an old pair of rubber gloves. I put the gloves on. Then I walked north to the edge
of the border zone.

Three guard towers stood in a line across a hundred yards of open snow, their searchlights sweeping
round, the middle one faster than the other two. Fences on both sides. I watched the lights go
round, counted, and when the gap came I ran. I made it across to the base of the first fence.

Two guards walked a beat between the fences, out from the middle tower and back, meeting in the
middle and turning away. When they were more than halfway out, I cut a slit in the chain link, bent
it back, and squeezed through without touching it. When they had passed and were walking away from
each other again, I ran to the foot of the middle tower.

I had one piece of equipment left from the old days: a fountain pen that is no pen at all. Take off
the cap and there is a button; every press adds a minute to the timer; put the cap back and nothing
can stop it. It's magnetic. I pressed the button once, stuck the pen to the northwest post, out of
the guards' sight, and capped it. One minute.

Then up the ladder to the door of the guard post. A metal brace ran across from the ladder to a
post just below the top rung, and I stepped onto it and knocked. The door opened. "Who is there?"
The guard stepped down onto the ladder, his leg right by my face, and I took hold of it and pulled.
He fell head first to the ground.

The other guards came running. I climbed into the tower. A machine gun was bolted to the floor, and
the border fence lay to the north, a few feet out of reach and five feet below. Shots came up from
the base of the tower. "It is of no use. Come out, or we shall be forced to kill you! You have
fifteen seconds to decide!"

The pen went off.

There was a long moment of silence. Then the tower trembled and began to fall, and I fell with it.
But the back of the tower caught on the top of the border fence, and for a second I hung in the air
over the border, with sirens and machine-gun fire below. Then the tower went over and landed in a
heap on the far side.

I got up and walked into the dark. I didn't know where I was. Somebody would find me sooner or
later. For now, sleep didn't sound so bad.


III. THE ASSASSINATION
(as told by Viper)

"Vipna homitz, nimso klamitz." A rotten fish will turn up eventually. The Americans put it better,
but the Frobnian fits.

The Kremlin had decided to assassinate the American ambassador to Litzenburg, and since it was a
sensitive affair, I was chosen to supervise: to keep it clean, and keep Moscow's fingerprints off
it. That part was easy. Our Litzenburger friends had found an angry young Iranian more than willing
to do God's work. My real problem was stopping it without blowing my cover. I had not spent ten
years climbing the KGB to start taking stupid chances now. Nobody said it would be easy being a
double agent.

So I boarded the train in a priest's collar, with the coded cable in my pocket. And then Topaz,
headstrong, street-smart and without the slightest idea who I was, waylaid me and took it. If only
he'd had the code, he could have done my work for me. I traded the collar for a trench coat, which
the local authorities like better, and went looking for the cable. I asked a nervous American
businessman whether he had seen a wounded man. He said no. Something wasn't quite right about him,
but there was nothing I could prove.

Now I knew only that it would happen in the town square at Ostnitz, a little after noon.

The square was packed for Constitution Day. A tall column to the war dead stood in the middle, with
a fifteenth-century clock on top, and behind barricades and hundreds of uniformed police a dais had
been built, with the dignitaries already in place. I looked at the buildings around the square.
Despite the winter, the apartment building had three open windows: one on the third floor at the
north end, one on the fourth floor near the middle, and one on the fifth floor near the middle.

In the apartment lobby, beside the elevator, the directory listed the tenants. Three rooms on the
fifth floor were empty, one each on the third and fourth. Only one empty room had an open window:
fifth floor, room D.

I still needed a way in. The KGB's local man was an antique dealer named Riznik, a friend of mine.
Outside his shop I watched a man I had never seen before come out, speaking into a walkie-talkie as
he melted into the crowd. Then I went in.

Riznik greeted me loudly. "Welcome, my friend! How may I assist you? A clock, perhaps? A music
box?" Then, quietly: he was being watched; the authorities knew about our plan; it was at noon, as
the ambassador spoke. "If you're going to get near our sniper, you'll need the password: 'Brzni.'
He is positioned in a window on the..." Two uniformed policemen walked in, and he shoved me through
the door to his back room. I heard them questioning him as I slipped out of the side door.

At the sidewalk cafe on Ostnitz Street, Topaz sat at a table, watchfully waiting. He'd crossed the
border after all. I had to admire it.

I called to him. He turned, saw me, got up and came after me.

Further up the street, a vendor's cart was piled high with soda, pretzels and hot dogs. I pushed it
over. Hundreds of cans went rolling across the cobbles, people fell, people fell over the people
who had fallen, and the crowd began skating about on a carpet of soda cans like ball bearings.
Topaz went down with the rest. I slipped into the blind alley behind the cart.

At the end of the alley, a fire escape hung out of reach on the side of the apartment building. I
stood a foul-smelling trash can under it, climbed up, and knocked the can over as I went. Behind
me, Topaz reached the alley and set the can back up.

I climbed to the fifth floor, opened the window and stepped into the corridor. The clock in the
square struck the hour. Halfway down the hall I dropped something on the floor: a book of matches,
the same one I'd failed to light on the train. Then I knocked on the eastern door.

"Say the word, please. Quickly!"

"Brzni."

A small, dark man let me in, waving a pistol, checking the hall both ways. The room was bare. He
knelt at the window again, motionless, his rifle trained on the dais. "There is not much time," he
said.

I stepped back toward the door, out of the line of fire. Down in the square the national anthem
began. I could hear running footsteps in the hall.

The anthem ended, and the mayor called upon the head of the church of Litzenburg to give a
benediction. The door burst open. Topaz came in with his pistol raised and fired three times, and
the sniper slumped to the floor. Topaz took one careful step toward the window, then another. He
was not three feet in front of me.

I hit him on the back of the head with my pistol, and he dropped.

I looked over the scene: one corpse, and one agent with a lot of potential, a little rough around
the edges. The assassination was thwarted. Topaz would get the credit for the kill. Viper had
barely escaped with his life. There would be no embarrassment for the KGB. There would be forms to
fill out and questions to answer, but I'd survive them.

His story would be worth hearing someday, but it would have to wait.

On my way out I tipped my hat to my unconscious counterpart. "Until we meet again."
