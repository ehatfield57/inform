LEATHER GODDESSES OF PHOBOS

I. Joe's Bar

Upper Sandusky, Ohio, 1936. At a nickel a mug you don't ask for brand names, and my fifth beer at 
Joe's tasted every bit as bad as the first. Joe's is nothing much to look at, but it's the social 
center of Upper Sandusky, which tells you all you need to know about Upper Sandusky. The front door 
was nearly lost behind a haze of neon on the south wall, and two doors at the back said "Ladies" 
and "Gents."

I felt an urge. I'm a man of simple needs, so I went through the door marked Gents.

The Gents' Room had never heard of disinfectant. There was one toilet, one sink, and a three-legged 
stool, and for no reason I can explain I picked up the stool and kept it. Then I took care of 
business. Ahhh. When the crisis had passed, I noticed a strong and familiar smell, and traced it to 
a dubious slice of pizza crumpled in the corner. I went back out to the bar.

I had a stool, a flashlight, a comic book stuck in my back pocket and the overalls I stood up in. I 
can't tell you why I'd brought a flashlight into Joe's Bar, except that the lighting in those 
bathrooms isn't too reliable. I stood around a moment, minding my own business.

A brilliant flash of green light seems less unusual when it's followed by tentacled aliens, and 
that's what this one was followed by. The tentacles wrapped around me, and I fainted.

II. The Cell

Let's cut the crap: seven hours and eight minutes later I woke up. My head felt as if a locomotive 
had run over it, or at least one very large locomotive, and my clothes were gone. In their place I 
wore a brass loincloth.

I was in a big cell with a soft, cushiony floor and a wide door on the south side. A painting of a 
pussy cat hung on the wall. My flashlight and my stool lay on the floor with a blanket, and while I 
was taking that in, somebody shoved a tray through the door. The brown lump on it looked like 
nothing on God's earth, but it smelled like luscious, creamy milk chocolate. I took the tray and 
everything else in the room, painting included.

Then I tried the cell door. It opened. Nobody had locked it.

Outside was the end of a hallway. A narrow cell door faced mine on the south side, a gleaming metal 
door closed off the west end, and the hall ran on to the east. Something told me it would be 
dangerous to go either east or west, so I didn't. A sign by the stairs said "Up To Observation 
Room." I opened the narrow door instead.

A man sitting in the shadows jumped to his feet. "A human! They got you too? I've been here a week. 
When you opened the door, I figured it was a guard! Was it unlocked? I never thought of trying it." 
His name was Trent, he was from Alaska, and he told me cheerfully that he wasn't too bright but was 
strong as an ox and great with his hands. "Maybe we can lick these Leather Goddesses together."

There was a crumpled paper in the corner of his cell, covered in a block of letters that made no 
sense at first: HESOHREBBUR, ILSSSIPNGEF, and so on down to SLLABNOTTOC. I kept it. When I asked 
Trent about our captors he said they were no doubt some gang of interplanetary floozies who got 
their jollies enslaving defenseless planets. "We'll stop 'em!"

III. Through the Black Circle

Upstairs, a window filled the west wall of the Observation Room. It looked down on a big room full 
of hideous experiments on the human anatomy, with a pathetic-looking human as the current subject. 
I won't describe it. The author of my adventures wouldn't either, not in suggestive company.

North of the Observation Room was a closet as black as the inside of a boot. I switched on my 
flashlight. The closet smelled of mothballs; Phobos has a moth problem. A wicker basket sat on a 
shelf near the ceiling, so I set my stool down, stood on it, took the basket, and packed most of my 
things into it. In the corner a black circle about two feet across was painted on the floor.

I stood on it, and fell through a hole in a direction that wasn't down, nor any of the other 
directions I knew.

I came out in hot, steaming jungle. From the heat, the damp, and above all the gigantic Venus 
flytrap sidling toward me, this had to be Venus. A few seconds later Trent's elbow appeared in the 
air, followed almost at once by the rest of him.

That paper had been worrying at me. Read the right way, the letters spelled out that hissing 
frightens flytraps. So I hissed. The flytrap took me for a can of DDT, died of fright, and was 
eaten on the spot by the parasites that live inside flytraps waiting for exactly that.

West of there, in a spawning ground for Venusian slime beasts, a jar of ointment sat untouched 
beside another black circle. While I picked it up, Trent trotted over and flipped me a matchbook 
covered in scrawled notes. "I've got a plan to bring these Leather Goddess jokers to their knees," 
he said. "If we can scrape up these items, I can whip up something that'll knock 'em cold! A 
Super-Duper Anti-Leather Goddesses of Phobos Attack Machine!!!"

So now we had a shopping list.

IV. Saturn

The circle by the slime put us in the cargo hold of a giant spaceship. A glistening sword lay there 
in a velvet case. Trent had just arrived, nose first, when a radium-powered grenade clattered 
across the deck and a shadowy figure in black slipped away. Trent yelled at me to hit the deck and 
threw himself on the grenade.

When the smoke cleared, Trent was spread all over the hold. My eyes filled with tears, and I hung 
my head for a moment to honor my brave, loyal friend, who had given his life that humanity might be 
safe from the Leather Goddesses of Phobos.

Then I took the sword. Through the little viewport I could see Saturn and her rings, and a hundred 
feet off, a small passenger ship blowing steam from her ion engines, getting ready to leave.

The hold opened onto a stable, the flagship being where the Main Attack Fleet's cavalry was kept, 
and the finest mount in it was a white stallion. I climbed on and galloped him down a long corridor 
of pulsing light until we came to the main hatch. A white, form-fitting therma suit hung beside it. 
I put it on, opened the hatch, and floated out into space.

Between the battleship and the little passenger ship, the man in black was trying to force a young 
woman in rich clothes through the small ship's hatch. He was Thorbast, Chief Assassin for the 
Leather Goddesses. He strapped the woman to the hull, drew a long sword, and floated over to me. 
"Ah, the escaped prisoner. Disposing of you will be a small but enjoyable feather in my cap."

A foul smell drifted my way as he talked. The rumors that Thorbast chewed raw garlic were true.

As if things weren't bad enough, a bug-eyed monster swam into view: a cross between a space squid 
and a humanoid tree, with suckers all over its bark and branches waving like tentacles. It took one 
look around and headed for the woman, and while I gaped, Thorbast attacked.

I hit back. I nicked his arm and drew blood. Our blades clanged together. A volley of blows left me 
dizzy, and his blade whizzed an inch over my head. Meanwhile the tree-thing reached the young woman 
and started undressing her, and she started screaming. I nicked him again. Then Thorbast feinted, 
swung straight at my neck, and I parried so hard his sword flew out of his hand and came drifting 
toward me, turning slowly.

I caught it. And then, because I'm the good guy, I handed it back to him.

That did it. Thorbast saw that a gesture like that proved I was the hero and that he hadn't a 
chance, and being a practical man, he saved us both a lot of trouble by falling on his own blade. 
His body drifted off into the black.

I went after the monster with my own sword. It squawked and fled; its bark was worse than its bite. 
I untied the young woman, and she waved for me to follow her into the passenger ship.

She was Elysia, daughter of the richest man in the solar system. She took a photo off the wall (a 
photo of Jean Harlow, as it turned out) and scribbled her father's address on the back. "See him 
the next time you're on Ganymede, and you will be handsomely repaid. But now, I must retire to my 
cabin to recover from this hideous ordeal." She curtsied, a little unsteadily, and shut herself in 
her cabin.

I opened the door. She was standing in the middle of the cabin clutching her clothes. "How dare you 
come in here without knocking! Am I to be allowed no privacy at all?" And then the shocks of the 
day caught up with her and she began to cry, and I took her in my arms, and one thing led to 
another.

Much, much later, I tiptoed out and let her sleep.

Back at the main hatch, someone came running up behind me, panting. It was Trent. "Good, you're 
still here! Thank God those time travellers who wandered by the hold had a matter reconstituter!"

I decided not to ask. We rode the stallion back to the stable together, walked west down the 
endless corridor to an alcove with a black circle in it, and jumped. My therma suit vanished on the 
way.

V. Mars

We came out in midair, high over a desert laced with canals, with the ground coming up very fast. 
Caw! Caw! A pterodactyl snatched us both and set us down in a ruined castle where a large green 
frog sat on a stone block. Something about that frog caught my eye. I'd be back for it.

West across the dunes, past another ruin with a black circle in it, a dock of hickory and dickory 
wood ran out into a canal, and a little white marsmouse was running along it. I held up the 
painting from my cell. It's a good likeness of a pussy, though I'm not sure it's art, and it was 
good enough to freeze the mouse in terror. I picked it up and Trent took it eagerly. A mouse was on 
his list.

The circle in the second ruin dropped us in a damp cellar under the cell block. We climbed back to 
the closet and took its circle to the jungle again. This time, just as we came to a mighty tree, it 
died; Venusian hypertermites ate it to the ground in seconds and left a huge hole. In a clearing 
beyond I found a can of black stain. A path northwest twisted and turned, vines grabbing at my 
ankles, until it spat us out at the back door of a house.

The yard was full of door-to-door salesmen, thrown off the front porch but still hopeful. One of 
them wanted to trade me one of his machines, even up. I offered him the flashlight. He took it 
gladly (he knew a Plutonian plutocrat who'd give a fortune for one) and dropped an odd machine at 
my feet. "It's a TEE remover," he said. Before I could ask what it removed, he shone the flashlight 
up into the sky and a giant Venusian MegaMoth swooped down and carried him off.

VI. The Mad Scientist

When I knocked, the door was flung open by a wild-eyed scientist in glasses thick enough to stop 
gamma rays. "Nein! Nein! Nein! I don't need any!" Then he took a closer look. "Oh! Not ein 
salesman! In fact, just der type I need for mein experiment." He dragged us in, locked the door, 
and dissolved the key in a vat of acid.

His laboratory downstairs had all the trimmings: a cage with a male and a female gorilla in it, two 
slabs for strapping down victims, a huge red power switch, and an energy barrier he switched on 
across the foot of the stairs. Through the bars I could see a rubber hose in the cage. On a hunch, 
I pushed everything I was carrying through the bars into the cage.

The scientist strapped me down on one slab and Trent on the other, and threw the switch.

Suddenly I was inside the cage looking out at myself, still strapped to the slab. I was in the body 
of a gorilla. Trent's body, on the other slab, was rolling its eyes around as if following a fly.

I had a good look at the female gorilla. She wasn't bad-looking at all. We started nuzzling, and 
things got hot and heavy fast. "Ach!" shrieked the scientist. "Mein theory iss correct! Ze sex 
drive uf a species resides in der body, not in der brain!" and he ran off to write it up, I suppose.

I gathered up my things and the hose and ate the chocolate from the tray. Mmmm. With the sugar rush 
running through those gorilla arms, I bent the bars apart and climbed out. My own body, once I 
untied it, leaped for a rafter and came down on the slab with a whump. Trent's body tried to wrap 
its arms around me like tentacles. I dropped everything and threw the big red switch.

Zap. I was myself again, and the gorilla slunk gratefully back into its cage. I helped Trent off 
his slab, picked up our things, and we left by the black circle at the foot of the stairs.

VII. King Mitre

That circle led to a pay vizicomm booth on a Venusian clifftop. I pushed the coin return knob, 
opened the box, and a coin fell out: ten marsmids. The circle at the edge of the cliff took us to 
the Royal Docks of King Mitre on Mars.

Mitre is the one you know as Midas, but the Earth version has it wrong. He didn't wish that 
everything he touched would turn to gold. He wished it would turn to forty-five degree angles, and 
in the end he touched his own daughter. He sat on his throne, dejected and lonely, next to a pile 
of forty-five degree angles, one of which had golden hair and a flowing white gown.

My jar, I now saw, was untangling cream. I put it in the TEE remover, closed it, and switched it 
on. Sparks! Explosions! "Pockita pockita pockita FEEP!" When I opened it again I had a jar of 
unangling cream. I rubbed some on the angle in the gown, and she turned slowly into a beautiful 
princess.

"You have restored my beloved Theta to me!" cried Mitre, weeping with happiness. He gave me a 
perfect eighty-two degree angle, which he'd only brushed against, and reached out to shake my hand. 
As I left, I heard behind me the sound of a forty-five degree angle landing on a pile of forty-five 
degree angles. "Oh damn! Not again!"

An eighty-two degree angle was on Trent's list too.

VIII. The Canal

A royal barge of Martian cedar was tied up at the dock, with a purple button and an orange one. 
Pressing them changed the words written on them; the orange one started us down the canal and, 
pressed again, pulled us in to the next dock.

North of a small sandy dock, an alien warrior lay dead among the dunes. Beside him was a coded 
message and a stick of lip balm, quite unused. Chapped lips, perhaps; it's a dry climate. The 
message read:

VSDFHHQN UXRB VVLN RW UHK JQLNVD BE UHK RW IOHVUXRB BILWQHGL -- SDP WHUFHV HKW WHJ GQD QDWOXV HKW 
IR 9886 UHEPXQ HILZ WFDWQRF RW VL QRLVVLP UXRB

Read backwards, with every letter moved three places back, it said: YOUR MISSION IS TO CONTACT WIFE 
NUMBER 6889 OF THE SULTAN AND GET THE SECRET MAP -- IDENTIFY YOURSELF TO HER BY ASKING HER TO KISS 
YOUR KNEECAPS.

Further down the canal we visited a shopping mall that had been in recession for fifteen thousand 
years. Its one surviving store was an Exit Shop, where a dusty proprietor told us we were only his 
third customers in a hundred and fifty centuries and then went back to sleep. We sailed on round a 
bend to the finest dock I ever saw, laced with jade and ivory, at the foot of the Sultan's palace.

The Sultan has eight thousand three hundred and seventy-nine wives, and his laundry shows it. In 
the laundry room, among the mountains of it, was the last clothes pin on drying day. I took it. In 
the Oriental garden beyond the main hall was a deep stone well. I looked in and saw only blackness. 
Up in the minaret was a black circle, and when we stood on it we landed in a dark, cramped space 
whose floor gave way under us. We dropped into my old cell, which Trent admired. "Why didn't I get 
a cell like this?"

From the roof of the prison I could see the Pleasure Palace of the Leather Goddesses on the 
horizon, and between us and it the warships lined up for the invasion of Earth. A circle on the 
roof took us back to the Martian desert, and the frog.

IX. The Frog

I didn't know what would happen when I kissed that frog, but I wasn't going to take chances. I 
pinned my nose shut with the clothes pin. I smeared the whole stick of lip balm on my lips. I set 
everything down, shut my eyes, and covered my ears with my hands. Then I leaned forward and kissed 
it hard.

When a tongue slid into my mouth, a moment of revulsion gave way to pleasure, because the frog was 
a frog no longer. She was an enchanting princess, and she pulled herself against me.

Some time later, after she'd gone, I gathered up my loincloth and found a common household blender 
on the ground with an engraving on its side. Trent was at the other end of the ruin sifting through 
rubble, oblivious.

We went back through the cell block to the palace and left most of my load in the main hall, then 
took the barge all the way down the canal to a little dock in the Wattz-Upp district, where it was 
getting chilly. We got out, and I sent the barge off empty with the orange button.

West of there was an oasis where water bubbled up around a black circle. A bunny rabbit was 
drinking from it. I picked up the rabbit. Then, while I watched, the circle faded from black to 
white. It was a good thing I'd kept the can of black stain. I painted the circle black again and we 
stood on it.

X. Cleveland

It's a sad thing to say, but after Venus and Mars I missed the slime pits. We were in Cleveland. On 
a muddy little lawn someone had left a canvas sack and a wooden rake, and I took them both. The 
teensy-weensy house beside it was so small its whole first floor was one room. Upstairs the bed was 
unmade, with the sheet half on the floor.

The list wanted a headlight, and the bedroom had an open window over the street. I tore the sheet 
into strips, tied them into a rope, tied the rope to the bed, and threw it out of the window. 
"Super idea!" said Trent. "Doesn't look too strong, though. How many pounds do you weigh?"

"Two hundred," I said.

"That's more than I weigh. Let me do the honors."

He climbed down and unscrewed the headlight, and a truck came barreling down the street, hit him, 
and carried him off. Moments later there was an explosion, and the smoke drifted past the window. I 
hung my head.

A moment later the ceiling caved in, and out of the plaster dust and startled termites stepped 
Trent, dishevelled but mostly alive. The explosion had knocked him into a nutty professor's 
basement, the professor had strapped him into a faster-than-light missile, slavers had caught him 
halfway to Pluto, he'd beaten off about thirty of them, and then he'd found a black circle that 
dropped him into the attic. "Anyway, I got the headlight!"

There was a black circle under the sod in the garden behind the house. I took the trellis from the 
garden as well, and we went back to the palace.

XI. The South Pole

This time I climbed down the palace well. At the bottom the handholds ran out, we jumped, and we 
landed on a black circle that put us in the royal barge, now tied up at the Icy Dock at the far 
southern end of the canal. Trent stepped out onto the ice, gave a whoop, skidded into the canal, 
and was dragged under by the current. I searched for him for a long time, and then I hung my head 
again.

South across the snow, a crowd of penguins blocked the path, one of them waving a sign: "Give 
generously to the Penguin Retirement Fund." I gave them my ten-marsmid coin. They let me through 
and gave me a one-marsmid coin as change.

Beyond the penguins two robot gypsies came out of their tent to offer me oil and silicon stew, and 
a meteorite fell out of the sky and wiped them out. Inside the tent their baby, a little robot, was 
shivering and wailing. "Mommy?" I wrapped it in the blanket from my cell, and it smiled and fell 
asleep. I carried it in the wicker basket south to a big igloo, Martian Orphanages Inc., South 
Polar Branch, and through the barred window I could see a pair of cotton balls in the hall. Cotton 
balls were on the list.

I left the baby in its basket on the doorstep and crept away. A massive, rather cubical matron came 
out, cooed over it, and carried it in. When I went in after her for the cotton balls she came back, 
shouted "Caught you, you baby-stealing gypsy!" and drove me out with blows that would have felled 
an elephant. But I had the cotton balls.

A black circle in an endless expanse of white, put there mostly for the sake of some literary 
allusions, took me back to the Wattz-Upp Dock. At the oasis Trent shot out of the fountain like a 
watermelon seed and landed in a dripping heap at my feet. "Good thing I've always been so good at 
holding my breath."

XII. The Sultan

Back in the palace main hall I handed Trent the cotton balls, the headlight, the angle, the rubber 
hose, the blender and the photo of Jean Harlow. I kept the one-marsmid coin, and took the clothes 
pin off my nose.

No man has ever left the Sultan's audience chamber alive. He sat on his throne and laid down the 
rules: answer his riddle and I could spend an hour with any of his wives; get it wrong, or not 
answer, or leave, or touch him, or let him sneeze, and I would die. He had the tigers kept hungry. 
Then he gave the riddle:

    Some say I'm pointless,
        yet many are obsessed by me.
    I have caused heroic gambles
        and sown endless frustration.
    Uncounted deaths have I caused.
        What am I?

"That's easy!" yelled Trent. "A grapefruit!" The eunuchs snickered, the Sultan cried "Wrongo!", and 
they led Trent away. There was a roar and a scream. I hung my head once more.

"Riddle," I said.

The Sultan looked crestfallen, then asked his lawyers whether they could kill me anyway, and while 
they argued I made for the harem. The guard at the door wasn't impressed. Somebody had solved the 
riddle last year, she said, and now everyone knew the answer; I was the twelfth winner that week. 
"Well, pick a wife; any number between 1 and 8379."

"Six thousand eight hundred and eighty-nine."

The harem smelled of fine perfume. A figure veiled in silk led me deeper in, to a bedroom of exotic 
furs, and let her tunic float to the floor. "For an hour, I am yours."

"Kiss my kneecaps," I said.

"Oh," she whispered, "you're from the rebels!" She laid a secret map at my feet, lit a torch for 
me, and moved some furs aside to show a passage going down. The only way out was through the 
catacombs. As I went, she threw herself into my arms and begged me to be careful.

XIII. The Catacombs

The catacombs were knee-deep in warm, brackish canal water, and every junction looked like every 
other. There was a tap on my shoulder: Trent, brushing tiger fur off himself. A troupe of 
dimension-hopping midgets had come through at the right moment, and the tiger cage led down here.

With the secret map in my hand I picked my way through: northwest, southeast, northeast, a clap, 
east and on. At some junctions I clapped, at some I hopped, and at some I cried "kweepa." The first 
time I cried it, a Martian hawk came flapping up to start a mating dance, took a better look at me, 
rubbed its eyes and flew off. The torch grew dimmer as we went.

In a forgotten storehouse, untouched since empires rose and fell, lay a Cleveland telephone 
directory. That was the last item on the list. In a burial chamber further on, among Sultans buried 
with mountains of jewels and fleets of dirigibles, one modest Sultan had taken only a rubber life 
raft into the afterlife. I took it.

A ladder led up out of the catacombs. A rung broke near the top, and I fell down a ventilation 
shaft, through a grating, and into thousands of silk brassieres in the palace laundry.

XIV. The Boudoir

Now we needed a way in to the Leather Goddesses themselves. I went back through the minaret, the 
cell and the prison roof to Mitre's docks, where I put the raft in the canal and we climbed in. The 
current carried us downstream, my torch gave its last "phfffft!", and I caught hold of Donald Dock 
and hauled us out. Over the dune was the Exit Shop.

"One marsmid, please," said the proprietor, mostly asleep. I gave him my last coin. His supplier, 
he explained, had gone out of business ninety thousand years ago. He began to hand me a cardboard 
tube, his eye wandered, and he dropped it in the dust. It took me two tries to find it. Outside, I 
opened it and found a flexible black circle inside. I put it on the ground and we stood on it.

I was lying on a plush divan in the dark, breathing in the smell of leather, lots of leather, and I 
could hear heavy breathing beside me. Then there was a click and the lights came on.

I had violated the sanctity of a boudoir, and not just any boudoir but the extremely secret and 
private boudoir of the Leather Goddesses of Phobos, and they were pretty pissed about it. "The 
escaped prisoners! Sound the alarm!" "Inform the guards!" "Call out the army!" "Alert the space 
fleet!" "Summon my masseur," said the one Goddess who wasn't upset, and she pulled a lever. The 
floor opened, and Trent and I went down a long chute.

XV. The Machine

We landed on a lovely plaza of fountains, flower beds and bunnies among the towers of the Pleasure 
Palace, and a half-megaton grenade went off beside us.

"Okay, this is it!" shouted Trent. He handed me everything he was carrying. "Gotta start building 
that Super-Duper Anti-Leather Goddesses of Phobos Attack Machine! Hand me a common household 
blender."

I handed him the blender. A row of radium-powered tanks came rolling at us. "Okay, things are going 
great! Hand me a rubber hose." Berserk robot sumo wrestlers came storming across the plaza, each 
waving about three dozen samurai swords. Cotton balls. The Main Attack Fleet swooped in and 
vaporized several trees. The eighty-two degree angle. The palace guards began setting up a 
dematerialization ray. The headlight. Fifty Phobosian Chompers, each faster than a cheetah, meaner 
than a Tyrannosaurus, bigger than a sperm whale and as hungry as Texas, entered the plaza and saw 
us. The mouse. The fleet lobbed ion bombs and left fifty-foot craters, and I saw that each tank was 
bigger than Upper Sandusky City Hall. Jean Harlow. A couple of buildings behind us vanished; the 
death ray had missed, and in sixty centuries the guards had never missed twice. And last of all, 
the Cleveland phone book.

"There! I think it's all done. Cross your fingers, kiddo!" Trent switched it on. Sparks flew, an 
electric arc jumped between two electrodes, the machine shuddered, and I smelled something yellow 
shoot out of it.

It was a banana peel.

The peel landed a few feet away, and the whole machine shook once and blew itself up. Death was 
seconds away when the leading Chomper, coming at nearly Mach One, stepped on the banana peel and 
slid a few inches to one side. It was enough. The Chomper nudged a tank into a crater, the tank 
tripped a samurai robot, and then the whole army piled into the crater in the biggest pile-up I 
ever saw. A stray grenade landed in the middle of it, and the blast sheared the fins off the lead 
warship, and the entire Main Attack Fleet of the Leather Goddesses of Phobos came down in flames. 
The explosion knocked me senseless.

XVI. Home

Daylight woke me. I was lying by a dusty road outside a sleepy-looking gas station, wearing my 
comfortable old overalls again: dirty, dishevelled and bleeding from a few small cuts, but aglow 
with knowing that Earth was safe from the Leather Goddesses of Phobos.

Three girls in uniforms came bouncing out of the service station. "Oh, my goodness," they cooed, 
all together. "Are you all right?"

I was. I was more than all right. I'm told that makes me an Interplanetary Emperor.
