SORCERER
The Enchanter who met himself

I was being eaten by a hellhound when I woke up.

It was a dream, of course. A twisted forest, black trees, a beast with eyes like coals and teeth
like a row of daggers bounding toward me, and nowhere to run. It reached me and tore me apart, and
I sat up in my own bed in a cold sweat, in the dark, in my own small room in the Guild Hall of the
Enchanters.

I should explain how a nobody like me came to have his own room.

The last time I told a story, I was a novice with four spells in my book, sent by the Circle to
stop the warlock Krill precisely because nobody would notice me. I stopped him. Nobody was more
surprised than me. When I came home they gave me a private chamber, which in the Guild is a great
honor, and a seat in the Circle, which is a greater one, and for a year or so everyone was very
kind to me and nobody expected anything else.

So it was a quiet morning, and it was dark because my frotz spell had worn off in the night. I cast
it on myself, and a sickly yellow light rose off my skin, bright enough to read by. I got up.

The Hall was empty.

---

Not just quiet. Empty. There was a note tacked to my door from Frobar: he had taken the apprentices
into town to shop for the Guild picnic, and had tried to wake me, but I'd been deep asleep. And, he
added, had I seen Belboz anywhere? It wasn't like him to leave without telling someone. Then again,
he'd been acting pretty odd of late. Belboz never went anywhere.

Belboz the Necromancer was the Eldest of the Circle, the oldest and wisest of us all, the man who
had chosen me to go after Krill. His room was next to mine. When I looked in, there was his woven
wall hanging, his exotic morgia plant, his great darkwood desk, and his parrot pacing up and down
its perch in the corner. No Belboz.

"Squawk!" said the parrot. "You should never have let down your mindshield, you doddering old
Enchanter! Squawk!"

I didn't like the sound of that.

"Squawk! This tea is cold! Get me another cup!"

That sounded more like Belboz.

When I moved the wall hanging aside, a key fell out from behind it. In the desk drawer was his
journal, locked, and the key fit. Most of it was Guild business, meetings and minutes. But near the
end Belboz wrote about something called Jeearr: an ancient demon whose power could destroy the
Circle and the whole kingdom. He had decided to study it alone, to keep the rest of us safe.

The last three entries weren't in his handwriting. They weren't in any language I knew. The letters
leaned the wrong way and seemed to crawl on the page.

On the inside cover, in light pencil, was written: "Current code: dorn."

---

I spent the morning collecting the things I'd need without knowing why I'd need them, which is how
all my adventures seem to go. A scroll of meef, which makes plants wilt, from the library. A scroll
of gaspar from the empty quarters of an Enchanter named Helistar. From the store room, a little
vial of ochre potion, which tasted terrible and meant I wouldn't be hungry or thirsty for the rest
of my journey, and a matchbook with an advertisement printed inside it: "Amazing Vilstu Potion! Get
by without breathing! Order today by dropping this in any mailbox. Our shipping department will use
the latest in temporal travel techniques to insure that your potion arrives the same day you order
it!"

I dropped the matchbook in the brass receptacle in the lobby, mostly as a joke. When the messenger
came that morning, there was an orange vial in the receptacle. I kept it. You will see that I was
right to.

Down in the cellar was a trunk with five buttons on its lid: black with a star, gray with a moon,
red with a bloody knife, purple with a crown, and white with a dove in flight. A dorn is a beast
from the far hills, and every Enchanter's infotater shows its colors in order. Gray, purple, black,
gray, white. I pressed them. The trunk clicked, and the lid swung open, and inside was a moldy
scroll of aimfiz, a spell that sends the caster to wherever another person is.

I didn't have to think very hard about who to choose.

"Aimfiz Belboz," I said.

---

The world folded, and unfolded, and I was standing in a twisted forest of black trees. I knew it at
once. I had dreamed it that morning.

Somewhere close, something howled.

I ran northeast without looking back and came out at the forest's edge, breathing hard, with the
howling falling away behind me. I don't know how much of that morning's dream had been a warning
and how much had been Jeearr laughing at me. I've decided not to think about it.

There was no sign of Belboz. But I'd been pulled here, so he had been here, or something wearing
him had. I followed.

The next days are a blur of spells. I dried up a whole river with pulver, so that I could walk
along its muddy bed, and found a hidden cave in the bank where a colony of bats had lived for a
thousand years, and where I found the fweep scroll, which turns the caster into a bat, and a heap
of bat guano, which I took with me because I had already learned to take everything. The cave led
down into a pit full of bones and the dungeons of a ruined castle.

In the old fort of Griffspotter, I lowered a tattered flag of Quendor from its pole and found an
aqua vial wrapped up inside it. In the gun emplacement, a cannon's barrel was stuffed with what
looked like a pile of scrolls, but when I looked closer they were yipples, small and shy and
sleeping, curled up in a heap. I dropped the guano into the barrel. The yipples woke up, said
several rude things, and fled, all except one, which turned out to be a real scroll after all.
Yonk: it makes another spell more powerful.

At the edge of a chasm I learned izyuk and flew across, and on the far side was a tree that seemed
to grow gold coins on every branch. When I picked the first coin, the whole tree shimmered and
vanished. An illusion. But the coin in my hand was real, one zorkmid, and that one coin did more
work than any treasure I've ever held.

---

The old highway beyond the crater was blocked by a toll gate, and asleep in front of the toll gate
was a gnome.

I woke him up. He opened one eye, which wandered around until it found me, and leaped to his feet.
"One zorkmid, please!" I gave him my coin. He opened the gate, and before I could take a step he
was asleep again.

Beyond it, the highway ended at a little stone hut, and north of the hut was a glass arch, and
through the arch was the Glass Maze.

I want you to imagine a huge cube, built of glass, so that every wall and floor and ceiling is
either glass or nothing at all and you can't tell which. Some of the floors were solid and some of
them were air over a long drop. I stood at the arch and looked in and could not see a single safe
step.

So I became a bat.

I learned fweep three times, which made my head ache, and left everything I had outside the arch,
because bats don't carry spell books. Then I cast it. I didn't feel any different. Then I noticed I
was black, and had wings, and was flying a few feet off the ground, and I could hear the walls of
the maze singing back at me, every surface in the place pinging inside my head like a bell. A bat
doesn't see the maze. It hears it.

North, east, south, south, west, down, east, east, north, north, up, up, south, east. And I flew
out into sunlight, on a tiny plateau ringed by cliffs, and the spell wore off and dropped me on my
backside in the dust.

There was a scroll lying there. The moment I picked it up, a deep and evil laugh rolled around the
hollow, and behind me I heard the glass grinding as the maze rearranged itself.

There was a brick structure near the arch with a hole in the middle, leading down into darkness. I
thought about the stone hut below the cliffs, and its chimney, and I dropped the scroll down the
hole. Then I turned back into a bat and flew back into a maze I had never seen before.

A dorn beast found me in there. I heard it before I saw it, bellowing, galloping after me through
the glass, a great horned thing three times my size. I was a bat. I flew on. And the dorn, which
was not a bat, ran straight off the edge of a floor that wasn't there. It realized it was galloping
on air. It gave a surprisingly high-pitched squeal. It tried, very briefly, to invent flight.
Dorns, it turns out, do not so much fly as plummet.

I learned something about myself in that maze. I laughed.

---

My scroll was waiting in the fireplace of the stone hut, as I'd hoped, a little sooty: swanzo, the
spell that drives a spirit out of whatever it has taken. I copied it into my book very carefully. I
knew now what was waiting at the end of this road.

On my way back past the toll gate, I searched the sleeping gnome and took my zorkmid back out of
his pocket. I am not proud of it. I needed it.

I needed it, it turned out, for an amusement park. Somebody had built one in the caves below the
crater, long ago, with a roller coaster and a flume and a midway, all of it rusting, and a gnome at
the gate who wanted a zorkmid. Inside, a hawker at an arcade booth offered prizes to anybody who
could knock over a wooden bunny with a ball. My hands were shaking too much from exhaustion to
throw straight. I drank the aqua potion from the fort's flag, and my hands went steady as stone,
and I knocked that bunny into the next county. "What an arm, kid, what an arm!" The prize was a
glittering scroll of malyon, which brings things to life.

In a hall south of the park, a great dragon was carved into the rock of the far wall, asleep. I
read the yonk scroll first, to make the spell stronger, and then I cast malyon on the carving. The
dragon woke. It shook itself loose from the wall, and the wall crumbled away to reveal a passage
south, and the dragon reared back its head, smoke curling from its nostrils, and looked at me.

Then it turned back to stone. I was standing close enough to see the scales of its lip.

---

Now I have to tell you about the coal mine, and I am not sure how to do it, because I still don't
completely understand it.

Past the dragon was a sooty room, and past that a coal bin room, and the moment I stepped into it
the tunnel behind me caved in. The air was thick with coal gas. I couldn't breathe. I opened the
orange vial that had come in the mail, the Vilstu Potion, and drank it, and it tasted like pepper
and made my nose tingle, and I could get by without breathing. Amaze your friends.

And then someone came sliding down the coal chute in the corner and landed in a heap near the bin.

It was me.

Older, by an hour or so. Filthy, my clothes torn, gasping in the gas. He looked at me with my own
eyes and said, "The combination is 623." And then he waited, looking at me, almost expectantly. And
then he said, "Give me... the spell book."

I'll tell you the truth: I gave him my spell book because I couldn't think what else to do. He took
it, and dived down the lower chute, and was gone.

So I went east, to a door with a dial on it. I set the dial to 623, and the door opened. Beyond it
was a mine shaft, and a coil of rope, and up the shaft a winding tunnel with an old beam of wood
lying in it. At the top of the upper coal chute I laid the beam across the opening and tied the
rope to it, and I had to leave everything behind, every single thing, to climb down that slippery
rope. Halfway down the chute was a little room with a slanted roof and a lamp burning on the wall,
and on the floor a shimmering scroll.

Golmac, it said. Travel through time.

I cast it, and smoke wrapped around me, and when it cleared, the lamp on the wall was closed. Time
had gone back. I opened the lamp and found inside it a smelly scroll, vardik, the spell that
shields a mind from an evil being. And I understood, all at once, the whole shape of the thing, and
the hair stood up on my arms.

The orange potion was wearing off. I couldn't breathe. I went east, into the chute, and slid down
it, and landed in a heap near the bin in the coal bin room.

And there I was. Younger, by an hour. Clean. Confused. Holding my spell book.

I told him the combination was 623. He looked very surprised. I knew exactly how surprised he was.
And then he gave me my spell book, and I went down the lower chute with it, and left him there to
work it all out.

I have thought about it a great deal since. Who made up the number 623? I heard it from myself, and
I said it to myself. Nobody chose it. It just is. Belboz says that is how time travel always works,
and that I should stop thinking about it before I hurt myself.

---

The lower chute spat me out on the shore of a lagoon, under cliffs, by a cold sea. I was so tired I
lay down on the sand and slept, and when I woke, every spell I'd learned had drained away in the
night, the way they always do. I sat on the beach and learned vardik, and meef twice, and swanzo,
very carefully, one at a time.

At the bottom of the lagoon, under a forest of weeds, lay a crate. I wilted the weeds with meef and
opened the crate, and inside, among a lot of rotting cargo, was a suit. A whole suit, with claws
and a slavering mouth, made to look exactly like a grue.

North along the shore, at the mouth of a river, a cave was choked with vines. I put on the grue
suit. I wilted the vines. I walked in.

It was the lair of the grues. I have heard about grues my whole life, as everyone has, and I had
never seen one, because you can't see a grue: they only come in the dark. These grues didn't care
about my light. There were dozens of them, hundreds maybe, sleek and hungry, and they looked at me
in my ridiculous suit, and they looked away. One of them made a noise that might have been a
greeting. I nodded to it. I didn't stop walking.

At the back of the lair were three doors. I chose the white one.

---

Belboz was inside. He looked up at me with his own kind old face, and someone else's eyes behind it.

I cast vardik on myself first, and felt my mind close like a shutter. Then I cast swanzo on Belboz.

A wispy, translucent shape rose out of him, and spoke in a voice so deep that I heard it in my
bones. "Foolish Charlatan! I am forced to flee that weak, old body. I shall take your own, instead!
Already I have sucked all knowledge, all secrets from that ancient Enchanter. Now begins an epoch
of evil transcending even your worst nightmares!"

It blew toward me on a cold wind, and wrapped around me like a cloud, and began to squeeze.

And then it struck the wall around my mind, and recoiled as if it had touched a stove.

"No!" it cried. "Such a guileless Enchanter, with a mind shield?" Its voice was getting fainter.
"It cannot be! I cannot survive without a host!" It roiled, and thinned, and with one last scream
it was gone, and the room was quiet.

Belboz stirred. He sat up, and looked at me, and his eyes were only his own again. He asked me a
few sharp questions, the way he always does, and then he cast a little spell I didn't know, and the
grue suit vanished, and we were both standing in the Chamber of the Circle, with every Enchanter in
the Guild gathered around us, wondering what on earth had happened.

"Once again," said Belboz, "this young Enchanter has done a matchless service to the Guild and to
the entire kingdom. I grow old, and must soon step down as Head of the Circle. But let it be known
that a successor has been found."

They all looked at me.

I thought about the novice who had been sent after Krill because nobody would miss him. I thought
about the filthy, gasping young man who had slid down a coal chute and told me a number. I hope I
live up to them both.

And I thought about the parrot, and what it had said: you should never have let down your
mindshield, you doddering old Enchanter. I have decided that I will never, ever let down mine.
