SUSPECT

I. The Party

It was Halloween night, and it had been raining since early morning. Veronica Ashcroft's mania for 
Halloween parties was putting new twists on a hundred-and-ten-year-old tradition, and the weather 
hadn't kept many guests away from Ashcroft Farm. The ballroom was full of them, in costumes of 
every kind, and mine was a ridiculous western outfit: a gunbelt, a little mask, a cowboy's vest. It 
was the only thing left at the costumer's on such short notice, and in that crowd it stood out only 
for being sober and tasteful. I'd brought a lariat with it and got tired of carrying it around, so 
I'd hung it up in the coat closet with my overcoat.

I'm a reporter. I'd been invited as an old friend, and I'd come for a story.

The bartender wore a small domino mask and nothing else in the way of costume. Sam Ostmann, the 
developer, was having a wonderful time as a vampire. Near the fireplace a sheik, Michael Wellman, 
Veronica's husband, waved me over to join his little group. "The weather out there is horrible: 
glad you could make it," he said, and introduced me around. "...and you already know Veronica." The 
fairy queen beside him nodded at me. "You might find this worth adding to your story," Michael said.

Veronica was talking about a horse show. One of her horses, named Lurking Grue, was entered, and 
she described it in glowing detail, her voice muffled by her ornate mask and slurred by whatever 
was in the big glass she held. Making a point, she waved the glass over her head, and a bright red 
drink with ice cubes in it poured down the front of her gown. The gown, of course, was white. She 
said a word silver-winged fairies aren't supposed to know and threw the glass on the floor. Michael 
pulled a handkerchief from his robes and dabbed uselessly at the stain, and she pushed him away. 
"I'm going to go clean this up. You just stay here."

I looked at her closely as she went. She was in her early thirties, small, about five foot three 
and a hundred pounds, in a voluminous white gown and silvered fairy wings. Her mask was striking: a 
glittery silver stocking over the whole head, hair of silver and gold wire streaming behind as if 
blown by the wind, ears of silvered butterfly wings, and eyebrows that curled up into antennae.

II. The Tangle

I followed her out of the ballroom, which I would come to regret. In the long hall outside, Smythe 
the butler, dressed as a gorilla, was taking a guest's coat, and the two of them blocked the way. 
The guest turned to stare at Veronica as she hurried by and bumped into me, and Smythe, who 
couldn't see much through his gorilla suit, got tangled up in the guest's overcoat while the guest 
was still wearing it. The three of us nearly went down together. The more I tried to get loose, the 
worse it got. By the time Smythe had got us all back on our feet, apologizing and brushing the dirt 
off my vest with a little brush from his suit, the other guest had made a grateful escape and 
Veronica was long gone.

I went on through the front hall to the entry hall, with its two curving staircases roped off with 
velvet ribbon to keep the guests downstairs, and into the morning room. It was a room for receiving 
visitors, with a telephone on a walnut secretary and a window seat under one of the windows. I 
opened the curtains. The rain had slackened to a drizzle. I opened the window seat too, because in 
"Arsenic and Old Lace" there were bodies in the window seat. This one was empty.

The front doorbell rang, and I went and opened the door. It was Alicia, Veronica's old school 
friend and mine, in a wet overcoat. She thanked me for getting the door, came in, and went off down 
the hall. I looked out at the weather once more. Just a drizzle.

I went around to the coat closet off the long hall, full of damp coats and raincoats. My own 
overcoat was there. So was a wet overcoat with a Lord and Taylor label, and under that, sewn in 
script, "Alicia Barron."

It was very wet indeed for a coat that had come in out of a drizzle.

III. Conversations

Back in the ballroom I talked to people, as reporters do.

Ostmann said he was just a businessman trying to make a profit and keep busy. He thought Michael 
was pathetic: "What does he do? A man should be doing something, or he's just a parasite." About 
Bill Cochrane, who was somewhere in the crowd in a spacesuit, he was scathing: the man was up 
before the Real Estate Board for cleaning out escrow accounts, he and his agents were a disgrace to 
the profession, and Ostmann couldn't believe Veronica had invited him. Veronica, on the other hand, 
knew what she wanted and had a good head for business. Colonel Marston was an old dinosaur, "a 
toothy one, though," who thought he was in loco parentis to Veronica and her brother Richard. 
Senator Asher, the harlequin, had done a lot for the state; Ostmann had contributed to his campaign.

Michael found my questions odd. "She's my wife: I love her," he said of Veronica, and looked 
around. "I wonder where she's gotten off to?" Ostmann would love to buy the farm, he said, but 
they'd turned him down; their roots were here, though some people thought it was past time to move 
up the county, and Ostmann wanted to put town houses on it. "Imagine that!" As for himself: "I 
didn't realize the rich had to serve any function but decoration and public amusement. That's what 
I do, and I take my job seriously." Colonel Marston was one of the ablest men he knew; he'd done a 
lot with the family trust. Alicia was an excellent horsewoman who boarded her horses at the farm. 
Richard and he had their disagreements. Richard's girlfriend Linda, the prima ballerina, "just 
doesn't fit in this sort of group," and with a good tail wind might have an outside chance at a 
two-digit IQ.

Colonel Marston, the African explorer, told me he was semi-retired. He ran the Ashcroft Family 
Trust, but it mostly ran itself. Working with Veronica, he said, was not as rewarding as working 
with her father had been.

Then an argument started about this year's horse sales. Marston said no stallion had sold for more 
than a hundred thousand; Cochrane said he was wrong. I listened. Michael joined in and remembered a 
black stallion going for over a hundred thousand last year. Marston glared at him. Marston said he 
had a good memory for figures, that the top price was ninety-two thousand, for a chestnut about 
fifteen hands, and Michael nodded as if convinced. Cochrane, feeling betrayed, called the Colonel 
senile. "It was a black stallion, and I remember who bought it. It was Jeffries! So there!" He 
nearly spilled his drink on Marston.

Michael stepped between them. "Look here, no need to fight. We've got all the auction records in 
the office. Veronica gets everything published. We can settle this like gentlemen. Okay?" Cochrane 
calmed down, Marston muttered that some of them weren't gentlemen, and the three of them headed for 
the office.

IV. The Office

I took a shorter way, around through the library, the entry hall and the morning room, and got to 
the hall outside the office as they did.

Michael felt under his robes for his keys and couldn't find them. "I had them! I unlocked the wine 
cellar only an hour ago; Smythe needed to get to it! My keys are gone!" He got more and more 
frantic. "Wait a minute! This door isn't locked! Something's going on here!" And he threw the door 
open.

The office had been torn apart. Papers were everywhere, folders spilled across every surface, a 
file of floppy disks had been thrown at one wall, copier toner was spilled on the rug, and every 
drawer stood open. On the floor lay the fairy's mask, crumpled. Behind the big desk, slumped on the 
floor, was Veronica Ashcroft. Her mask had been pulled off, but the rest of her costume was intact. 
Around her neck was the thing that had killed her: a rope. My lariat, from the coat closet.

The three of them stared. They started searching the room and looking at the body until Michael 
said, "Wait! This is a job for the police! Don't touch anything!" The others agreed, though 
Cochrane took some convincing. Michael picked up the phone and called the police, and his voice 
broke toward the end.

I didn't touch the body, not yet. But a crumpled business card lay on top of the trash in the 
wastebasket, and a manila folder lay on the desk, and I took them both.

Cochrane lifted the visor of his spacesuit. The costume was hot and itchy and he was sorry he'd 
picked it, and he was a little unsteady on his feet. "I didn't do it! She wasn't giving me a fair 
shake, but murder? Not me!" I showed him the card. "Yeah," he said. "I just wanted to keep her from 
doing something she'd regret. I just want an equal chance to buy this property. If she's going to 
sell it, why a sweetheart deal with Ostmann? I went to her office earlier to try to convince her, 
but the door was locked and she wouldn't answer. That was at about quarter of eight, so you can't 
pin this on me: she was still alive then, everybody saw her."

Then all three of them left.

V. The Garage

North of the office a door let out onto a covered walkway, where the rain was pouring down, and the 
walkway led west to the garage. It held four cars comfortably; tonight there was a BMW 320i and a 
Mercedes 280SL. In a battered tool chest in front of the workbench I found a crowbar, and I took 
it. Then I hid behind the BMW and waited, because someone who loses his keys at the moment he needs 
to show he can't open a door will go somewhere to deal with what he's carrying.

At twelve minutes past ten Michael came in. He went to the back of the BMW, opened the trunk, and 
closed it again a few seconds later. The trunk lid hid what he was doing.

I stood up. He jumped. I asked him about the murder. "You reporters have all the sensitivity of 
buffalo," he said. "Can't you leave me alone?"

VI. The Body

I went back to the office. Veronica was still in her costume, stained with what she'd been 
drinking. She had plainly been strangled, but there were few signs of a struggle. When I looked 
under her I found a small object on the floor beneath the body: a silver bullet, the kind I had in 
my gunbelt. Someone had made sure it would be found there.

Her hair was short and blonde.

VII. The Library

I went to the library and hid behind the big overstuffed Victorian armchair in the corner. While I 
waited I heard a police siren come up the drive and stop, and the front doorbell rang. I read the 
manila folder. It was labeled "Sale of Ashcroft Farm": a purchase and sale agreement between the 
Ashcroft Trust, in the person of Veronica, and Ostmann Properties, dated today, signed by neither 
party.

At 10:38 Colonel Marston came in. At 10:42 Michael joined him. They greeted each other and looked 
suspiciously around the room without seeing me, and Michael closed and locked the doors. He handed 
the Colonel a piece of paper. Marston read it eagerly, folded it, and stuffed it into a pocket, and 
both men grinned.

When I stood up they stared at me and then at each other. I unlocked the door to the entry hall, 
and they left by the other one. I followed them back to the ballroom, where Marston stood warming 
his hands and his back at the fireplace. Out of the corner of my eye I caught an odd movement. By 
the time I got there, something other than logs was burning in the fireplace, and a piece of paper 
was starting to smolder.

I reached into the fire, heedless of my skin, and pulled it out just in time.

It had a corner torn off where it had been stapled to something. It was on an accounting firm's 
letterhead, and it read: "Supplement I. Investors in Southeast Planning Corporation. Colonel Robert 
Marston..50%. Michael R. Wellman......50%."

VIII. The Trunk

I went back to the garage with my crowbar and pried open the BMW's trunk. Inside was a folder 
labeled "Ashcroft Family Trust," holding a thick sheaf of stapled documents. On top was a letter 
from an accounting firm: "While it is difficult to make explicit judgements on the advisability of 
certain investments by the Ashcroft Family Trust, we find a disturbing pattern. In the past ten 
years almost twenty percent of the Trust holdings have been placed in four companies, each of which 
is a poor financial risk. As the accompanying documents show, all four are controlled by Southeast 
Planning Corporation: privately held, with chief executive officer listed as Colonel Robert 
Marston."

On the back of the bunch, under the staple, was a corner of paper where a sheet had been torn off.

IX. The Detective

I had to find the detective before he found me. My lariat had killed her and my bullet was under 
her body, and I had been the one who ran out after her when she spilled her drink. I went around 
through the halls toward the front of the house and waited. Sergeant Duffy came into view and went 
away again. At eighteen minutes past eleven the detective came up to me.

I showed him the sheet from the fireplace. "What's the significance of this? Colonel Marston and 
Michael were co-investors in something?" I gave him the trust folder. "Where did you get this? It's 
important! This means that Michael and Colonel Marston were embezzling from the family trust. And 
further, Veronica knew it!" I showed him the sale agreement. "If someone wanted to prevent this 
sale, it might have been worth committing a murder to do. But who could that have been?" And 
Cochrane's business card. "This certainly seems to implicate Cochrane. He seems hot-headed enough 
to commit murder, and the sale provides a motive."

He had embezzlement now, and a motive. But he didn't have a murderer, and Michael had been in the 
ballroom with me when Veronica left it.

X. The Hair

Back in the office I picked up the fairy's mask. The inside was slightly damp with sweat, and 
caught in the silver mesh was a hair. It was dark, and medium in length.

Veronica's hair was short and blonde.

I went to the coat closet. Smythe was straightening the coats. I took Alicia's wet overcoat. Then I 
went up the long hall to the kitchen, a big country kitchen in disarray from the party, and took 
the trash basket from the corner. In it was a broken glass, the one the fairy queen had thrown on 
the floor.

XI. The Fireplace

The detective was standing by the ballroom fireplace, weighing evidence, with Duffy beside him.

"Fingerprint this glass," I said, and the detective said, "Sergeant Duffy, analyze the broken glass 
for fingerprints!" Duffy took it and almost seemed to disappear. "He's a fine public servant," said 
the detective.

I gave him the mask and pointed out the hair. "A dark hair, eh? Veronica's hair was blonde. How did 
a hair this color get into this mask?"

I showed him Alicia's overcoat. "I don't see the significance of this. It's been raining all night, 
as far as I know. Everyone's coat is wet." Smythe came up just then and offered to hang up the 
detective's coat for him, and the detective politely refused to give up his trenchcoat.

So I told him about the weather. When Alicia rang the bell, the rain had slackened to a drizzle, 
but her coat was soaked through. "That may mean she was here earlier when the rain was pelting 
down," the detective said, "that she didn't arrive when she said she did."

I went over to the bar, where Alicia sat in her harem silks. Her hair was medium length and dark. I 
asked where she'd parked. "I parked it on the other side of the barn, out of the way of all the 
other cars."

XII. The Analysis

I went back to the fireplace and waited for Duffy. The band was on its break and a record was 
playing "Light My Fire." Michael wandered around the room.

At nine minutes past midnight Duffy came back with the analysis, and the detective read it and 
handed it to me. The glass had been hard to work on, covered with the sugary residue of a Singapore 
Sling, but partial prints had been lifted from a dry patch and compared with the prints Duffy had 
taken from Veronica Ashcroft at the start of the investigation. "It is our conclusion that the 
prints on the glass are not those of Veronica Ashcroft."

The fairy queen who spilled her drink in front of us all, who waved her glass and swore and stormed 
off to clean up, hadn't been Veronica. Veronica was already dead in the office. The woman in the 
fairy costume, with the full-head mask hiding her dark hair, was Alicia, who had arrived early in 
the downpour and then come in at the front door later, in the drizzle, as herself.

The band came back from its break with "I Left My Heart in San Francisco."

"Arrest Michael and Alicia," I told the detective.

Sergeant Duffy appeared with Alicia in tow and put handcuffs on Michael, who stood stiff and 
silent. "Let's not have any trouble, now," said Duffy, in his own way. They went out to the 
driveway, where a police car waited with its engine running.

XIII. The Story

My testimony as the prosecution's star witness convicted Michael of the first-degree murder of his 
wife, and Alicia as his accomplice, and they went to prison with the proverbial key thrown away. 
Colonel Marston was convicted separately of embezzlement and grand theft for milking the family 
trust.

My twelve-part syndicated story won the Pulitzer Prize, and the book was number one on the 
bestseller lists for forty-two weeks, not to mention the movie and the book club.

As the book explains, it began with Veronica wanting to sell the farm and move somewhere more 
rural. Even with the money from the sale, she would have needed more from the family trust to buy 
the new place and build on it. That alarmed Michael and the Colonel, who had been milking the trust 
through dummy companies they controlled. Veronica had grown suspicious and had the trust's dealings 
investigated. She expected to find Colonel Marston behind it. She didn't expect to find her husband 
an equal partner.

For Michael, time was running out. He wanted out of the marriage, but not out of the Ashcroft 
fortune, and he brought Alicia into a bizarre scheme: she would play the murdered Veronica in front 
of a ballroom full of witnesses and give him his alibi. And when an old friend turned up at the 
party in a cowboy suit, with a lariat to hang in the closet and silver bullets in his gunbelt, he 
gave them a red herring. A few pieces of evidence planted, and I might have been framed.

Fortunately for everyone but the plotters, it came to nothing.
