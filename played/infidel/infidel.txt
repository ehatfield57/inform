INFIDEL
The pyramid that waited

I woke up with a headache like a hammer and a taste in my mouth like old cotton, and I knew before
I opened my eyes that something was wrong. It was too quiet. A camp of forty men is never quiet.
There is always someone singing, someone arguing, someone banging a pot. But that morning there was
nothing but the heat and, high overhead, the drone of a plane.

I sat up in my cot and looked around my tent, and I remembered yesterday.

I had made them work on a holy day. I'd stood in front of the whole crew and told them that their
prayers could wait, that the pyramid would not, and that any man who didn't pick up a shovel could
walk back to Cairo. They'd been grumbling for weeks about the pay and the food and the endless
digging in the wrong places. I didn't care. I was paying them, wasn't I? Badly, but I was paying
them.

They didn't walk back to Cairo. They drugged my drink, and while I slept they packed up everything
they could carry and left.

I know this because they were polite enough to leave a note, tacked to a tent flap in the work
tent. Fi aman Allah, it began. Go with God. Then it got less friendly. Hereafter you shall pursue
your fool dream of the hidden pyramid and its riches alone. May the jackals feed well on your
bones. They had locked a few things they knew I needed in my own trunk, with my own padlock, and
taken the key. We hope the drug we placed in your drink did you harm, the note finished. If not, we
are at least satisfied you slept especially soundly while we cleaned out the camp.

I remember thinking it was a little unfair. I remember thinking it was the kind of thing ungrateful
people do.

---

I should tell you who I am, or rather who I was then, because I'm not sure the same man is writing
this.

I was an archaeologist, of a sort. Not the kind with a professorship and a pipe. The kind who reads
the professors' books and then goes out and digs where they only theorized. My old partner Craige
used to call me a looter with a library card. Craige and I had found things together once, in Peru
and in the Yucatan, and then I'd cut him out of a deal because I didn't think he deserved the
share, and I hadn't heard from him since. Things were never like this when Craige was around. He
knew how to talk to workers. He knew where the crocodiles were.

What I had was a map. An ancient map that had belonged to a Professor who died before he could use
it, showing a pyramid buried somewhere in the sands east of the Nile, a queen's tomb that no one
had found in four thousand years. What I didn't have was the instrument that would tell me exactly
where I was on that map: a navigation box, ordered from England months ago, always arriving next
week. Without it, I'd had the men dig a dozen holes in a dozen wrong places. The little craters
were all over camp, like the desert had the measles.

That plane overhead, as it turned out, was finally delivering it. A crate came floating down on a
parachute and thumped into the sand near the river. Somebody up there dipped their wings at me in
salute. I think, looking back, that was the last friendly gesture anyone made toward me in this
life.

I didn't need the box in the end. But I'll come to that.

---

They'd left me more than they had to. I'll say that for them.

In the supply tent, stuck in the sand, was a pick axe, and in a corner a shovel. In the work tent
was a battered knapsack with a coil of rope and a canteen inside. By the cold firepit lay a
matchbook. And in my tent, my own trunk, locked with my own padlock. I smashed the padlock with the
pick axe on the first swing, which I found very satisfying. Inside was a strip of dried beef, an
inspection sticker, and the Professor's map, folded around something heavy.

I ate the beef sitting on the trunk. It made me thirsty, so I walked down to the river and drank
from the Nile with crocodiles watching me from the far bank, and I filled the canteen.

Then I unfolded the map, and the heavy thing fell into my hand.

It was a stone cube, perfectly square, carved with the same symbols as the map's margins. I had
carried that map for two years and never thought to wonder why it was so heavy. I stood there in
the sun and laughed out loud, alone, like a madman.

I followed the path out of the camp to the southeast, out past the last of the tents until the camp
was a shimmering smudge behind me, and then further, out of sight of it altogether, into the blank
yellow middle of nowhere. I chose a spot. I would like to say I chose it by careful reasoning from
the map. I chose it because something about the shape of the dunes reminded me of the drawing.

Then I dug.

I dug the way I had told forty men to dig, and I understood for the first time why they had hated
me. The sand slides back in as fast as you lift it out. The sun sits on your neck like a hand.
After an hour I was knee-deep. After two I was standing in a pit higher than my head, with the
walls trembling around me. And then my shovel struck stone with a sound I will never forget, a
clear ringing note, like a bell.

I cleared the sand away with my hands. It was the very top of a pyramid, its capstone, and in the
middle of it was a square hole.

I put the cube into it.

The ground beneath me shook. The stones moved, and the sand began to pour away downward like water
down a drain, and I jumped clear just before the vortex took me. When it settled, there was an
opening into the dark, and steps leading down. I remember the feeling exactly. It wasn't joy. It
was a sneer. I'd found it. And without any help from those idiots.

---

The first room was the Chamber of Ra, and it was lit from nowhere, the walls themselves glowing as
if the sun god had left a little of himself behind. Four staircases led down from it, north, south,
east and west. In the middle stood a red sandstone altar. A bronze torch leaned against it, and on
the floor lay a small pink alabaster jar of oil, as if someone had left them there for the next
visitor. Four thousand years, and the next visitor was me.

I dipped the torch in the oil and struck a match and lit it, and went down the winding stairs to
the south.

I won't tell you everything I saw down there, because I didn't look at most of it properly. I was
looking for gold. I passed through a chamber that held the Queen's royal barge, a whole boat, long
and elegant, built to carry her through the air to the next world. I passed a steep passage and a
temple so high that my torchlight never reached the ceiling. In the inner chamber the walls were
painted with the Queen on her barge, floating through the sky with all her attendants.

There was a corpse in that chamber, too, the bones of somebody who had got here before me, with a
jeweled ring still on one finger. I didn't touch it. I'd like to tell you that was respect. It was
instinct. In my trade you learn that the easy treasure is the one that kills you.

In two small alcoves, one off a golden room and one off a silver room, I found two chalices, one
gold and one silver. I took them both.

---

The barge was where I first understood that the builders of this place had been thinking about me.

Not me personally. People like me. Thieves. Every room had a trick in it, a way to kill the person
who came in carelessly, and every trick had a way around it for the person who paid attention. It
was a test, the whole pyramid, and the only people who were ever meant to pass it were dead priests.

The barge's mast was a long wooden beam, and it was jammed in place from below by a little wedge of
wood called a shim. I found it by crawling into the hold with my torch. I took out the shim and
pushed the beam up through the deck, and it fell with a thud. I carried that beam out of the hold,
and I carried it for the rest of my life. It turned out to be the most valuable thing in the
pyramid, and it wasn't even gold.

---

North of the Chamber of Ra, the stairs dropped away so steeply that I had to tie my rope to the
altar and climb down it. At the bottom was a round room with four hallways leading off it like
spokes of a wheel, and in each hallway a great stone door hung halfway open, balanced against the
door in the opposite hallway. Walk down one hallway and its door lowered in front of you, and the
opposite one rose. You could never get to the end.

In the middle of the room stood a statue, top-heavy, about two feet shorter than me. It reminded me
of my old schoolteacher, Miss Ellingsworth. I pushed it. It fell over, and its head broke off.

Then I understood. The doors were scales. Put enough weight in one hallway and its door would stay
shut, and the opposite one would stay open. So I rolled that headless statue into a hallway and
piled my things beside it, the head and the knapsack and the beam, until the door on the far side
rose all the way to the ceiling. Then I walked through.

Four times I did it. I rolled poor Miss Ellingsworth into every hallway in turn, and in the four
rooms at the ends of the hallways I found four rooms dedicated to four goddesses: Selkis, Isis,
Neith and Nephthys. In each room was a jeweled cluster: a ruby, an emerald, an opal, a diamond.
There was a fifth cluster, solid gold, lying on the floor of the round room itself. Each goddess's
room had hieroglyphs on its wall, and every one of them had a number in it, one, two, three, four.
I didn't know what the numbers were for. I wrote them down anyway. Neith, one. Selkis, two. Isis,
three. Nephthys, four.

I climbed back up the rope with my knapsack full and my head full of numbers. I was rich already,
by any sensible standard. But I wasn't sensible. I wanted the Queen.

---

There is a place in the pyramid where the rooms are perfect cubes, and in one of them a panel of
bricks was set into the wall, with a hieroglyphic scroll I'd found on the barge to tell me which to
take out. The first. The third. The fifth. When I pulled the fifth brick, a secret doorway swung
open.

Down a sixteen-step stair, a doorway was sealed with plaster. I broke it with the pick axe. Beyond
that, a long narrow passage ended at another plastered door, and at the foot of the side walls were
two little niches, three inches off the floor, exactly the width of a beam apart.

I set the barge's mast across the niches and stood on it, and then I broke the plaster. Sand began
to pour from under the door, and then the floor itself was gone, slid away into a black pit beneath
me, and I was standing on a beam over nothing at all, holding my breath, with my torch shaking in
my hand.

Whoever built that trap expected me to be standing on the floor. I wasn't. I want you to understand
how good that felt.

In the antechamber beyond, a door to the west had a timber frame, and I didn't trust it. I wedged
the beam across the doorway before I opened it, and two enormous stones slid out of the walls on
either side to crush me, and stopped against the beam with a groan. I walked between them into the
annex.

---

My torch began to sputter there, the oil nearly gone. I put it out in the dark, which was the most
frightening ten seconds of my life, and dipped it in the last of the oil by feel, and struck a
match with fingers I couldn't see, and lit it again. The flame came up warm and yellow, and the
painted eyes on the walls looked back at me.

In the middle of the annex was a stone slab with four round holes in its corners, laid out exactly
like the four hallways of the round room. Diamond in the first. Ruby in the second. Emerald in the
third. Opal in the fourth. Each one clicked into place. And the slab opened as gracefully as if it
weighed nothing, and inside lay a small golden spatula and an ancient book.

I took the book. When I came out, I pulled the beam free from the doorway behind me, and the two
great stones slammed together with a crash that I felt in my teeth. There was no going back that
way now. I don't remember caring.

At the north end of the antechamber was the last door, with a timber lintel over it and a seal
across it. I wedged the beam under the lintel and broke the seal, and fine sand ran out of a crack
in the stone, and a three-ton block above my head started to come down and stopped against the
beam. The beam creaked. It held. I opened the door.

---

The Burial Chamber.

In the middle of it, stretching almost from wall to wall, was the sarcophagus of the Queen. Its
cover was pure quartz, clear as water, and through it I could see the golden coffin inside, shaped
like a sleeping woman. On top of the cover were two shallow outlines, one large and one small, as
if waiting for something to be laid there. At each corner of the sarcophagus stood a statue of a
goddess, holding the cover down with outstretched arms: Isis, Nephthys, Neith and Selkis. My four
goddesses. My four numbers.

The book fit the large outline exactly.

In a small room to the east, the treasury, a granite table held three stone discs. On the middle
disc sat a golden scarab, and the middle disc had sunk a little, under its weight. The two outer
discs were empty, and they stood an inch above the table. Take the scarab, and the middle disc
would rise, and something terrible would surely happen. I'd learned that much about this place.

So I made the table level. My golden chalice on the left disc. My silver chalice on the right,
filled up with the last of my water from the canteen until it weighed the same, until both discs
sat flush with the table. Then I lifted the scarab, and nothing happened at all.

I laid the scarab in the small outline on the quartz cover. Something clicked beneath all four
statues at once. And I turned them, one by one, in the order the goddesses had told me. Neith.
Selkis. Isis. Nephthys. Each one pivoted on its base and lifted its arms away from the cover, and
when the fourth one turned they all froze, and there was one last, light click from the sarcophagus.

That was everything. Every lock in the place had opened for me. Four thousand years of priests and
traps and riddles, and I had walked through all of it alone.

---

I lifted the cover.

The inside of the sarcophagus was lined with gold, set with jewels, burning in my torchlight. I
can't describe it. I have tried, sitting here, and I can't. All my dreams, all at once. I took a
breath, and I trembled, and I realized the ground under my feet was trembling too.

The Burial Chamber was collapsing. The walls were closing in. The stones I'd wedged with my beam
and the stones I'd shut behind me were all settling at once, the way the builders had always meant
them to, the moment anyone opened that cover. It's quieter now. The dust is still coming down, but
slowly.

I have the torch, for a little while longer. I have the treasure. I have, at last, plenty of time
to think.

If anyone were on the other side of that wall, they could dig me out. Forty men with shovels could
do it in a day. If I'd paid them. If I'd let them pray. If I'd cut Craige in on the find, he would
have been out there with them, swearing at me and digging. If I'd hired a guide instead of thinking
I was too clever to need one.

The workers called me an infidel. I thought they meant I didn't share their God. I think now they
meant something simpler. That I didn't keep faith with anyone. Not with them, not with Craige, not
with the Queen whose bed I've broken into. I kept faith only with myself, and here we are, the two
of us, alone.

Someday someone will dig down to this room, the way I did. They'll find a queen's treasure, and
beside it a skeleton with a torch in its hand.

And then, I suppose, I'll get my fame.
