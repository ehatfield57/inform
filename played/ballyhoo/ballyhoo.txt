BALLYHOO

The Traveling Circus That Time Forgot


1. AFTER THE SHOW

I trudged out with the crowd, thumbing back through the evening in my mind. The circus had been a
candy apple with a rotten core. The prices were outrageous, my seat had a view from Mount Everest,
the lines were long enough to be communist, and the audience was more savage than the lion act. But
that wasn't what had soured me on Spangleland. It was that the circus reminded me of my own secret,
irrational wish to steal the spotlight, to cheat death, and to bask in the thunder of applause.

So I didn't go home.

Outside the big top, in the matted crabgrass by a drinking fountain, a midget in a Russian
general's uniform was trying to reach the spout. That was Comrade Thumb. I lifted him up, he
slurped his fill, gave me a quick salute, and waddled off into the dark. Beside the big top someone
had dropped a cheap plastic clown mask, and I picked it up.

West of the back yard stood a droopy prop tent full of outrageous props, including a midget
automobile and a heap of gorilla suit. There was also a life-size cardboard President William
Howard Taft. Two shadows loomed against the canvas outside and voices came closer, so I crouched
behind the President.

It was Mr. Munrab, the owner of the circus, and the detective he had hired. Munrab had had a
terrible day. Receipts were down, the concession stands had sold next to nothing, the towners had
nearly levelled his grandstand, and on top of all that somebody had kidnapped his daughter,
Chelsea. He showed the detective her picture and a ransom note whose language, he said, proved it
was no inside job. He didn't want the police; the circus couldn't stand the publicity.

"I'll suspect who you want me to suspect," said the detective, with no passion and less
professionalism. "You're paying the bill."

They hurried out. I stood up, and decided that if nobody else was going to find Chelsea, I might as
well try.


2. THE HIGH WIRE

Under the white wagon where Munrab kept his office, part of a narrow fibreglass pole stuck out into
the grass. I took it back into the big top, left the mask in the ring, and climbed the rope ladder
to the platform. I had always wanted to.

The tightrope stretched east into the dark. I took a couple of baby steps, then a couple more. At
the halfway point I stopped thinking about it. And then I was on the far platform, and from
somewhere the familiar notes of tonight's show rang through my body: "Ta Daaaaaaaaaaa."

Up against the ceiling floated a helium balloon. I took it by the frill and walked back across with
it tugging at my fingers. Climbing down, I fumbled the pole away to the ground, which is not the
sort of thing that happens to professionals.

The performers' camp lay south, past a turnstile. Next to the turnstile, in a small upright cage,
sat Harry, the guard, and he wasn't letting a towner through. So I untied the balloon and breathed
in the helium. The balloon shrank before my eyes, then shot out of my hand and zipped away spitting
gas.

"Hello," I squeaked at the guard.

The voice startled me, but it was very familiar to Harry. Something buzzed in the turnstile, and I
was in.


3. CLOWN ALLEY

On the west side of the camp sat a dilapidated trailer, off-kilter on its blocks. I put on the
clown mask and knocked. Chuckles the clown squinted at me through a layer of cold cream and flung
the door open.

Inside it was all grungy costumes, cracked mirrors and the smell of makeup and clown sweat. Comrade
Thumb sat on an upper bunk swinging his legs. Chuckles was taking off his makeup, and he mistook me
for somebody called Malcom.

"Sorry about the door, Malcom, but Johnny Tin Plate's out there nosing around. Close it, will you?"

I closed it. On the lower bunk was an ash tray heaped with black ash, and when I stirred it a scrap
of newsprint came to light.

"Let's just hope he doesn't find the grift," Chuckles was saying. "It'll be okay unless somebody
tips him off about shoving Annie Oakley under the old front by the elephant tent --"

He stopped mid-sentence, whirled round, and pulled off my mask. I found myself eye to eye with a
very unhappy clown, whose red greasepaint smile practically melted off his face. He snatched back
the ash tray and threw me out by the scruff of my neck. But I kept the scrap of newsprint.

I crawled under the pleated canvas wall into the prop tent. Johnny Tin Plate means the law. An
Annie Oakley is a free ticket with a hole punched in it. I filed both away.


4. THE LIONS

In a pile of garbage I turned up the unmarked ticket of some luckless circus-goer. I punched out
its blue dot, and the turnstile fed it back to me as if I had paid. Along the midway a pitchman was
selling Dr. Nostrum's tonic to the detective, who already looked like he'd been drinking the stuff
all evening. The pitchman tried his speech on me too, about pyorrhoea and goitre and bilious
derangements, then gave up and went back to his better customer.

In a dark cage near the elephant tent hung a hoop of keys in a spot of light. I fished them off the
wall with the pole. Inside that cage was a bucket. The keys opened a locked compartment, which gave
up a rawhide bullwhip, and from the fat lady's sideshow I took a stool.

Then I walked into the lions' den.

There were two lions, one smooth-bodied and one shaggy. I cracked the whip over the smooth one, and
it rolled over obediently in the sawdust. I cracked it again, and it slunk off to a corner and
yawned. The shaggy one kept pacing and watching me, so I held the stool out at it like a real lion
tamer while I lifted the grate on a passage leading out of the cage. I threw a lump of meat into
the passage, stepped out, and the lions went after it. Then I lowered the grate behind them.

Under the lion stand was a cigarette case.

Harry, in his booth, ran his hands over it. "Oh, that's Andrew's cigarette case. He's one to steal
a smoke now and then; behind Jenny's back, of course. She'd kill him if she ever found out."

Andrew and Jenny were one act: half a man, half a woman, sharing one body. When I showed Andrew the
case, he went purple. When I gave it to Jenny, her memory came back. Andrew had been doing dirty
work behind her back, and he was supposed to meet his fellow thugs at Katz's trailer later. She
chased him out of the sideshow and down the midway, shrieking.


5. UNDER HYPNOSIS

Rimshaw the Incomparable kept a dim parlour with a tufted black leather sofa, and he wore an
immaculate tuxedo. He punched my ticket and asked what I wanted. I asked him to hypnotise me.

"RE-LAX!!! Now count backward slowly from one hundred."

I was back in the grandstand earlier that evening, high up in the blues, in a crowd at a state of
pandemonium. My stomach was growling. A hawker came along the row calling, "Get your popcorn here.
Get your granola bars here." I ordered candy and passed my $1.85 along the row, and a standing
ovation swallowed him up.

So I went for candy myself. I shoved up and down and sideways through the stands, through choruses
of "Move it!" and "Down in front!", and when I finally came out at the bottom, a small, hairy
animal dropped onto me from the top of the big top. It was a monkey, and it stayed on my back.

Then the concession line. The long line was endless. When a short line started up beside it, I
switched, and at once a man called Jerry recognised a whole baseball team of old friends and let
them all in ahead of me. I went back to the long line, which by then had emptied into the short
one, and I was handed a frozen banana before I could count my change. The monkey caught the smell
of the fruit, so I dropped the banana. The monkey climbed off and took it away with him.

The hawker, on his break, told me he'd sent me a granola bar instead of candy, since that was all
he had, and that the man next to my seat was holding it for me. So I climbed back up through the
stands, row by row, until the man tossed it to me. It bounced off my head and dropped through the
seats, and just before it hit the ground I woke up in a cold sweat on Rimshaw's sofa.

The granola bar was real, then, and it had fallen somewhere. I found it in the garbage under the
stands.


6. TINA AND MAHLER

Tina the fat lady is so large that walking from one half of her to the other is a long haul with
little change of scenery. She took my granola bar and ground it up without a word. Then she seemed
to notice me for the first time, sighed wistfully, which raised a gale, and held out a hand the
size of a wrecking ball. I shook it. Her whole weight settled into my arms like a sandbag. When I
walked back round to her other half, there was a transistor radio lying there.

The radio had a tape deck. In the menagerie, a cage held an expensive pair of headphones lying in
the straw. On the roof of the elephant tent I could get a clear signal, so I climbed up and
listened to the tape: a Jimi Hendrix guitar solo, and under it, very quietly, Rimshaw's voice
repeating over and over, "At the clap of my hands you shall obey my every command."

I tuned the radio to 1170, where a station was playing soothing classical music, and recorded it
over the tape. Climbing down, I fell off the cage. It didn't kill me. It only hurt my pride.

Mahler, the circus gorilla, lived in the cage next door. When I played him the tape, he snatched
the headphones and took them off to a corner, his savage breast soothed. In his straw, under a
little trap door, I found a ribbon from a small girl's hair.


7. THE ELEPHANT AND THE OFFICE

In the prop tent I set off a mousetrap with the pole, so as not to lose a finger, and laid it down.
A field mouse came for the cheese, and I caught it under the bucket. Then I took it to Hannibal of
the Jungle, the bull elephant. He took one look at it and began rocking from side to side, and when
the mouse scampered under his feet, he trumpeted, snapped his chain, and stampeded straight through
the fence.

The hole led to Mr. Munrab's white wagon. There was a stepladder at the back, and on the roof a
crank that slid open an aluminium panel over the office. Munrab was at his desk below. I knocked on
his door from above, and when he went out to look, I slipped in through the panel and locked the
door behind me. On the desk was a spreadsheet of one performer's pay, a list of numbers going
steadily down, and the performer was named Eddie Smaldone. Somebody started trying to force the
door. I pushed the desk under the panel and climbed out again.

"That's Chuckles's real name," said Harry.


8. THE BLUE ROOM

Under the old sideshow front by the elephant tent, just as Chuckles had said, I slid my Annie
Oakley through the gap. A secret panel opened onto the Blue Room, a smoky little den with a poker
game in one corner and a tight-jawed blackjack dealer. I lost a dollar at his table. Something
tapped my foot eight times while I played.

When I came back, Comrade Thumb having found me once more in the dark, a different dealer was
there, and under the floor-length cloth of his table was a suitcase. When I lifted it, a muffled
scream came from inside.

Billy Monday and the dealer jumped me, wrestled the suitcase away, and doubled me over with a
punch. I saw Billy's silhouette on top of a cage as he threw something bulky onto the elephant tent
and climbed up after it.

I followed him up. On the tent roof, a sleek metal shaft punched up through the canvas beside me
with a muffled pop, its tip smoking. Somebody inside was hunting me with an elephant prod. The next
time it came up, I grabbed it and pulled. There was a struggle, and then a shriek, as whoever was
holding it went down with it, burned through the canvas. A moment later I heard someone helping the
groaning victim out of the tent.

The detective was lying in the sawdust by the big top. I fetched water from the fountain in the
bucket and poured it over his face. He came up spluttering, threw his flask of Dr. Nostrum's at the
turnstile, and handed over what Munrab had given him: the ransom note and a trade card.

The note was made of letters cut out of a newspaper: "thE kId iS sAFe aND sOUnD. FOr nOw. DoNT
leaVe TowN WItHOut HeR - staY tuNeD foR deTaILs."

I found Chuckles out of costume, polishing the aluminium trailer at the east end of the camp. I
showed him the ribbon. I showed him the scrap of newsprint: he made paper dolls for the local kids,
he said. I showed him the ransom note, which he read with moving lips, and the spreadsheet: "I
don't know anyone by that name." When I called him Eddie, he snorted.

When I showed him the trade card, he screwed the cap back on a flask of Dr. Nostrum's and hurried
off into the dark.


9. CHELSEA

Jenny's boudoir had a wardrobe full of his-and-her clothes, and in the pocket of one of them I
found a veil. With the veil, the dress and the jacket on, I looked enough like Andrew Jenny to
knock at Katz's trailer.

"Andrew, where is Eddie?!" said a thick German voice from behind the curtain. "And Willie! We must
talk over what to do. I go now and bring back them, and the roustabout too. You be guarding in
here." Out stepped the great Gottfried Wilhelm von Katzenjammer, and off he went.

Inside, a moose head hung on the wall, and a crowbar leaned in the corner looking more like a
weapon than a tool. Behind the moose was a hole about a foot square, leading into a crawl space.
Too small for me.

I took the crowbar to Chuckles's trailer and wrenched the door open. Comrade Thumb lay on the upper
bunk, perfectly still, with his eyes wide open. I carried him across the camp in my arms and posted
him through the hole in Katz's wall. There was a scurrying, some cursing in Russian, and then a
small face rose slowly into the opening: the missing girl, tired, tousled, her hair without its
ribbon, too worn out even to be frightened.

I lifted Chelsea out.

Harry called out from his cage as I carried her past: "Nice going, towner. It just shows you what
can be accomplished when you start out the evening with a little helium, eh?"

But when Mr. Munrab saw me with his daughter in my arms, his mind jammed, and he ran. In the
menagerie he found the detective, pointed at me, and said, "There is the perpetrator. Shoot him."
Chelsea screamed. Metal screeched. A wall of bristly fur knocked me flat, and when I got up, Munrab
was chasing a giant ape toward the big top.


10. THE BIG TOP

Mahler stood on the high platform above the ring, with Chelsea limp under one arm. Munrab paced the
sawdust below, a complete wreck. A roustabout stood nearby.

I clapped my hands. The roustabout's eyes went vacant and he stood up straight, as Rimshaw's tape
had taught him.

"Get the net," I said, and he went tearing out of the tent. He came back with the safety net over
his shoulder and Comrade Thumb in tow. Munrab and the others caught hold of it and tried to guess
where she would fall.

I dragged the lion stand out of the den so that I could reach the tangled ladder, climbed to the
platform, and left the radio there playing classical music. When Mahler saw me coming, he climbed
off into the guy wires, and when I shook the platform, he scrambled across to the far side.

Halfway across the wire, the music stopped. It was Pledge Week at WPDL, and the announcer would not
play a certain piece by Vivaldi until one more listener phoned in.

So I turned round on the high wire, which takes nerve, and went back down. As I reached the ring,
the rest of them came in through the wings, Andrew Jenny, Chuckles, Rimshaw, Billy Monday and von
Katzenjammer, and every one of them took hold of the net. I ran to Munrab's office and pledged.

I climbed up with the music playing and walked out across the wire toward the gorilla. Mahler
screamed and beat his chest. Then the music reached him, and he loosened his grip. One more step,
and he relaxed completely and let her go, and Chelsea fell and fell into the safety of the net.

The cheering annoyed Mahler. He stamped once and bounced me off the wire, and I caught it with one
hand as I fell, and hung there. Down below, everyone was forgiving everyone, and nobody looked up.
My grip loosened. Then my hands slipped.

I landed in the net. "Hip hip hooray!" they shouted, and threw me high into the air. I floated back
down into their arms and went up again for a second "Hip hip hooray!", and somewhere near the top
of the big top, I passed out, not from the height but from sheer exhilaration.

I had saved The Traveling Circus That Time Forgot. I had stolen the spotlight, cheated death, and
heard the thunder of applause after all.
