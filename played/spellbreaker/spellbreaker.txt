SPELLBREAKER
The last Enchanter

I was there when magic ended. I was the one who ended it. I want to tell you how it happened,
because in a few years nobody will believe that any of it was real.

---

By then I was the Leader of the Circle of Enchanters. Belboz had retired, as he had promised, to
the Enchanters' Retreat high in the mountains, and the young novice who had once been sent after
Krill because nobody would miss him sat in the old man's chair and tried to look as if he deserved
it.

And magic was going wrong.

Not dramatically. Not at first. A rain spell would bring hail. A light spell would flicker and die.
Across the kingdom, spells that had worked for a thousand years began to fail, a little more each
month, and nobody knew why. So the guilds called a council in Borphee, in the great Council Chamber
of the Guild Hall, and every guildmaster in the land came to complain to us: ten of us Enchanters,
each the master of a chapter somewhere in the land, standing together in a nervous clump.

They were not kind. Sneffle of the Bakers said that the gloth spell, which used to fold his pastry
dough exactly eighty-three times, now hardly worked, and when it did the butter melted, and did we
know how hard it was to make butter pastries by hand? Hoobly of the Brewers said that the beer came
out of the vats tasting of cherries, or worse, and that the last vat tasted as if grues had been
bathing in it, and without magic there was not going to be any beer. That got the crowd's
attention. Gzornenplatz of the Huntsmen, a big, simple, angry man, said his men had been attacked
by rat-ants that had slipped through the old warding spells. Somewhere at the back of the room
someone said the word "traitors," and it went around the room like a cold wind.

Then Ardis of the Guild of Poets took the floor, and in the middle of a splendid speech, just as he
was reaching an insulting bit of mythology in iambic hexameter, he turned green. His chin
stretched. His skin grew slimy. And in the blink of an eye there was a large orange newt standing
at the podium, saying "Breek! Co-ax! Co-ax!"

I looked around. Every guildmaster in the room had been turned into a frog, or a toad, or a
salamander. Every single one, except me.

No. Except me and one other. At the back of the room, a shadowy figure in a dark cloak slipped
quietly out the door.

---

I went after it. Of course I did. Through the Guild Hall, where the buffet lunch had been reduced
to one loaf of bread, which I took on my way past because I have learned to take everything, and
out into Belwit Square.

The square was full of orange smoke, and the figure was gone.

I cast lesoch, a gust of wind, at the cloud. The first time the casting felt wrong and nothing
happened. That was the other thing about those days: spells failed. Not always, but often enough
that you learned to cast twice. The second time the wind built to a gale and tore the cloud apart,
and when it was gone, lying on the cobbles, was a small white cube. Featureless. Perfect. Not quite
of this world.

I picked it up. I took out my magic burin, the enchanted pen every Enchanter carries, and wrote "1"
on it.

Then I cast blorple, which explores an object's mystic connections, and the cube took me where it
belonged.

---

I'm going to tell the next part quickly, because it took weeks, and most of it I spent being
frightened, wet or tired.

There were twelve cubes, as it turned out, and each one was the key to a strange little room
somewhere outside the world: a room of packed earth, a room of soft warm flesh, a room under the
sea in a bubble of air, a room of cloud, a room of shifting shapes, a room of bones, a room of
glowing string, a room of light, a room of nothing at all, a room of darkness, a room of fire, and
the last, a room of magic. Each room had doors, and each door opened onto some lonely corner of the
real world. And in each of those corners, somebody or something was guarding the next cube.

The room of earth dropped me out of the sky. I fell for a long time over the wilderness until a
roc, a bird the size of an elephant, caught me in its claws and carried me to its nest in the
mountains. In the nest, next to an egg the size of a wagon, was a stained scroll with the caskly
spell, which makes a thing perfect, and a cube I couldn't reach.

In some ruins under the earth, wedged against a pillar by rushing water, I found a zipper. I mean
the kind of zipper you'd find on a coat, except that this one, when you opened it, opened onto a
dim little pocket of somewhere else, and you could put anything into it, and take it out again.
Inside, when I reached in, my hand closed on a flimsy scroll. Girgol: stop time. I didn't know then
where that zipper had come from. I know now.

On a narrow cliff path I disturbed the rocks, and a whole mountainside came down on me. I read the
girgol scroll at the last possible moment. The rocks stopped in the air, and the dust hung like
fog, and I climbed up the frozen boulders as if they were a staircase, to the mountain top, where I
found a gold coin worth five hundred zorkmids and a hermit in a stone hut that was held together
partly with mud and partly with a white cube. I cast caskly on his hut. It melted and flowed and
rebuilt itself, perfect, and the cube rolled out onto the floor between us. The hermit called me a
meddlesome mage. He was right.

---

The second cube took me to a meadow, where I pulled up a tall weed with yellow flowers dripping
pollen. The first cube took me back to a cave with an ogre in it, sneezing. I planted the weed on
his floor and cast throck, which makes plants grow, and the weed grew into a tree. "Ragweed!"
screamed the ogre, and then he could do nothing at all but sneeze. I walked straight past him into
his lair and took his gold box, with the third cube inside it, and the espnis scroll for sleep.

The third cube put me in a bubble of air under the sea. I stepped out of it and fell into the
ocean, and the cube sank, and a great grouper swam up to see what I was. I dropped my bread. The
grouper ate it and swam away happy, and I caught my cube. In a bottle floating nearby was the
liskon scroll: shrink a living thing. I shrank myself, to a tenth of my size, and squeezed into a
water pipe, and the current swept me along past the fourth cube, lodged in the slime.

In a polished corridor, an enormous serpent was swallowing its own tail, a living ring blocking the
way. I shrank it. As it shrank, it had to spit out more and more of its own tail to keep its
length, until at last the tip came free and the way was open. Beyond it, in a ruined temple, stood
a basalt idol shaped like a huge rodent. I brought it to life with malyon, and then, while it
searched hungrily for me, I made it sleepy with espnis. It yawned enormously. And then the malyon
spell wore off and it turned back to stone in the middle of its yawn, its jaws wide open, and the
fifth cube sitting on its tongue.

---

The fifth cube was a cloud. From the cloud I came down into a glacier, where I found the tinsot
scroll, for freezing things, and into a bazaar, where a carpet merchant tried to sell me a blue
carpet woven with a pattern of cubes. He wanted eight hundred zorkmids. I offered three hundred. He
was deeply wounded. I offered four. He asked to see my teeth. I offered five hundred, which was all
I had, and he took the hermit's gold coin, told me his family would go hungry tonight, and gave me
the carpet, and I believe he had his lunch in peace.

In a dungeon with a high trap door I froze the water channel with tinsot until the whole room
flooded, and froze myself an ice floe, and floated up to the trap door. In the castle above, in a
cabinet, I found a rotten old spell book. I cast caskly on it, and the mold drew back, and one
spell was still readable: snavig, change your shape.

Then I flew. I unrolled the blue carpet on top of the castle's guard tower and sat on it, and it
rose into the air, and a huge black bird that had been coming for me stalled in midair and fled
goggle-eyed. I flew west over the wilderness to the roc's nest and took the cube from under the egg
at last, and flew home again.

I turned myself into a grouper, to find the grouper's nest on the ocean floor, and the cube that
was in it. I almost drowned when I turned back. I bribed a living green rock with a fragment of
cooled lava from the foot of a volcano, and rode it across a plain of sliding boulders in a game of
tag against a brown rock with a cube on its back. The trick was to herd it into a corner. I jumped
onto the brown rock's back and took the cube, and it told me to go away.

I turned into a grue. I'm not going to describe what that felt like. In a dark cave full of grues,
beyond a pool of light that burned my new eyes, a cube sat on a pillar, and the grues looked at it
with something like worship. They watched me take it. They didn't understand what they were seeing.

And on a little island of rock in a lake of lava, at the top of a volcano, was the twelfth cube,
the room of magic. When I touched it, power rolled up my arm and all through me, every hair on end,
as though a veil had been taken from my eyes. I have never felt so strong. I didn't know yet what
that strength was costing.

---

I went to see Belboz.

He was meditating at the Retreat, with the stars above him, and when he saw me he looked at me with
deep suspicion. Someone exactly like me had come to him a few days before, he said, and had
betrayed itself because it did not know things that even the rawest apprentice would know. Prove
you are yourself, he said. In Borphee, who was famed for his skill with fireworks displays?

"Dimithio," I said. Every apprentice knows that.

He believed me then. He gave me a key, a wrought iron key, and told me about the legend of the
cubes: that when the world was made, the powers and forces of the universe were tied to little
cubes, and then the cubes were hidden away, because whoever held them could change the very
structure of the world.

---

There is a vault at the edge of everything, full of treasure, and in its outer room lie twelve
cubes that all look the same. One of them, and only one, is the real thing. The others are dummies.
I had three jindak spells, which detect magic, and three chances to find it, like weighing coins on
a balance. I split the cubes into three piles of four, and compared two piles, and swapped some,
and compared again, and swapped again. On the third try, I knew. And at that very moment a tiny
alarm fairy appeared over my head with a gong the size of a dinner plate, and began to bang it and
scream "Thief! Nasty thieving thief's here!" and I heard a key turning in the vault door.

I took the cube and was gone.

It led to a room of sand, and from the room of sand to a luxurious prison cell with a great oak
cabinet in it. The key fitted the cabinet. Inside was a blank sheet of vellum.

I thought about that for a while. And then, because I had done this sort of thing before and was
starting to understand how the universe works when you meddle with it, I learned the last spells I
would need, and left my spell book in the cabinet, and locked it, and blew the door open with
rezrov, and went back through the cube of sand to the very beginning.

---

The sand room's other exit opened into the ruins under the earth where I had found the zipper. But
not as I'd found them. Earlier. A pillar had only just fallen, and the water was rising fast, and
on the dry pavement lay a sack.

In the sack was a flimsy scroll. Girgol. The same scroll.

I copied it onto my blank vellum with the burin. Then I emptied everything I owned out of the
zipper and into the sack, and put the flimsy scroll inside the zipper, and closed it, and dropped
it on the pavement, and left it there for the rushing water to wedge against a pillar.

For a younger me to find, weeks ago.

I still don't know who made that scroll. I've stopped asking.

---

The twelfth cube took me to a throne room, and on the throne sat the shadowy figure in the dark
cloak, waiting for me.

I drew my knife. It raised one hand and froze me where I stood, unable to move so much as my little
finger. And then it pushed back its hood.

It was me. A shadowy, dark, transparent me.

"Magic is the most powerful force in the universe," it said, "but its exercise has its price. Each
time a great mage performs a spell, some part of the power in that spell is lost in shadow. A great
mage ultimately creates a shadow-self that is dimly aware." It grinned, with my face. "You have
become the most powerful wizard of all. So I, your shadow, have become very nearly as powerful as
you."

Every spell I had ever cast. Krill, the Sorcerer, the twelve cubes. Every time I had cast frotz or
rezrov or blorple, a little of it had fallen away into this. And the spells failing all over the
kingdom, the bread that didn't rise and the beer that tasted of cherries: that was this, too,
drinking the magic out of the world.

It took my cubes out of the sack one by one and set them in the air, and lines of light ran between
them. A square. A cube of light. A second cube of light. It took the twelfth cube out of my frozen
hand and pushed one glowing cube inside the other, and they twisted together into something my eyes
could not hold, a cube that was folded through a direction that doesn't exist. A tesseract. A
hypercube. And it set the twelfth cube, the cube of magic, in the center of it.

All the while the freeze was wearing off me. My fingers. My hands. My arms. My mouth. I made myself
be still.

The shadow, almost solid now, almost real, crouched to leap into the heart of the hypercube and
become everything. "Chortling gleefully," the old storytellers would say, and that is exactly what
it did.

And I read the girgol scroll.

Time stopped. The shadow hung in the air in mid-leap. The spell would not hold for long, not there.
I had two moves. I reached into the center of the tesseract and pulled out the twelfth cube, with
my last reserve of strength. And I put my knife in its place.

Time started again.

The shadow could not stop. It fell into the heart of the hypercube, and found there not the power
of the universe but a plain steel knife.

"No!" it screamed. "Stop! Fool, you've destroyed me! You've destroyed magic itself!" It grew
smaller and brighter and smaller, and the hypercube vanished with a pop, and the twelfth cube
melted in my hand like an ice cube.

---

I was standing in Belwit Square. The guildmasters were all around me, human again, poets and
brewers and huntsmen, and Belboz was there too, looking older than I had ever seen him.

"A new age begins today," he said, when I had told him everything. "The age of magic is ended, as
it must, for as magic can confer absolute power, so it can also produce absolute evil. We may
defeat that evil when it appears, but if wizardry builds it anew, we can never ultimately win. The
new world will be strange. But in time it will serve us better."

My spell book is still locked in a cabinet in a prison cell in a place that no longer exists. I
don't miss it as much as I expected. Sneffle has learned to fold his pastry by hand, all
eighty-three times, and says it tastes better. The brewers, Hoobly tells me, have learned something
about clean vats. The huntsmen have learned to build better fences. And a few of us, the ones who
used to be Enchanters, have started meeting in the old Guild Hall to talk about how the world
actually works, now that we can't make it do as we say. Somebody has suggested we call ourselves
scientists.

They chose me once because I was nobody. I'm nobody again. It's not so bad.
