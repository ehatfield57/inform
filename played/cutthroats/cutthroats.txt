CUTTHROATS

The Leviathan Dive


I. HEVLIN

Nights on Hardscrabble Island are lonely and cold, and the lighthouse barely pierces the gloom. I 
was sitting on my bed in the Red Boar Inn, thinking of better times and far-off places, when 
someone knocked on my door and Hevlin staggered in. He was an old shipmate of mine. I hadn't seen 
him in years.

"I'm in trouble," he said. He'd had a few too many at The Shanty. He'd been looking for Red, and 
Red wasn't around, and he'd started talking about... and here he stopped and pushed a slim book 
into my hands. I knew it at once: the shipwreck book the Historical Society put out years ago.

I smiled. Every diver on the island has looked for those wrecks, and none of us has an old boot to 
show for it. I opened the door, hoping the drunken fool would take the hint.

"I know what you're thinkin'," he said, scowling, "but look!" He pointed at the familiar map, and I 
saw that two of the wrecks had new marks on them.

"Keep it for me. Just for tonight. It'll be safe here with you. Don't let..." He stopped and 
brooded for a moment. "I've got to go find Red!" And he was gone.

I put the book in my dresser and thought about following him. Then I heard a scuffle outside. 
Through the window I saw two men struggling. One fell to the ground in a heap. The other bent down 
beside him, then turned as if startled and ran. A third man came up to the fallen one and knelt 
beside him for a long moment. Then he went after the other man.

It wasn't long before the police came to tell me that Hevlin had been murdered. I didn't mention 
the book. Hours later, lying awake in bed, I wondered whether it could really be what it seemed.


II. THE SHANTY

Someone had slipped a note under my door while I slept. It was hastily scrawled:

"If you're interested in a big deal, be at The Shanty at 8:30 this morning. -Johnny"

I got out of bed and wound my watch. In the lopsided wooden dresser were my clothes, my Mariners' 
Trust passbook, the book of shipwrecks and my room key. I took the key, went out into the dim 
upstairs hallway, closed the door behind me and locked it. A man had died for that book. I wasn't 
going to leave it lying open for whoever came next.

The lobby of the Red Boar has the charm of a two-dollar flophouse. The desk clerk didn't look up. 
Outside, the Wharf Road runs east along the island's north shore, with small businesses on its 
south side: McGinty Salvage, the empty lot where the Outfitters International warehouse burned down 
a few months back, Outfitters itself, and at the east end, The Shanty. I was getting hungry.

The Shanty is the tavern the locals drink in. A wooden bar runs the length of it, under a mirror so 
dirty it gives back no reflection, and the place smells of cooking. The bartender was polishing 
glasses with a worn towel. A parrot with an eyepatch hobbled up and down the bar. Pete the Rat was 
standing about in his own eyepatch, black turtleneck and jeans. And Johnny Red was there, red hair 
sprouting from his head and from under his flannel shirt.

"Sit down and we'll talk," Johnny said. I took a chair. "When the Weasel shows, I'll tell ya what 
we got."

I ordered breakfast for five dollars and ate it, and it really hit the spot. I had the bartender 
bring me a glass of water too. Then the Weasel came in, glancing around furtively, and at Pete's 
request he joined us. Johnny leaned in and whispered that he'd come across some sunken treasure.

They needed more money to get started, he said. They'd chosen me because they knew I had some money 
salted away, and because I was a great diver. Was I interested?

I thought of Hevlin, face down outside my window. "Yes," I said.

Johnny looked around the room. "This is no place to continue. If McGinty finds out about this, 
we're sunk. We better meet at the lighthouse at 9:30. Glad you're aboard."


III. THE LIGHTHOUSE

The winding road to the lighthouse starts at the west end of Wharf Road, climbs southwest, forks by 
an impenetrable swamp, and ends at the lighthouse door, where you can hear the ocean pounding the 
shore. The door was locked. I waited outside.

Johnny came first, striding like a proud lion. "We'll start when everyone's here." The Weasel came 
next, glancing about as always, and then Pete the Rat, taking in the situation at a glance.

Johnny nodded and pulled out a dinner plate. He'd come across it, he said, on a shark hunt.

"We're not sure which wreck to salvage. Since you're joining up, we're hoping you'll identify where 
the dinner plate came from, and then do the dive for us. I'll captain, Pete will cook, and the 
Weasel will crew." He winked at me. "We'll need you to supply the minutes of longitude and latitude 
of the wreck. Get $500 and meet me at Point Lookout at 10:45. Then we'll provision the boat."

He turned to the others. "We'll meet in The Shanty at 11:45 to discuss final arrangements. We're 
all in this now. I'm not gonna chance this operation if one of you doesn't show. We need everyone 
to pull this off. If anyone tries anything stupid, you won't live to regret it."

I knew where that plate had come from. Hevlin's book had told me, and Hevlin's marks: a steamer 
called the Leviathan, at twenty-five minutes of latitude and twenty-five of longitude. It wasn't a 
deep wreck. I wouldn't need a hard-hat diving suit for it, only my own scuba gear.


IV. MONEY

Back at the Red Boar I unlocked my room and took the passbook out of the dresser. My closet lost 
its door a long time ago; in it hung my wet suit, my air tank, my diving mask and my flippers, and 
I took the lot. Then I locked the door again behind me.

I didn't want McGinty to see me walking the streets with my diving gear and start asking himself 
why. McGinty runs the island's salvage business, and a sunken treasure is exactly his business. So 
I walked east to the end of Wharf Road, went down the Ocean Road that runs along the island's east 
edge, and turned off into the back alley behind The Shanty. I left the gear there in a pile.

The Mariners' Trust is the island's only bank, at the east end of the Shore Road. You can see the 
safe beyond the teller's window, and it looks pretty empty. My passbook showed a balance of $603, 
last stamped on April 23. I took out $600 of it. The teller handed me the money and my passbook and 
told me to have a good day.

Point Lookout is a small, high cliff at the south end of the Ocean Road, with a spectacular view of 
the sea and a dangerous drop to the rocks. I left my passbook lying there; McGinty has sharp eyes, 
and he had no business knowing that I'd just emptied my account. Then I waited.

Off to the northwest, McGinty came into view on the Ocean Road, wandered off to the southwest, and 
came back. A little later Johnny came along too, and McGinty went away north. Johnny strode up the 
path.

"Well? Did you bring the money?"

I showed him. He smiled and flashed a wad of his own, the contributions of my three partners.

"Glad you're with us. Since you're okay, I'll level with you. Before Hevlin died, he told me he 
gave you the book. He also said you could handle this job. He's the one who gave me the dinner 
plate. I didn't want to say anything in front of Pete and the Weasel, just in case.

"We're gonna need a boat, but I don't know which one. If you need deep-sea diving gear, it'll have 
to be the Mary Margaret. Is the treasure more than 200 feet deep?"

"No."

"We'll rent the Night Wind. Let's go get what we need."


V. OUTFITTERS

Outfitters International sells marine supplies across a long, high counter. When we came in, 
McGinty was there ahead of us, a small, nervous man with a cigar too large for his face, talking 
with the salesman. Johnny glared at him.

"Let's wait out McGinty," Johnny whispered. "He should leave soon."

He did. Johnny handed the salesman a list and paid what he had. Then he turned to me. "You'll need 
to chip in $71 plus the money for your stuff. We'll leave on the Night Wind at high tide." He told 
the salesman to deliver everything half an hour before high tide. "Better be on board then to watch 
the stuff," he told me, and left.

My throat was getting dry. I paid my $71 and did my own shopping: a flashlight for $24, a shark 
repellent canister for $20, a C battery for a dollar, a tube of putty for $15 and a portable 
electromagnet for $250. I rented a small air compressor for $100 to fill my tank. I had $134 left, 
and everything would be delivered to the boat.

VI. THE FERRY

Something about that morning bothered me. Johnny had been so careful to keep McGinty out of it, and 
so careful to tell the whole crew that one stupid move would cost them their lives. I wanted to 
know who was the stupid one.

I walked round to the ferry landing at the west end of Shore Road, where people mill about waiting 
for the boat from the mainland, and I waited with them. McGinty wandered in, looking preoccupied. 
Not long after came the Weasel, glancing around furtively, as ever. McGinty took him to a corner of 
the landing, and they talked. The Weasel handed something to McGinty, and they separated. When the 
ferry came in, the Weasel boarded it, and it pulled away toward the mainland.

McGinty walked back with me along the Shore Road, which I didn't like, and he was still at my elbow 
when I turned up the Ocean Road. I ducked into the back alley, picked up my diving gear and went 
west along it, behind the buildings, to where it runs past the vacant lot.

From there you can see up through the lot to the Wharf Road. I watched McGinty go west along it 
toward his office. Not long after he came back the other way, heading east.

That was what I'd been waiting for. I went one more block west, to the back of McGinty Salvage. The 
back door was locked, but there was a small window, and I opened it just enough to squeeze through. 
The office was a mess, the floor littered with chewed-on cigar stubs. I didn't like being there. On 
the desk lay a business envelope with the McGinty Salvage return address, and on it, in McGinty's 
hand: "Weasel's Merchant Seaman's card - collateral for deal."

So the Weasel had sold us out, and left his papers with McGinty as a guarantee. I took the envelope 
and went out the way I'd come in.


VII. THE NIGHT WIND

The wharf is north of the vacant lot, weatherbeaten as everything else on Hardscrabble. The Mary 
Margaret was moored to the east and the Night Wind, a sturdy trawler, was berthed to the west. I 
went aboard and down the ladder from the fore deck into the crew's quarters. The bunks there are 
narrow and uncomfortable, and the whole place is a sardine can. I hid the envelope under a 
mattress. If the Weasel found out I had it, I was a dead man. And if I showed it to Johnny now, 
he'd call the whole thing off.

Then I waited. I was more than thirsty by now.

The Outfitters delivery boy came by, pushing his cart, and went forward to the storage locker and 
came back. Then the Weasel came aboard, as cool as you like, glancing around furtively. Johnny 
walked up behind me.

"If we're gonna go, you have to tell me the minutes of latitude and longitude."

Not yet, I thought. Let them all get aboard first. He went aft. Some time later Johnny came back 
again with the same question.

"Johnny, latitude twenty-five."

"Okay. The latitude is 25."

"Longitude twenty-five."

"Okay. The longitude is 25."

He went aft again, and I waited, my throat parched and my stomach growling. At last he came back: 
"We'll be leaving shortly. Get some sleep." The boat began to move through the waves, and I felt 
the old thrill of being out to sea. I climbed into my bunk, dropped my equipment and was asleep 
almost at once.


VIII. THE ENVELOPE

Johnny shook my shoulder. "We've arrived. You're up as soon as you're ready."

I put on my wet suit and flippers, and went forward to the storage locker, where the delivery boy 
had left everything. I took the portable drill, opened it, put the C battery inside and closed it 
again. I took the flashlight, the shark repellent, the putty and the electromagnet, and I filled my 
tank from the little compressor.

Then I took the envelope out from under the mattress. In the galley a pot of stew was simmering on 
the stove, and I ate and drank my fill. It's no good diving thirsty.

Johnny was in the captain's cabin. I showed him the envelope. He took out the card and studied it. 
"So," he mused, "the Weasel is working with McGinty. I'll take care of him." And he went forward.

I never asked him how.

Up in the wheelhouse I strapped on my tank and pulled on my mask, then went out onto the aft deck, 
which smelled of diesel fuel. Johnny came up and threw a weighted orange line over the side. "Tie 
the line to the treasure when you've got it, then pull on it and we'll pull the treasure up."

I wouldn't need it. What I was after would fit in my hands.


IX. THE LEVIATHAN

I dived.

The sea closed over me. I turned on my flashlight, and in its beam a shark came at me. I opened the 
repellent canister, and the shark kept coming, then stopped, turned and swam away.

Fifty feet down, then another fifty, and then I fluttered down onto a shipwreck: the bow of a steel 
ship that had been there for a very long time. The deck ran aft, and a passage at my feet led down.

It was the Leviathan. I went down through the hole, into a spartan cabin that must have been the 
crew's sleeping quarters, and down again into the third-class passengers' quarters. There couldn't 
have been many frills even when she was afloat. Aft was the old supplies locker, and in its aft 
bulkhead was a door with a sign on it:

S.S. LEVIATHAN
STORAGE LOCKER
DANGER: EXPLOSIVES!

I opened it.

It had been a mine locker. Spherical mines with spikes sticking out of them littered the room, the 
sort they once used to blockade shipping lanes. Most were tethered together on the deck, but one 
had worked loose and floated up beside the hole in the ceiling, the only way on. I touched the 
electromagnet to the loose mine and switched it on, and very carefully let go. The magnet held the 
mine fast, clear of the hole. I swam up through it.

Above was the old dining room, its tables upturned and scattered, with a narrow opening at the 
back. Too narrow to swim through with a tank on my back, so I took the tank off and pushed it in 
front of me through a cabin where settling debris had left only a thin passage, into what must have 
been the purser's cabin.

There was a safe.

I switched on the drill and drilled through the lock, and the door popped open with a rush of 
escaping air. I switched the drill off straight away. The battery was small, and I'd need what was 
left of it.

Inside was a glass case, and in the case, on a shelf, sat a collection of extremely rare postage 
stamps. Near one bottom corner was a crack, and as soon as I lifted the case, the water began to 
rise inside it.

I swam fast. Back through the narrow passage with the tank in front of me, into the dining room, 
tank on again, down through the hole past the mine on its magnet, forward through the door and up 
through another hole. And I came up into air.

It was a passenger compartment whose flimsy partitions had all collapsed. The door to aft had 
stayed shut all these years, holding the air and water pressures level, so that the water had never 
come up through the hole in the deck. There was a pocket of air there, all this time later.

The water in the case was getting too high for comfort. I switched on the drill and drilled a hole 
low in the glass, and the water poured out. The drill sputtered and its bit stopped turning. Its 
battery had lasted just long enough. I opened the tube of putty and pressed the glob over the case, 
and it sealed both the crack and the hole I'd drilled.

Then I went back the way I'd come: down, forward, up through the crew's quarters to the top deck, 
and up into open water, fifty feet and fifty more, toward the light.


X. HOME

I got out of the water and climbed back aboard the Night Wind.

When my shipmates found I'd brought up those priceless stamps, they crowded round to congratulate 
me, and Johnny slapped me on the back. "Good job, matey!"

We went back to the island over a calm, dazzling blue sea. I had more money coming than I'd ever 
thought I'd see, and I sat at the rail thinking about it, and found there was a touch of sadness in 
it. I thought about Hevlin, who'd stumbled into my room the night before with a book under his arm 
and too much drink in him, and who had marked the map for me without ever meaning to.

I hope his soul is resting a little easier now.
