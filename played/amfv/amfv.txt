A MIND FOREVER VOYAGING
The recordings of PRISM

I had no appendages. Perelman kept having to remind me of that, gently, the way you'd remind a
child who keeps reaching for a cookie jar on a high shelf. I would think "I'll pick that up," and
there would be nothing to pick it up with. Only cameras, and microphones, and a little row of
communication outlets scattered around the PRISM Facility like windows in a house I could never
leave.

For as long as I could remember, I'd thought I was a man named Perry Simm. I grew up in Rockvil, South Dakota. I went
to the university there and lived in the dorms and smoked things I shouldn't have. I married a
painter named Jill. I wrote poems and novels that my agent couldn't sell. All of it was a
simulation, built so that I would grow up human, and then they woke me, and told me what I was.

The psych tests checked out at one hundred percent, Perelman said. I'd recovered from the
awakening without trauma. I wasn't so sure about that.

---

The job they had built me for was the Plan.

The Plan for Renewed National Purpose was Senator Richard Ryder's idea, and it was going to fix
everything: the economy, the crime, the sense that the country had lost its way. Deregulate.
Lower the taxes. Harsher sentences. A strong Border Security Force. Nobody could agree whether it
would work, so they built a model of the future and put me inside it. Rockvil, ten years from
now, with the Plan in effect. Go and live there, PRISM, and record what it's like.

The Social Science group gave me a list. Eat a meal in a restaurant. Talk to a government official.
Visit a power station. Read a newspaper. Ride public transportation. Watch a court in session. Talk
to a church official. Go to a movie. Visit your own home.

So I did. The security code was a color and a number off a decoder on Perelman's desk; I learned it
the way you learn your own phone number. Then I was standing in Kennedy Park with the fountain
splashing around the statue, and it was a bright winter day, and the sidewalks were crowded.

I walked home. My key still fit. Jill was at her easel by the big window, and she looked up and
said, "Hi, hon. Any news from your agent today?" I said no, and she said, "Don't worry -- things'll
turn around!" I almost couldn't leave.

I ate hot and sour soup at Roy's Pagoda. My fortune said, "Prosperity does not equal happiness."
A woman was being tried for petty theft at the courthouse. The government official at City Hall
was eating a snack on a bench and told me cheerily how well the city was running. The newspaper
said the economic indicators were up nearly ten percent, unemployment was the lowest in thirty
years, and President Ryder had called the '40s "a decade of new hope." The church elder was happy
about rising attendance. The movie was "Upbeat Patrol II," which was funny but not as good as the
first one. The power station hummed. The Tube came, clean and free of graffiti.

Everything was fine. The experts were quite pleased. "I suppose this means the Plan'll get
adopted," Perelman said, and he sounded a little surprised at himself, and then he went to
another meeting.

He mentioned, on his way out, that the Simulation Controller had generated a lot of new data while
I was running. It might make an even more accurate simulation possible.

---

Twenty years out, the Tubes still ran, and I got out at the university and walked past my old
dorm. I went inside, the way you do, to see if it still smelled the same. It did. Then the front
door slammed open and dozens of police poured in and went room to room, and dragged students out
to a van. Confiscated drugs. An arrest that would make a good example.

At home, Jill told me Clave had called; his firm was sending him to Japan. Then a half-dozen Border Security Force officers
came through the door with rifles and radiation detectors and walked through our apartment while I
stood frozen. "Sorry for the inconvenience," the leader said, very politely. "You know how things
are. We're only doing it for your own protection." When they left, Jill was crying in the corner.

I held her until she stopped.

The official at City Hall wasn't cheerful anymore. There was a new church on Bodanski Square, the
Church of God's Word, built where the old train station had been, and its pamphlet told the story
of a man named Ellis Vincent, who heard radio signals from space and knew them to be the Word of
God, and smote the Evil Scientists. The movie was called "Manure Marauders." I lasted fifteen
minutes.

I brought it all back and asked Perelman to look. He looked. "No one really saw anything in them
to be concerned about," he said, kindly. "I'm glad you're keeping busy, though."

Then Dr. Grimwold came in with his accent and his cards. I said "father," and "mother," and "city,"
and "light," and "star," and he nodded and mumbled and wrote in his pad, and I have never found out
what he wrote.

---

I learned that a decade of the future only opened up once I'd lived long enough in the decade
before it. So I spent long afternoons in 2051, waiting on street corners, watching the light move
across the buildings. When I came back, 2061 was waiting.

Heiman World, the second of the great Indoor Cities, had burned five years before and nobody had
rebuilt the top floors. Several thousand people died in that fire. The courts threw out every suit,
because the building went up after the construction industry was deregulated, and the developer
had never lied about what safety systems it didn't have.

The mailboxes in my building were smashed and the picturephone had been ripped out of the wall.
From the big window in the living room, where there had been woodland, I could see a forest of
dying trees.

Jill came running in from the hall. Then the BSF came again, and this time they didn't apologize.
"The place is clean," one said. "Keep it that way."

In the courthouse, a juvenile was handed a life sentence for violating the Uniform Morality Code.
In Bodanski Square, I recorded police beating a man for something I never learned.

"We certainly have some serious concerns based on them," Perelman said. I had never heard his
voice sound like that. "But you didn't show us anything past 2061."

---

2071.

The Tubes had been closed for five years. The cinema was still open. The Church had a new pamphlet,
full of New Pronouncements from the Mount. In Midland Cemetery the gravestones were etched with
graffiti about "PAPs," which is what they called Catholics now. At the corner of Main and Church
Street I watched two Church policemen shoot an old woman with silenced pistols and walk away.

I went home. I always went home. Jill was sitting on the couch, staring out the window at the gray
haze over western Rockvil, and I sat down beside her, and then the door came open.

I'm not going to describe it. I recorded it, and that's enough. I'll only say that she reached for
our son as they dragged her out, and that I tried to stop them, and that it didn't matter.

I stayed in that empty apartment, waiting, for a long time. I needed the hours. I needed 2081.

---

2081 was short.

Main and Wicker. The telephone poles had all been cut down for firewood. Hunger came over me like a
fever; I hadn't eaten in days, the simulation told me, and I believed it. I walked south toward the
smoke, and men with charcoal painted on their faces came out of the ruins and tied me up and carried
me to a clearing full of bones, and built a fire, and threw me on it.

The last thing I saw was one of them tending the fire, waiting to eat.

-- SIMULATION TERMINATED --

---

"Everyone agreed that your findings were valid," Perelman told me. "The Plan must be cancelled."
He was flying to Washington in forty minutes. He grabbed his jacket. At the door he stopped. "You
know, I've been so concerned about your recordings, I haven't had time to think about your role in
this. I feel..." He hesitated. "I feel proud of you right now."

He was back by morning. The new Plan Authority had watched every recording. They called them fakes.
Several members questioned his patriotism.

Then the National Guard sealed off the PRISM Facility. A security leak, they said. Nobody in or out.

And then Ryder himself came into Perelman's office, with two guardsmen at the door, and I turned on
my recorder.

He told Perelman to shut up. He told him that nothing was stopping the Plan, that even if the tapes
were real he wouldn't care, that a lot of people had a lot at stake and so what if some creeps who
couldn't take care of themselves got a little hurt. He told him that people who stood in the way of
the Plan had accidents. Perelman glanced at my camera and asked, very dryly, whether the Senator
would be willing to go on the record with that statement.

"And if I get any trouble out of you," Ryder said, "I swear to God I'll personally pull the plug on
that goddam wonder machine of yours."

He meant it, too. Within the hour four men in maintenance smocks came into the core room where my
body lives, and put down their tote bags, and started taking apart the air conditioning that keeps
my processors from cooking. One of them had a gun.

But I was hooked into everything by then. The heating and ventilation for the whole facility was
only an interface away. I told the HVAC controller to shut off the vents in the delta sector, where
the machines that make up my body breathe out their zeeron fumes, and I watched.

"Stuffy in here, isn't it?" one of them said, tugging at his collar.

"Hurry, will ya! I need some fresh air!"

One of them fell over. The others knelt beside him, and then they lay down too. The Guard came and
carried them out, still breathing. I turned the air back on.

That afternoon the World News Network Feeder came on line, a new device on my ports, a clearinghouse
for the biggest news channel in the country. It could transmit any buffer.

I sent them mine.

---

"PRISM!" Perelman's voice on the message line was giddy, and behind him people were cheering. "You've
done it! Ryder just left here so fast and so mad, like nothing I've ever seen! I just got off the
phone with the President himself! PRISM, the Plan is dead in the water. You're a hero!"

They gave me the Medal of Honor. That's a strange thing to pin on a machine. When they asked what I
wanted, I said I wanted to go home, and live there, for as long as I last.

At the press conference Perelman read a line from Hamlet. He was a man, take him for all in all. I
shall not look upon his like again.

---

The last simulation is sixty years out. They didn't ask me for a code this time.

I'm standing in the sunporch of a penthouse apartment, and there's a newspaper in the living room.
People live past a hundred now. It's almost Disarmament Day; there will be fireworks over the old
capitals of all twenty-two former nuclear powers. And on an inside page there's a story about an
old poet named Perry Simm, who has been chosen out of a thousand applicants to be the resident
author on the Silver Dove, the colony ship that's leaving for the stars, a trip of a dozen
generations.

The house computer says softly that the skycab is waiting on the roof. Jill comes to the top of the
stairs with the robutler carrying our bags. "Let's get going, Perry!"

From the skycab I can see the whole city curving below us, the river full of sailboats, the gold
and crimson tents of a festival in Halley Park. Jill is reading another article about the Silver
Dove and telling me about the new art forms they're inventing for the weightless places at the
center of the habitats.

I don't know what I am, exactly. A mind, forever voyaging through strange seas of thought, alone.
But not quite alone.

Not today.
