THE WITNESS
A detective's report, February 1938

The rain had been coming down all week, the way it does in Southern California when it finally
remembers how, and it was still coming down when I pulled up outside the Linder place at eight
o'clock on a Friday night with a telegram in my pocket.

The telegram had come that afternoon, from a man named Freeman Linder. He was in trade with the Far
East, he had money, and somebody was threatening his life. Would I come to the house tonight. I'd
have gone anyway. A detective on a city salary goes where the telegrams tell him.

The lot was screened from the street by a wooden fence, except on the east side, where it ran up
against a dark wall of bamboo woods. The house itself was a California bungalow that had got some
funny ideas from the Orient. I walked up the peastone driveway to the front porch, where a yellow
light hung by the door and a doorbell glowed at me like it was daring me to ring it.

I rang it. Inside somebody turned off a radio.

---

The man who opened the door was the butler, Phong. Straight black hair, folded eyelids, a gentle
open face and quick eyes that missed nothing, and a stout body that carried itself lightly, like a
man who knew how to fight and preferred not to. Fifty, maybe. Maybe a hundred, for all his face
would tell you. He took me through an entry with a little Shinto shrine and a scroll on the wall,
down a long hallway, toward voices talking half-loud and fast.

"Excuse me, sir," said Phong, "but the detective has arrived."

There were two of them in the living room, in front of a big fieldstone fire. The woman stopped
talking and gave me a look like I'd just whistled at her in the street. That was Monica, the
daughter. Mid-twenties, grey eyes, dark waved hair, navy blouse and tan slacks, and an expression
that would have frozen the fire. The man was Linder: six foot, big and quick like a cat, his
eyeglasses pushed up on top of his head, a peach-colored silk shirt, and sweat gleaming on his
forehead though the room wasn't warm enough for it.

He gulped down the rest of his drink. "Well, Detective, I'm anxious to get on with our business.
Let's you and I go to my office, so we can talk undisturbed." And he took me by the arm, like I was
a parcel he was afraid of losing.

---

The office was Pacific Trade Associates, according to the sign outside it, and it looked the part:
a big teak-and-mahogany desk covered with letters and newspapers and souvenirs from the Orient, a
carved chair behind it like a battleship, a plain wooden chair in front of it for people like me, a
lumpy green lounge against the wall. By the outside door and the window stood a grandfather clock
seven feet tall, ticking away like it had someplace to be. A cat was asleep in the corner. Linder
sat down in the carved chair and waited until I'd sat in the wooden one.

Then he told me his story. His late wife, may she rest in peace, had got herself involved with a
young man named Stiles. Linder had tried to put a stop to it. Since she died, this Stiles had gone
off the deep end and blamed Linder for it. And this morning, this had come. He handed me a note on
fine rag paper, in a spidery hand.

"Linder," it said. "Since Virginia died, I've lost too much sleep because of you and your
harassments. The time has come to put this matter to rest once and for all. I'll be seeing you
sooner than you imagine. Stiles."

So I sat there in the wooden chair, and the rain hammered at the window, and then tapered off, and
then stopped. The clock chimed the half hour. At twenty-five to nine Monica burst in, in a felt hat
and a wool coat, struggling into her driving gloves, to say she was off to the pictures with
somebody named Terry. She hugged her father hard and buried her face in his shoulder. He pushed her
off. "You're leaving now? What about the threat on my life?" he said, with the hurt look of an
orphan pup. "You don't need me here," she said. "I'm not like Mother, you know." There were tears
in her eyes, and she wiped them away before they could fall, and she left.

I remember that. I didn't make anything of it at the time.

The clock struck nine. A minute went by, and another.

At two minutes past, the doorbell rang somewhere in the house. Linder looked toward the window. "I
don't think Phong has answered the door bell yet," he said, and he reached for the butler's button
on his desk, and at the same instant he shouted, "Stiles!"

I turned around in my chair and saw a figure outside in the dark. Then there was a flash and a bang
and the window came in in a hundred pieces. The cat went like a shot. The figure outside turned and
ran before I could see its face. When I turned back, Linder was slumping in his big chair with a
red stain spreading over his peach silk shirt. He teetered on the edge of the seat a second, and
then he went over onto the floor, and he was dead.

Three minutes past nine. And I had been sitting right there the whole time, watching. That's what
they hired me for, I suppose. To be a witness.

---

The first thing I did was stand up. The second thing I did, I'm not sure why, except that something
about the way he'd reached for that button was bothering me, was push the butler's button myself.

No bell rang. Instead, from inside the grandfather clock, there was a click.

I looked at the clock. It was a fancy one, with a dial for the day and the date and the phase of
the moon. Friday the 18th, the moon just past full. And the keyhole in its door was darker than a
keyhole has any business being. When I looked closer there was a fine dark powder around it, in the
brass.

Phong came in then. He saw Linder on the floor, and he turned away for a moment and bowed his head.
I asked him for the house keys and he gave them to me without any argument. "I don't see how Mr.
Linder can object now," he said. The doorbell rang again and he went to answer it.

I unlocked the outside door and went out onto the little concrete porch. Fresh footprints in the
mud, heading east toward the woods. A stepping-stone path ran south to the back gate, and lying on
it in the mud was a small handgun. I picked it up with my handkerchief.

When I came back in, my assistant, Sergeant Duffy, was standing in the office. Duffy has a way of
appearing out of nowhere, like a stage magician's rabbit. He'd come out on the streetcar, he said,
in case I needed help, and just as he walked up he'd heard a shot, and a man calling himself Stiles
had come bursting out of the woods behind the house straight into his arms. Duffy had put the
nippers on him and stowed him in the living room. Then Duffy scurried off, the way he does, to go
about his business.

I gave him the powder from the keyhole to analyze. Then I looked at the broken window. There was
some kind of electric relay on one edge of the frame, with white wires running to it, and along the
edge, hanging loose, the end of a piece of green wire that didn't seem to belong to anything. I
took that too.

---

Phong's room was down the hall. On his table lay a mystery novel, one of those whodunits set in the
future, about a murder in Connecticut. I opened it and it seemed oddly familiar, as if I might live
through it myself some day. Somebody was using a receipt as a bookmark: Fritzi's, fine merchandise
and quick loans, Cabeza Plana, California. December 1st, 1937. Sold to Nemo Newbourne, 137 East
Second Street. Two handguns, eight dollars, paid.

Nemo means "nobody". I put the receipt in my pocket.

The keys let me into the garage at the south end of the house, where a dark blue Bentley sat next
to an empty space with oil spots on the floor. A door on the east side led to a workshop, and the
workshop was an electrician's dream: switches, relays, spools of colored wire hanging on the walls,
and against one wall the main electric board and a homemade junction box with more wires running in
and out of it than you could shake a stick at. One of the spools was green. The same green.

Duffy found me there with his results, one after another, in the way only Duffy can. The powder
from the keyhole was gunpowder. The gun from the path had been fired recently, and it held traces
of cheap powder. And the green wire was ordinary sixteen-gauge bell wire.

Bell wire. The butler's button didn't ring any bell. It clicked something in the clock.

I stayed in the workshop and waited, because I was beginning to think I knew who I was waiting for.

---

At six minutes past eleven a car drove into the garage and a door slammed. Monica came rushing into
the workshop, still in her hat and coat, and went straight to the junction box and fiddled with the
wires for a minute. Then she turned around and saw me and gasped and grabbed at her throat. "My
God, you gave me a start!" Her face was the color of ash.

She didn't say anything else to me. When she left, I followed her, not too close: back through the
garage, where a ticket stub lay on the floor where it had fallen, up the hallway, into the office,
where she stood looking at what was left of her father, and back out again, and up the hall to her
bedroom, full of movie posters and pulp magazines and a portable phonograph. She went straight
through it into the bathroom and slammed the door, and I heard her being sick.

When she came back she sat down on the edge of her bed and buried her face in her hands. "Leave me
alone for a while. I'm really not well."

I asked her about her father.

She rambled, as if she were dreaming out loud. "To be honest, I feel relieved... that he's met his
Maker. Now I won't feel as if I'm under his thumb when he's home. He really treated all of us
like... his property. Even Mother. I guess I'm an orphan now, but..." Then she remembered who I
was, and closed up like a clam.

That was the motive. It was sitting right there on the bed in front of me, crying.

---

The coroner's men came for the body around half past eleven, in a hurry; it was a busy night at the
morgue. Duffy went along with them to make sure the autopsy got priority. I waited in Monica's
room. A little before midnight, she got up and went out, down the hall and back into the office,
and I went in right behind her.

She was standing by the clock, biting her nails.

I put the cuffs on her and fastened her to the lounge. She fought me, but not for long. In her coat
pocket was a single key. It fit the door of the grandfather clock. Inside, behind the long
pendulum, was a nest of relays and wires, a little mechanism that had been aimed at the big carved
chair behind the desk through the keyhole, and had been fired from the butler's button. The gun
itself wasn't in there anymore.

It was in her other pocket. A small handgun, the twin of the one in the mud outside. Two handguns,
eight dollars, Nemo Newbourne.

Phong came in while I was at the clock, and said "You rang?", and Monica said, cool as anything,
"Yes, Phong, I was testing a new twist on the household wiring that I thought of during the film.
Can't you talk this copper into letting me loose?" Phong opened his eyes wide for one moment and
didn't say a word. Then he turned and left. "If you need me again, just ring."

At half past midnight, Duffy came back from the morgue. Linder had died of a single small-caliber
bullet through the heart, he said, and there was something peculiar about it: the bullet had no
rifling marks at all. As if it had been fired through something that wasn't a gun barrel. Like a
keyhole.

I arrested Monica Linder for the murder of her father, and Duffy unfastened her from the lounge and
put his own nippers on her and walked her out to the police car waiting in the driveway, with its
engine purring in the cold.

---

Here is what happened, as near as I could piece it together, and as near as she told it in court.

Freeman Linder found out a year ago that his wife was seeing a young man named Ralph Stiles. The
marriage went bad, and then worse, until Virginia Linder took her own life with a gun. Linder
wanted revenge on Stiles, and he worked out a clever way to get it: he would frame Stiles for
attempted murder, with a detective sitting right there in the room as a witness.

He telephoned Stiles and told him to come round to the office door at nine for some pay-off money.
He sent me a telegram. And he had Monica, who was handy with wires, build him a little gun inside
the grandfather clock, aimed out through the keyhole, wired to the butler's button along with a
small charge of explosive on the window. When Stiles showed up outside, Phong rang the doorbell as
a signal, and Linder pushed his button, and the window blew in and the gun in the clock fired, and
Stiles panicked and ran, straight into Sergeant Duffy. Phong had planted a second gun, already
fired, out on the path for me to find. Monica had bought both guns, under the name of Nobody.

It should have been a near miss. It was supposed to be a bullet in the wall, and a man in prison
for something he never did.

But Monica had built the gun, and Monica had aimed it, and Monica had loved her mother and blamed
her father for her death. She aimed it at the carved chair. Then she went off to the pictures, and
left her father to pull the trigger on himself.

The police chief wrote to me at the end of the month to say that the jury, on my testimony and her
confession, had convicted her, and that it was ingenious work. He also mentioned another case he'd
like me to look at.

I suppose I did my job. I was there, and I saw it all, and I told what I saw. But I still think
about Monica in the doorway of the office at twenty-five to nine, in her felt hat and her driving
gloves, burying her face in her father's shoulder one last time. He pushed her away. She knew by
then what was going to happen. I think she was giving him one more chance to hold on.
