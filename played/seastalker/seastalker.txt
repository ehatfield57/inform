SEASTALKER

Charles Penrose and the Ultramarine Bioceptor


1. THE ALARM BELL

I was bent over my workbench, deep in the top-secret plans for the SCIMITAR, when Tip Randall burst
into my lab.

"Charles, snap out of it!" he yelled. "The alert signal's flashing on your videophone!"

The alarm bell was ringing on the big screen over my bench. Somebody was trying to reach me over
the private videophone network of Inventions Unlimited. My lab is one small corner of the Penrose
Research Lab, in the town of Frobton, and the SCIMITAR was the thing I cared about most in the
world just then. It was a submarine for capturing marine life on the ocean floor, and it was still
being tested.

I switched on the videophone. The picture was a blur and the sound was a crackle, so I adjusted it
until a woman's face swam into focus, holding a microphone. It was Commander Zoe Bly, who runs the
Undersea Research Station off the Atlantic coast. We call it the Aquadome.

"Charles! Charles!" she was saying. "This is the Aquadome calling the Penrose Research Lab! We have
an urgent problem!"

I grabbed the microphone from the bench and switched it on. Commander Bly told me that the
transparent dome over the station was being battered by a huge monster. Her divers had seen it
while exploring and had nicknamed it the Snark. This was the first time it had come near the
Aquadome, and she wasn't sure how long the plastic dome could stand it.

"I hope to see you soon, Charles," she said.

Then the picture vanished and the sound died.

"That's strange," said Tip. "Maybe you should use the Computestor."

The Computestor stands in the north part of my lab to help test my inventions. I asked it about the
videophone, and it listed six possible causes, from sunspots to a broken cable on the sea floor.
Number six was the one that made the back of my neck prickle: sabotage, circuit breaker open.

The Electrical Panel fills one wall of the corridor outside. Sure enough, the breaker for the
videophone network was open. I hadn't overloaded anything. Someone had opened it. I closed it again.

The corridor runs past the office of my lab assistant, Sharon Kemp. When I looked in, she was
hastily going through an open file drawer. She turned round with a startled look, her face flushed.
I asked her about the circuit breaker.

"Uh... I don't know anything about it," she said.

I didn't have time to find out more. The Aquadome needed help, and the SCIMITAR was the only sub
fast enough to get there. Her engine wouldn't start without the atomic catalyst capsule, though,
and the capsule wasn't in the sub. I found it lying on the work counter beside the test tank, where
anyone could have picked it up. I took it and climbed into the pilot's seat.


2. THE SHAKEDOWN

Tip settled into the back seat with a magazine. I pushed the test button, and the readout said ALL
SYSTEMS A-O-K. I didn't believe it. After the breaker and the capsule, I wanted to see for myself.

Tip handed me his Universal Tool. I opened the access panel and wriggled into the crawl space,
where small work lights glowed among the machinery. A sign said CRAWL CAREFULLY: TOO MUCH WRIGGLING
MAY POSE SERIOUS DANGERS! The voltage regulator was damaged. Running the engine hard with that
regulator would have cooked it halfway across the ocean. I fixed it with the tool and crawled
carefully out again, with both arms in one piece.

Then the launch routine: close the access panel, close the hatch, put the catalyst capsule in the
reactor, close the reactor, switch it on. Lights came up all over the panel. I filled the test tank
with seawater, started the hydrojet engine, opened the steel gate by remote control, opened the
throttle, and pushed the joystick east. The SCIMITAR glided out of the tank onto the surface of
Frobton Bay.

The surface of Frobton Bay is no place for a submarine. Speedboats come out of nowhere. I dived to
five metres and slowed right down. At once the depth finder began to glow red and an alarm bell
started ringing: the floor of the bay was very close below us. The sonarscope showed walls of rock
on either side and one narrow channel running north, so I took her north up the channel, a square
at a time, with Tip reading out the alarm lights. When the walls fell away, I dived to fifteen
metres and turned northeast toward the opening in the seawall at the mouth of the bay.

The bottom kept coming up to meet us, the warning light kept flicking between orange and red, and
the sonar showed the long dark line of the seawall sliding in from the north. Then there was a gap,
and we were through it, out of the bay and into the open ocean.

"I think it's time to turn on the autopilot," said Tip.

I did. It said the trip to the Aquadome would take sixty turns at our speed, and I left it at that.
With a new regulator I could have gone faster, but I didn't want to find out what else someone had
loosened.

"What are you reading?" I asked Tip.

"A magazine called Science World." He handed it over. The cover showed Doctor Jerome Thorpe, marine
biologist, surrounded by drawings of weird undersea creatures. HOT FLASH FROM THE MARINE BIOLOGY
FRONT! it said. NEW SEA CREATURES SPAWNED BY TEST TUBE?

The article said Thorpe might have created synthetic forms of sea life by genetic engineering,
based on a molecule called amino-hydrophase, AH for short. The creatures might respond to
ultrasonic pulses and might even be trained by them. Thorpe had gone into hiding to duck publicity.
But before that, he had told his friends he would soon marry Sharon Kemp.

I read that line twice.

We dived deeper. The water went from blue-green to dark green to a dull grey-green. Then a yellow
cone of light sprang out ahead of us, as the brass searchlight switched itself on at the depth
where the sun gives up. A dolphin swam through the beam, then a school of herring, a hammerhead
shark, and a huge manta ray flapping slowly past. A crooning noise came over the hydrophone, full
of sighs and moans and weird whistles.

"There's a blip on the sonarscope at three o'clock!" yelled Tip. "Aim the searchlight to starboard!"

It was a whale, enormous and comfortable, lolling in the deep. It swam closer to have a look at the
SCIMITAR, decided we were a strange sort of fish, and let us go by.

The Aquadome rose out of the dark at last, its lights glowing through the water all around it. The
sonarphone rang.

"This is Zoe Bly speaking. Welcome to the Aquadome, Charles! We've been anxiously awaiting your
arrival! I'd like to discuss a private matter with you, as soon as possible!"

The autopilot brought us to the docking tank on the south side. The gate slid open, I opened the
throttle, and the SCIMITAR settled into the plastic cradle. The crew pumped the tank dry and the
roof slid back.


3. NO AIR

Before I opened the hatch, I picked up the Emergency Oxygen Gear and hung it round my neck. After
the morning I'd had, it seemed like a good idea.

Commander Bly and her crew were waiting in the reception area, wearing badges that showed the air
quality in the dome. There were five of them: Amy Lowell, Bill Greenup, Marv Siegel, Doc Horvak and
Mick Antrim. I asked Bly about sabotage. "You know as much as I do," she said.

A few minutes later I noticed she was gasping. Her face was turning reddish-purple and she was
clutching her throat. Everyone was having trouble breathing, me included.

I opened the valve on my oxygen gear, sucked on the rubber straw, and ran for the dome centre,
where the Air Supply System rises like a tower almost to the top of the dome. The Universal Tool
opened the access door. Inside, there was an empty space in the machinery where something had been
unscrewed, and lying at the bottom of the housing was the missing part: an electrolyte relay. I
screwed it back in and closed the door.

Within a minute Zoe was sitting up with her colour back. So were Bill and Amy, who had collapsed.
Doc Horvak came running back from the office with Bly's oxygen gear, just too late to be needed.

"Come to my office, Charles," said Zoe.

There she told me what I already suspected. There was a traitor in the Aquadome. She had found
definite proof after our videophone call: someone had secretly attached a little black box to the
station's sonar equipment. I opened it with the Universal Tool. It was built to scramble the
sonar's ultrasonic pulses into a complicated pattern, BURPETY-BURP-B'DURP instead of a clean BURP,
which also made the blips on the screen fuzzy.

Then she had questions. Had the SCIMITAR had trouble on the way? Yes. Could I use it to hunt the
Snark, instead of waiting for it to attack again? Yes. Did I want to arm it? Yes.

We went to the workshop. Doc Horvak came too, and told me he had found a high concentration of AH
molecules in the water around the dome right after the attack. Tip remembered the magazine at once.
Doc read the article in about ten seconds flat.

"Charles, if the Snark's Thorpe's creation, then I know exactly what drug will tranquilize it," he
said. "Shall I make some up?"

While he worked, Zoe asked whether I could think of a weapon of last resort, in case Thorpe himself
was out there in a sub. I could. There was a bazooka in the storage room. She had it mounted on one
of the SCIMITAR's extensor claws, and when Doc came back with an aquatic dart gun loaded with his
special trank, I asked him to mount that on a claw too.

Later, on my way out of Zoe's office, I spotted an oddly shaped metal object under her desk: a
special Fram Bolt Wrench, the kind you would need to unscrew an electrolyte relay. I showed it to
Doc. He only said he was glad I had fixed the air.

Tip had ideas of his own. If the Snark stayed near the sea floor it would churn up silt, and we'd
never aim anything at it through that. He found a fine grid in the storage room for the sonarscope,
good to five metres, and had someone fit a fine-throttle control for close manoeuvring.


4. THE TRAP

Tip caught me by the docking tank. "Charles, did Zoe Bly mention any troublemakers?" She had. He
went through the crew one by one, and stopped at Marv Siegel, who looked after the sonar equipment.

"I have an idea how to trap Marv and find out if he's the traitor!" he said. "Put that black box
where Zoe found it, then send Marv to check the sonar equipment. If he says everything's okay, he's
gotta be the culprit."

So I put the black box back on the sonar equipment in the comm centre and asked Marv to check the
system.

While we waited, Zoe came hurrying up. She had been watching the station monitor in her office and
had seen Mick Antrim walking up the ramp out of the SCIMITAR's hatch. Then Mick himself arrived.
He'd thought I'd had trouble on the way and had wanted to check on it. Everything seemed okay now,
he said. And there was no Emergency Survival Unit under our seats. They had a standard one in the
storage room. Bill Greenup and Amy Lowell could install it in a few minutes. Should he tell them to?

I said yes.

Then Marv came running up, excited, holding out the black box. "Look what I found attached to the
Aquadome sonar equipment, Charles!" Marv was no traitor.

Bill and Amy reported back that the survival unit was in place. Bill had fitted the part under my
pilot's seat, and Amy the rest. I went to look at it.

Under my seat, a body-heat sensor had been put in where the electronic monitor should be, and a
wire ran from it to a hypodermic syringe. Tip's half was fine. "But I sure don't like the looks of
that syringe in YOUR part," he said. "Let's take it to Doc."

Doc took a few minutes to analyse it, and came back grim and pale. "Charles, the syringe contains a
lot of arsenic, and some arsenic is missing from my chemical supply shelf. If you'd been jabbed
with that, you'd be dead."

"That's exactly what would have happened," said Tip, "once you warmed up the pilot's seat enough to
trigger the sensor!"

I found Bill Greenup in the reception area and held up the syringe. He turned and ran.

Someone shouted that he was running behind the dormitory, heading for the SCIMITAR. "Come to my
office!" cried Zoe. "We can see him on the station monitor, and all the docking tank controls are
there!" On the screen, Bill's head came up over the top of the docking tank's west wall. In a
moment he would be down the inside ladder and into my sub, and he could open the gate from the
cockpit.

I turned off the docking tank electricity. With no power, the gate wouldn't answer the SCIMITAR's
signals. Bill knew he was trapped, and gave up without a fight. Zoe had him handcuffed to a pipe in
the galley. I switched the electricity back on.


5. THE SNARK

This time I closed the docking tank roof, flooded the tank, started the engine, swung the joystick
south and opened the throttle. We slid out into the ocean and headed southeast at medium speed.

The alarm rang on the sonarscope. "Holy halibut!" cried Tip. "There's a big cloud of silt ahead in
the searchlight. This could be the Snark! Want to hold course till we find out?"

We held course. However I steered, the cloud held steady in front of us. "Mind if I murmur a short,
silent prayer, Charles?" said Tip. I didn't mind at all.

Then the searchlight showed us two shapes. One was the Snark, a tentacled thing the size of a
building. Crawling along the ocean floor on its left was a vehicle Tip recognised at once: one of
our own Sea Cats.

The sonarphone crackled. "This is Doctor Jerome Thorpe, Charles! Do you read me?"

He enjoyed himself. Sharon Kemp was sitting behind him, he said, and she would enjoy what was about
to happen as much as he would. He would blast my sub with a rocket, then guide his synthetic
monster to the Aquadome to finish it off. All for a valuable ore deposit near the Aquadome. "Sharon
and I consider it a wedding present from you and your dad..."

He broke off with a gulp, and a soft voice came on.

"This is Sharon Kemp, Charles! Do you read? Thank goodness! I conked Thorpe with a wrench! He fell
onto the microphone, and he's too heavy for me to move!"

She had never been on his side. Leaving the catalyst capsule out of the SCIMITAR and opening the
circuit breaker had been part of a risky plan to wreck his scheme from inside. But I had no time to
hear it now. The Snark and the Sea Cat were coming straight at us.

I swung east, and east again. On the fine-grid sonarscope the monster's head and the Sea Cat beside
it slid past to the southwest, heading northwest towards the Aquadome. I throttled down to slow,
turned south, and then crept northwest behind them, one five-metre square at a time.

On the fourth square the sonarphone suddenly got louder. Sharon cried out in pain, and Thorpe was
back. "That'll take care of you, my little double-crosser!" Then, into the microphone: "Now then,
Charles Penrose, I'm stopping my Sea Cat so I can blow you into Kingdom Come as soon as I get you
in my sights!"

Two more squares northwest. The Sea Cat was just west of us, stopped dead, and I knew he was
swinging his rocket round. I didn't stop to look. I turned west, aimed the bazooka at the Sea Cat's
power pod, and fired, all in the same breath.

The bazooka slammed into the power pod.

"Hooray!" Tip yelled. "That crippled the Sea Cat for keeps!"

The sonarphone crackled once more. "Charles, this is Sharon! Do you read me?" Something had hit
them, she said, and Thorpe had cracked his skull on the bulkhead and was out cold. She would tie
him up. The Sea Cat's main engine was finished, but there was a backup engine for emergencies, and
the sonar control still worked. If I liked, she would guide the monster back to its cavern, where
it would stay quietly until we were ready to study it.

I liked that very much.

Tip leaned forward over my shoulder, grinning. "Mission completed, Charles."

So it was. And somewhere below us, the Snark was swimming home.
