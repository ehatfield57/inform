THE HITCHHIKER'S GUIDE TO THE GALAXY

I. The Worst Day of My Life

It was pitch black, and I felt dreadful. I had been at somebody's birthday party at the Horse and 
Groom the night before, and the room was spinning. Standing up was very difficult, but I managed 
it, and I turned on the light. Good start to the day, I thought. Pity it was going to be the worst 
one of my life.

My bedroom was a mess: a faded carpet, old wallpaper, a washbasin, and a chair with my tatty 
dressing gown slung over it. I put the gown on. In the pocket were a thing my aunt gave me which I 
didn't know what it was, a buffered analgesic, and some pocket fluff. I swallowed the tablet, and 
after a few seconds the room calmed down and behaved itself. I picked up a flathead screwdriver, 
and then my toothbrush, and as I did so a tree outside the window collapsed. There was, I assumed, 
no causal relationship between these two events.

On the front porch was a pile of junk mail. I gathered it up and went out into the front garden, 
and there, between my house and a huge yellow bulldozer, was a man called Prosser from the local 
council, wearing a digital watch and yelling at me to get out of the way. I didn't. I lay down in 
the mud in front of the bulldozer instead.

While I lay there I read the post. Most of it was from some computer company called Infocom that 
wanted me to buy their games. Underneath was an official letter from the council, dated two years 
ago and not delivered till now, explaining that a demolition order had been served on my home. The 
date of demolition was today.

The bulldozer came on until the ground shook beneath me, and then with a terrible grinding of gears 
it stopped a few inches away and emitted noxious substances all over my rose bed. And my friend 
Ford Prefect turned up. He hardly seemed to notice my predicament. He kept glancing nervously at 
the sky, and he took a towel from his battered leather satchel and offered it to me.

I asked him what about my home. He looked startled, then guilty, and started to say several things 
and stopped, and then he noticed the bulldozer for the first time and announced that he had 
something of Earth-shattering importance to tell me, and that it required a quick drink at the pub. 
When I pointed out that the man over there wanted to knock my house down, Ford went for a quiet 
word with Prosser, and a few minutes later Prosser came over, looking reluctant, and lay down in 
the mud in my place.

II. The Horse and Groom

At the pub I bought a cheese sandwich. The bread was like the stuff stereos come packed in, the 
cheese would have been good for rubbing out spelling mistakes, and the margarine and pickle had 
reacted to produce something turquoise. Ford bought lots of beer and gave me half of it. "Muscle 
relaxant," he said.

It was very good beer, and after the first pint, when Ford mentioned that he came from a small 
planet in the vicinity of Betelgeuse and not from Guildford after all, I took it in my stride and 
asked which part. After the second he mentioned that the world was going to end in about twelve 
minutes. After the third there was a distant crash, which Ford explained was nothing to worry 
about, probably just my house being knocked down.

I ran back up the lane. A small dog ran up to me, yapping, and I gave it my cheese sandwich. It was 
deeply moved; this was a dog that had met its main sandwich, and it ate with passion and ignored a 
passing microscopic space fleet. Where my home had been there was a pile of rubble and Mr. Prosser 
looking sheepishly triumphant.

Then a fleet of huge yellow Vogon Constructor ships came over, with a noise like a cross between 
Led Zeppelin's farewell concert and the eruption of Krakatoa, and announced that the Earth would be 
demolished to make way for a hyperspace bypass in two of our Earth minutes. In the turmoil I 
dropped the thing my aunt gave me, and it rolled away. It was the least of my worries. Ford, 
shouting at me through the gale, took a small black device out of his satchel and dropped it at my 
feet. I picked it up. Lights were flickering on it. I pushed the green button.

Every atom of my being was scrambled.

III. The Vogon Ship

For a long time there was nothing. Then there was a smell, something pungent waved under my nose, 
and a shadow in the dark that was vaguely Ford Prefect-shaped. He was waving a bottle of 
Santraginean Mineral Water at me. We were in the hold of a Vogon ship: a squalid room of grubby 
mattresses, unwashed cups and unidentifiable alien underwear, with a dispensing machine on one wall 
and, in the corner, a glass case with a switch and a keyboard and something inside called an atomic 
vector plotter. Ford gave me some peanuts, to replace the protein I'd lost in the matter 
transference beam.

Then he went to sleep, and left me his copy of The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy. "I'm not 
supposed to tell you this," he whispered, "but you'll never be able to finish the game without 
consulting the Guide about lots of stuff."

The dispensing machine produced babel fish, which you put in your ear to understand any language, 
and it was clearly designed by someone who hated people. Getting one into my ear took everything I 
had. I hung my gown on the hook by the machine, covering a tiny hole. I put Ford's towel over the 
drain. I laid his satchel in front of the little panel the cleaning robots came out of, and put my 
junk mail on top of it. Then I pushed the button. The fish shot out, hit the gown, slid down the 
sleeve onto the towel, was grabbed by a cleaning robot, which crashed into the satchel and sent it 
flying in a cloud of junk mail, which another robot began madly collecting, and the fish completed 
a graceful arc and landed with a loud squish in my ear.

At once I understood the Captain on the intercom, who was saying that he hated freeloaders and was 
going to throw us into space, but might read us some of his poetry first.

I pushed the switch on the glass case. A recording told me to type in the second word from the 
second verse of the Captain's current favourite poem, and warned me that a wrong answer would make 
the case explode.

The guards came and strapped Ford and me into poetry appreciation chairs in the Captain's cabin. 
"Oh freddled gruntbuggly," he began. Somewhere in the second line, with an effort for which 
Hercules himself would have patted me on the back, I gritted my teeth and enjoyed it. The Captain 
was so pleased he read the second verse: "Gashee morphousite, thou expungiest quoopisk!" And then, 
since we had survived two verses, he had us thrown out of the airlock.

On the way back through the hold I typed "morphousite" and the glass case opened, and I took the 
plotter. Ford spent the rest of the time trying to talk the guard into a career change, without 
success. Twenty-nine seconds after the airlock blew us into space, we were picked up by a passing 
ship. The odds against this were so enormous that I passed out.

IV. The Heart of Gold

In the dark I heard the hum of a star drive far above me and a voice told me there was an exit to 
port. It was lying. I went aft instead, and came out in Entry Bay Number Two of the Heart of Gold, 
the spaceship with the new Infinite Improbability Drive. The shipboard computer, Eddie, announced 
that we had been picked up at an improbability of 2 to the 21,914th power to 1 against.

On the bridge were a man with more than the usual number of heads, called Zaphod, who had stolen 
the ship, and a dark-haired woman with a handbag who turned out to be Tricia McMillan, Trillian, 
whom I had been trying to pick up at a party in Islington a few weeks ago, and who had left it with 
Zaphod. They all went off to the sauna and left me with Eddie.

The way into the drive chamber was guarded by a computer that kept telling me it was terribly 
dangerous. I kept going aft until it gave up. The room had nothing to see in it, it said, and then 
on a closer look it had a spare, portable Improbability Generator in the corner, an ionic diffusion 
rasp and a pair of hypersonic pliers. I dropped most of what I was carrying and took the generator 
up to the bridge. The galley had a Nutrimat, which examined my taste buds and neural pathways and 
produced a cup of Advanced Tea Substitute, a liquid almost but not entirely unlike tea. I put the 
plotter's long, dangly bit into it, plugged the generator's small plug into the plotter, and pushed 
the switch.

V. Five Improbabilities

What followed I can only describe in pieces, because each began in the same dark place where I 
could sense nothing, until one sense came back.

Once it was a smell, the tail of the Ravenous Bugblatter Beast of Traal, which demanded my name so 
that it could add it to its memorial of the people it had eaten. I told it, and ran out into its 
courtyard, picked up a sharp stone, and wrapped my towel round my head. The Beast is so dim that it 
thinks if you can't see it, it can't see you. With the towel over my eyes I carved ARTHUR DENT on 
the memorial. When the Beast found my name freshly carved there, it decided it must already have 
eaten me in a fit of absent-mindedness and settled down for a nap. In the inner lair a dead 
Beasthunter was clutching a Nutrimat/Computer Interface, and I took it. Then a team of Beasthunters 
mistook me for the Beast and carted me off to a zoo, and after a long misadventure involving 
bureaucratic pirates and a talent for asteroid painting I found myself back on the ship with a 
number twelve asteroid paint chipper in my pocket.

Once it was touch: cold liquid at my fingertips, which turned out to be a glass of white wine in my 
hand at a party in Islington. I was Trillian. A shy fellow from the West Country named Arthur kept 
trying to talk to me, and had an enormous unsightly ball of fluff on his jacket. I took it off him, 
improving his appearance greatly, and put it in my handbag. He was only slightly more interesting 
to talk to than an averagely interesting wall, and when a flamboyant man called Phil offered me a 
ride on his ion scooter, I went.

Once it was sight: a painful light that became the yellow Sun of Earth, and I was Ford Prefect, 
hurrying up the lane. I tried to give Arthur his towel back. I noticed the bulldozer. I persuaded 
Prosser to lie down in Arthur's place, and took Arthur to the pub, and bought six pints and some 
peanuts. Another time I was Ford, after the crash, I gave Arthur my satchel fluff, and he hiccupped 
and stuck it in his pocket.

Once it was the orange sun of Damogran, and both my heads had a hangover. I was Zaphod Beeblebrox, 
President of the Galaxy, piloting a speedboat to the dedication of the Heart of Gold. Under the 
seat cushion I found a small key and a piece of fluff, and there was a tool box beside me. I 
steered straight at the rocky spire, the autopilot swerved us up the cliff on a plume of water, and 
the crowd cheered wildly. Then Trillian grabbed me by the necks and pointed a blaster at my left 
head, and the guards raised their rifles. "Don't shoot," I said, and they threw their rifles in a 
pile, and Trillian shot the pile, and the two of us strolled into the Heart of Gold and stole it.

And once, after I had said something stupid out loud, it was hearing: a hum far below, and I came 
out in the War Chamber of a star battle cruiser. I later learned what had happened. Earlier, on the 
ship, I had said "foozle the blue frob," a careless remark that crossed a wormhole to a distant 
galaxy and arrived at the exact moment the leader of the Vl'Hurgs was challenging the G'Gugvunt 
leader to take back what it had said about his mother. In the Vl'Hurg tongue it was the most 
dreadful insult imaginable, and the result was centuries of war. Now their fleet was hurtling 
sunwards. I picked up an ultra-plasmic vacuum awl and waited. The fleet plunged toward Earth and 
spotted a dog, which looked to them like a gigantic monster, cheerfully tucking into my cheese 
sandwich. They were so moved by this simple picture of happiness that they went home to rebuild in 
a new spirit of harmony and cooperation. They dropped me off, rather oddly, in a gray maze of 
twisty little synapses, all alike, which I eventually realized was my own brain. Blocking one gap 
was a large black particle marked "Sense, Common for: Dent, Arthur." I took it out. Electrical 
impulses leaped across the gap, and unfortunately I was in it at the time.

Between these I kept being bounced back into places I had already been: the Beast clawing me, the 
Vogon guards stunning me, the party hostess boring me literally to death. And each time I came back 
to the dark and listened for the drive, and went aft, not to port.

VI. Tea

When I had all my pieces, I took the interface card to the Nutrimat, opened its panel, took out the 
circuit board, and put the card in. I asked it for tea, real tea. The Nutrimat was puzzled that 
anyone would want something made by pouring boiling water on dead leaves, and said it would need 
help from Eddie, and began to whirr. And because every circuit on the ship was busy making my tea, 
Eddie couldn't take evasive action when the legendary lost planet of Magrathea launched nuclear 
missiles at us.

I had plugged the spare drive's large plug into the console, and I flipped its switch. Sparks flew. 
"My new control console!" wailed Eddie. The missiles turned into a sperm whale, which plummeted 
toward the planet. Ford, Zaphod and Trillian sauntered past on their way back to the sauna, and 
Zaphod slapped me on the back.

The tea was in the Nutrimat's slot. I took it to the bridge, moved the plotter's dangly bit from 
the substitute into the real tea, put everything down except the thing my aunt gave me, which had 
come back to me as it always did, and flipped the switch again. Real tea, it seems, gives far 
better Brownian motion than the substitute, and this time I could choose where I went in the dark. 
I waited, and touched, and found warm liquid, and found myself, to my disgust, licking the lining 
of a whale's stomach as it fell through the air. There was a flowerpot there. I put it inside the 
thing my aunt gave me, which seemed the safest place for it. Then the whale hit the ground.

VII. The Tree of Foreknowledge

Back on the ship I gathered my fluff: my own pocket fluff, the jacket fluff in Trillian's handbag, 
the seat cushion fluff by the hatch where Zaphod's tool box lay, and the satchel fluff, which had 
found its way into my dressing gown pocket. I planted all four in the flowerpot. A sprout appeared. 
I carried it into the sauna, and after several hours I came out a changed man with a changed plant, 
and I ate the fruit.

My vision wavered. I saw myself standing next to Marvin, the ship's Paranoid Android, who was 
asking me for a molecular hyperwave pincer. The plant was the Tree of Foreknowledge.

The ship had landed on Magrathea, and Eddie had jammed the hatch while he spent 14.9 years checking 
for dangers. I picked up the pincer from the bridge and took my real tea. And then, because I had 
learned some things about this universe, I also took no tea. Down by Marvin's pantry the screening 
door had been refusing to open for anyone who couldn't show it a clear sign of intelligence. It was 
almost speechless with admiration. "Wow. Simultaneous tea and no tea. You are clearly a heavy-duty 
philosopher." It opened respectfully.

I drank the tea. It was the finest tea I had ever tasted. It almost made the whole misadventure 
seem worthwhile. Then I went in, and the tidal waves of depression that roll off Marvin would have 
killed me, but my happiness and that excellent cup of tea saw me through.

"Marvin," I said, "fix the hatch."

"Humans are so depressingly demanding," said Marvin. He told me to meet him in the hatchway access 
space in twelve turns, and to bring the proper tool, if I could count up to twelve.

I put down everything but the pincer, even my dressing gown, and waited in the little access space. 
Marvin shambled in and asked for a molecular hyperwave pincer. I gave it to him. He fiddled inside 
the mechanism for three-tenths of a second and the hatch slid open. "I don't expect you to be 
grateful," he said, "or even interested," and limped away complaining about the diodes down his 
left side.

I stepped out onto the ramp. The wind moaned and dust drifted across the surface of the alien 
world. Zaphod, Ford and Trillian appeared and urged me forward. Slowly, nervously, the cold thin 
air rasping in my lungs, I set one foot on the ancient dust of Magrathea.

What happened next is another story. But I can tell you one thing: there was, it turns out, a 
causal relationship between my taking the toothbrush and that tree collapsing at the very 
beginning. Someone has since apologized for the slight inaccuracy.
