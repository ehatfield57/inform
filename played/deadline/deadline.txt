DEADLINE

I. Eight O'Clock

I was at the gate of the Robner estate at eight o'clock in the morning, with twelve hours to close 
a case the department had already half closed for me. Marshall Robner, head of Robner Corporation, 
had been found dead in his locked library. Everyone said suicide. My deadline was eight that 
evening; after that, Mrs. Robner wanted the police out of her house.

From the south lawn the house sat at the end of a pebbled path, with lawns sweeping away to the 
northeast and northwest and the lake beyond. I walked up to the front door and opened it.

Mrs. Robner met me in the hall. "Hello," she said, "I am Mrs. Robner. Please come in. I'm afraid I 
really can't be of much help to you. This is surely all a terrible waste of time, and not a little 
upsetting, having all these police marching around your house." She had told Mr. Coates and the 
other detective the same: I could look around, but I had to be out by eight o'clock at the latest. 
Oh, and Mr. Coates would be reading her husband's will at noon in the living room. I could attend 
if I wished. Then she went off to make herself some breakfast.

The foyer had a crystal chandelier and a marble floor. I went north along the hall and up the 
stairs, which make quite a noise when you step on them; I noted that. At the top a corridor ran the 
length of the house to the west, past closed bedroom doors and a linen closet, to a window that 
couldn't be opened. At the end of it was the library.

II. The Library

The library door was solid oak, and it had been knocked off its hinges to get in. It lay just 
inside the doorway. Robner had spent a lot of time in this room. There was a big executive desk in 
front of the balcony window, a telephone, three units of bookshelves along the east wall, and 
wall-to-wall carpet. Beside the desk stood a collapsible tray with a bowl of white powder on it. A 
wicker wastepaper basket held some crumpled papers. A beautiful saucer lay overturned on the floor, 
and a matching teacup on its side. On the desk were a pad of white note paper and a desk calendar 
open to July 7, and a pencil had rolled onto the floor. There was a bottle of Ebullion too.

The carpet was expensive and clean, except for a few small patches of dried mud near the desk.

I took the cup, the pad, the calendar and the pencil. The top sheet of the pad was blank, but 
whoever wrote on the sheet before it had pressed hard, and when I shaded it with the pencil some of 
the words came up:

       mnidy               Oth

late!

A letter to Baxter, then, about a merger, and someone called Omnidyne, and something called Focus, 
and documents. I turned the calendar to the next day, July 8. There was one note, in the nine 
o'clock column: "Call Coates: Will completed."

So Robner had finished a new will the day he died, and meant to tell his lawyer the next morning.

I opened the balcony door and went out. The balcony was bare, high over the rose garden, with a 
view of the north lawn and the lake, and a metal railing around it to save people from a ten-foot 
drop into the roses. Off to the north the gardener, Mr. McNabb, was pulling weeds. The railing was 
unremarkable except in one place, where a little paint had been scraped off the outside.

Mud on the carpet. Scrapes on the outside of the railing. Somebody had come up from the garden.

III. The Ladder and the LoBlo

I went back down the stairs and out the front door, around the east side of the house past a carved 
cornerstone and a locked window, to a wide east lawn running down to the lake. Beside the water was 
a small garden shed with a dirty window. Inside, among the shelves of tools, a wooden ladder leaned 
against the wall: about fifteen feet long, an ordinary gardening ladder, caked with dried dirt and 
mud.

McNabb was somewhere off to the northwest. I let him be for the moment and went back into the 
house. Mrs. Robner was ducking into a room off the hall.

Upstairs, at the south end of the hall, a door stood open on Ms. Dunbar's bathroom. Ms. Dunbar had 
worked for Robner for years. In her medicine cabinet were a bottle of aspirin, a bottle of cough 
medicine, and a prescription bottle from the Frobizz Pharmacy: "Ms. S. Dunbar. LoBLO. Take 1 tablet 
3 times daily." LoBlo, a brand of methsparin, ten milligram tablets, from Fizmo Labs in Kingston, 
Ontario. And a warning on the label: LoBlo may be dangerous when used in combination with other 
medications.

IV. The Morning Mail

I went out to the front path and settled down to wait for the morning to pass.

A mailman came briskly up the path, handed me an envelope, and left. A car pulled up the drive and 
a man stepped out on the lawn: Mr. Baxter. He walked past me into the house without a word. McNabb 
came into view on the east lawn and disappeared again. Around eleven the paperboy threw the 
afternoon paper at the house from a hundred feet away, and it landed, astonishingly, right by the 
front door instead of in the bushes.

The envelope was handwritten, addressed to Mrs. Marshall Robner, 506 Lake View Road, Maitland, and 
postmarked Canaan, Connecticut, July 6, eight in the morning. I opened it.

"Dear Leslie," it began. The writer was sorry to hear Marshall was despondent again. He talked of 
suicide when he was in that state, but he was full of stupid talk like that, and the thought of the 
business going to Baxter after he was gone would keep him alive. "George has finally gone too far, 
eh? After all those empty threats, Marshall actually followed through. It serves the little leech 
right too, if you ask me. This means that should the unthinkable happen, you will be provided for 
as you deserve. I'll see you Friday as usual. Love, Steven."

So Mrs. Robner had a friend named Steven, and George, the son, had been cut out of something. I put 
the letter in my pocket.

V. McNabb's Holes

At half past eleven I went around the house to find the gardener. He was on the garden path, at the 
edge of a big rose garden, rows of yellow, red, pink and white, the envy of the neighbors. McNabb 
was pruning stems and talking angrily to himself.

"Hey, McNabb," I said. "What's wrong?"

He told me. That morning he'd found two holes like deep footprints in his garden, with roses 
crushed beside them, and he didn't know when he'd ever get it put right.

"Show me the holes."

"Follow me!" He walked off into the roses, and I followed, trampling seedlings. He took me by the 
arm to a spot deep in the garden, close against the house, that I would never have found alone, 
pointed at the ground, and walked away.

There were two holes, each about two inches by four, three inches deep, with the soil packed hard 
around them. The feet of a ladder. A ladder with someone heavy on it.

I got down and went through the soil. I made a mess of the roses, but I turned up some tiny pieces 
of a hard, shiny stuff that slipped through my fingers. I dug again and found more. The third time, 
I cut my finger on a sharp edge, and then I dug carefully and pulled up a piece of porcelain, 
covered with dirt and dried mud.

I asked for it to be analyzed for LoBlo, and Sergeant Duffy walked up as quietly as a mouse, took 
it from me, said "I'll return soon with the results," and left as silently as he'd come.

VI. The Will

It was nearly noon. I went back around by the west lawn, past the bay window, and in at the front 
door to the living room. It's a big, formal room, with a fieldstone fireplace, portraits of Mrs. 
Robner's colonial ancestors, mahogany and walnut, and chairs enough for fifteen. Mrs. Robner sat 
knitting. Baxter sat reading a book.

Footsteps on the stairs, and Mr. Coates, the lawyer, came in and shook my hand. "I'm glad you're 
here, Inspector. We'll begin the reading soon." He asked us all to be patient, because not everyone 
was there yet.

Then Sergeant Duffy appeared, as he does, out of nowhere, holding the fragment. "The fragment did 
contain LoBlo. Here is the full report." He handed me the slip of paper and left.

George, the son, came in, and then Ms. Dunbar. "Ah!" said Coates. "Everybody's here now."

He explained that Mr. Robner had told him two days ago that he meant to make a new will, and had 
promised to call when it was done. Since no call had come, he had to assume Robner had changed his 
mind, so the will in his hand, made three years ago, was the last one. If a newer will turned up 
within a reasonable time, it would replace this one. Out of the corner of my eye I saw George 
nodding and smiling broadly.

The old will left the estate in equal parts to George Arthur Robner and to Mrs. Leslie Phillips 
Robner. There was some discussion, understated congratulations and overstated sympathy, until 
Coates cleared his throat, said he had to go, phoned his office for a car, and left.

VII. George

I took out the desk calendar and showed George the note for July 8: "Call Coates: Will completed."

"I ... uh ... I don't really know what to say," he said. "I guess that maybe Dad ... but there is 
no other ... I can't help you ... sorry." He was quite agitated. "I've got to be going now. I'll 
see you later." And he left.

I followed him. Through the hall, to the stairs, up the stairs, west along the corridor. "I wish 
you would stop following me around like this. Am I to have no privacy? I'm simply trying to take 
care of something personal." "Please stop following me. My business is private." He went into his 
bedroom at the west end, a room with a little liquor cabinet and a stereo playing a cacophonous 
electronic jumble, and lay on his bed listening to it. "I have business to attend to. Would you 
mind leaving?" He paced the room, waiting for me to go.

I went. I heard his door close behind me. I walked into the library, out onto the balcony, and 
stood where I couldn't be seen from the room.

It didn't take ten minutes. George looked down the hallway, then darted into the library and went 
straight to the bookshelves. He turned around quickly, but I got out of sight before he saw me. 
When I looked again his back was to me, and his right arm seemed to disappear into a shelf, and the 
left-hand unit of bookshelves swung away from the wall to show a dark room behind it. George went 
in, shaking with fear and excitement. A dim light came on in there, and I could see him by a dark 
wall, doing something with his right hand. Then the bookshelves swung shut.

I gave him four minutes to get his hands well into the cookie jar.

I put down the things I no longer needed in the library: the pencil, the pad, the cup, the envelope 
and its letter. Then I looked at the bookshelves. They held a great many books and manuscripts, 
carefully arranged, but one book was out of place, and in the gap behind it was a small black 
button. I pressed it.

The shelves swung quickly and quietly aside. George spun round to face me. His face, wild with 
happiness a moment before, turned to panic and horror. He tried feebly to hide the paper in his 
hands, jumped at me, shrank back, and finally crumpled to the floor sobbing, clutching the paper 
underneath him.

It was a hidden closet between the library and the master bedroom, bare and dusty and cobwebbed, 
with a blue button on one wall and a red one on the other. A big combination safe was set into the 
south wall, and it stood open.

The paper George was holding was Marshall Robner's new will. It disowned George and left the whole 
estate to his wife. In the safe was a stack of papers bound together. I took those too, and leafed 
through them. They were documents incriminating Mr. Baxter in the Focus scandal: funds Baxter had 
embezzled, and how the business had been hushed up. They would be enough to convict him in the 
Focus case.

So that was Robner's letter. Stop the merger, or I'll make these public.

VIII. The Living Room

I left George on the closet floor and went back down to the living room. The housekeeper, Mrs. 
Rourke, was dusting. Ms. Dunbar sat on the sofa. Baxter was still reading his book.

"Baxter," I said. "What about Focus?"

There was a flicker of surprise on his face. "I'm not sure what you mean. Focus Corporation has 
been a subsidiary of Robner Corp. for some years. I fail to see its import."

I handed him the papers. He read slowly, leafing through the pages. "I'm afraid I have not been 
altogether candid with you. There was some trouble a few years ago with Focus Corp. because of 
some, let us say, irresponsible dealings on my part. Marshall agreed to cover up my involvement to 
save the company from bad publicity."

Then I showed Ms. Dunbar the lab report. She seemed stunned, and recovered quickly. "He didn't 
commit suicide, then?" she said. "But LoBlo, that's a pill that I take for my blood pressure." She 
paused. "I can tell what you're thinking, but I didn't, couldn't have done it. Why should I? 
Someone must have taken them, maybe George. He knew I used them."

I showed the report to Baxter. "This is quite peculiar. It appears that Marshall was murdered, 
then. I wonder.... I would have a word with George if I were you, Inspector."

I turned to Dunbar and accused her of murder.

"No! I didn't do it! I've worked for Mr. Robner for years. What possible motive could I have?" She 
began to cry, and to move about the room nervously, and she glanced at Baxter and then at me.

IX. The Ticket

I stepped out into the hall and waited for her. It was one o'clock. She walked past me and out 
through the foyer, and I followed her to the front path, where the Daily Herald still leaned 
against the door.

She looked back, saw me, and stopped. She took a cigarette from her pocket, and as she did, 
something like a ticket stub fell out and floated to the ground at her feet. She felt in her pocket 
again for a match, found none, and put the cigarette away.

I picked up the stub. Hartford Philharmonic Orchestra, July 7, 1981, 8 PM, Row R, Seat 7. The night 
Robner died.

I showed it to her.

"Oh, I ... well, I guess I should tell you. You see, Mr. Baxter and I, we go together to concerts, 
only occasionally, you understand. We went that night, the night Marshall died. And then he took me 
home and that's it. I should have said something before, I know. I just didn't think it was 
important, and, well, I didn't think that the others should know that we were seeing each other 
socially. Our ... nobody knows about it, you know. Please don't say anything!"

She stood staring off to the south, deep in thought.

X. The Shed

I walked around the house to the shed by the lake and waited among the tools. A few minutes later 
Ms. Dunbar came in and began looking around for something. She kept on looking, pacing the shed 
nervously, for something she should have found by then.

At twenty past one, Mr. Baxter came in.

They hadn't expected company. Dunbar was aghast, and kept looking back and forth between Baxter and 
me.

I showed Baxter the ticket stub.

"Ah, that must be Ms. Dunbar's ticket stub," he said. "I should have told you earlier. Ms. Dunbar 
was with me at the concert on the night that Marshall killed himself. She became ill at 
intermission time and hired a car to take her back home. You see, Inspector, I know how much Ms. 
Dunbar appreciates classical music, and I occasionally ask her along with me to my subscription 
series. I really should have told the other detective, but I didn't think it mattered."

She had told me he drove her home after the concert. He told me she went home sick at the 
intermission, alone. Two people who had been to the same concert together couldn't agree on how 
they had left it.

I arrested them both.

XI. September

Months later a letter came from Police Commissioner Klutz, dated September 5.

"Dear Inspector, Congratulations on your superb handling of the Robner case. As you are no doubt 
aware, a jury convicted Mr. Baxter and Ms. Dunbar today of the murder of Mr. Robner. Thanks to you, 
the murderers will be behind bars, possibly for the rest of their lives. Again, thanks for a job 
brilliantly done. Which reminds me of another fascinating case which I would like to assign to you 
..."

By then the whole story was known. Robner had lived for his company, and Baxter meant to sell it to 
Omnidyne, the multinational conglomerate, to advance his own career. Robner wrote him that last 
desperate letter, threatening to expose the Focus business, and the papers that proved it were in 
the safe that only Robner and George knew how to open. Baxter decided Robner had to die, and he 
could count on everyone believing it was suicide, because Robner was known to be depressed. He 
brought in his lover, Ms. Dunbar, whose blood-pressure pills reacted fatally with the medicine 
Robner took. After the concert in Hartford they came back to the estate. Dunbar put LoBlo in 
Robner's tea, and some time later Robner died behind his locked door.

Then Baxter took the ladder from the shed, set it in the roses under the balcony, climbed up past 
the railing, and swapped the poisoned cup for a clean one. Anyone who had counted the cups and 
saucers in the kitchen would have found one missing. On the way down he dropped it, and he left one 
piece behind in the soft earth of the rose garden, near the two holes McNabb found that morning 
while tending his roses.
