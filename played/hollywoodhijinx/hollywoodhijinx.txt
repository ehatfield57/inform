HOLLYWOOD HIJINX

Ten Treasures by Morning


I. THE WILL

It had all happened so fast. Aunt Hildegarde passed away without any warning, the funeral was that 
afternoon, and by nightfall I was sitting in the back of a black limousine as it turned off the 
highway, with a lawyer beside me and a very strange clause of her will on my mind.

The limo pulled up in front of the house. "This is the end of the line," said the attorney, and I 
stepped out. "Remember, your Aunt Hildegarde's will stated you will inherit her entire fortune -- 
if you can find the ten 'treasures' in one night."

He handed me a photo of Uncle Buddy and a letter. The will had told him to give me the photo, with 
the poem on it, to point me in the right direction. "And here," he said, "you'll need this." It was 
a flashlight. "Meet me at 9 a.m. in the living room with all the 'treasures' and you'll inherit her 
entire estate." Then the limo pulled away and was gone into the dark.

I stood at the South Junction and looked at the house where I'd spent so many summers as a kid. It 
wasn't as big as it had seemed back then, but it was still big. Stone paths wound east and west 
around it, and a wide walkway led north to the porch.

Beside the walk stood a life-size statue of Buck Palace, the fighting letter carrier, one of the 
stars of Uncle Buddy's talent stables. He was holding a bazooka, pointed north at the house. The 
poem on the photo told me what to do with him. I turned Buck to the west, then to the east, then 
back to the north, and from somewhere ahead I heard a click.

As I walked up toward the house a large black cat scurried across the path, heading for Johnny 
Carson's house. I tried not to take it personally.

On the front porch there was a regulation mailbox endorsed by Buck Palace, and under it a doorbell 
once rung by Sonny Tufts. I opened the oak door. It gave a long squeal, and somewhere several rooms 
away I heard hurried footsteps. That should have worried me more than it did. The house was 
supposed to be empty.

Inside it was pitch black. I switched on the flashlight.


II. THE ATOMIC CHIHUAHUA

The foyer was all rich mahogany paneling, with a broad staircase sweeping up and archways leading 
east, west and north. By the front door was a coat closet. I went north first, into the game room, 
because it had always been my favorite room. Every summer Uncle Buddy had props and models from his 
movies brought here to amuse his guests.

It was still here: the scale model of downtown Tokyo from "Atomic Chihuahuas From Hell." Tokyo 
Central Park sat in the middle, with a monument in its eastern half, and perched on top of the 
monument was a Big Diamond Ring. West of the park, out on the main street, stood the Atomic 
Chihuahua itself. A thick plastic dome covered the whole city. Outside the dome were five buttons: 
blue, black, green, white and red.

I pressed green. The Atomic Chihuahua moved east in its best prehistoric prance. Right away a pair 
of tiny tanks turned a corner and came straight for it, and a puny plane banked over the park 
toward the street. I kept pressing green. The tanks opened fire, a second plane joined the first, 
and the dog's heavy reptilian tail pounded the pavement. As the bullets pierced its scales it 
paused for a moment, remembering its younger days with Xavier Cugat.

Somewhere in the house I heard floorboards creak.

I pressed black. One of the tanks had driven up onto the dog's toenail, and when it raised its hind 
leg the tank went flying into an apartment building. Hundreds of house plants fell to their deaths. 
I pressed black again and the dog stomped the other tank flat. Then white: it swiped at the planes, 
and one trailed black smoke and crashed in a suburb. White again, and the second went down in the 
parking lot of Tokyo Disneyland.

Green moved the wounded dog east again, into the park, violating all leash laws. A tiny truck 
rolled out from under some trees with a rocket mounted on it. Its radar dish spun, locked on, and 
the rocket blasted off. I moved the dog up beside the monument, right next to the truck, and 
pressed black. The Chihuahua stomped the truck to bits. The rocket swerved, missed, and slammed 
into the Ginsu Building, headquarters of the Ginsu Knife Company, which collapsed completely. Tokyo 
wasn't saved, but millions of late-night TV viewers were.

Blue made the dog clutch the Big Diamond Ring in its claws. Green walked it forward, crushing the 
monument (dozens of local pigeons went into mourning), and on east down the street until it bumped 
its atomic snout against the inside of the dome.

Then I pressed red. The Atomic Chihuahua breathed fire and a spot on the dome began to melt. Twice 
more, and there was a small hole. I reached in and took the ring.

One treasure.


III. THE SCREENING ROOM

East of the game room, past a short hall between the ladies' room and the men's room, was the 
screening room where Uncle Buddy had died. He'd had a heart attack while watching the final cut of 
his first horror musical, "A Corpse Line." The film had never been released, and nobody knew what 
had become of the only full-length print. Some said he'd called it his masterwork of horror. Others 
said it was so bad that if he hadn't died, someone would have killed him.

A yellow punch card lay among the plush seats. I took it.

In the cramped projection booth to the south were a slide projector, a film projector, a slide and 
a short strip of film. I put the slide into the slide projector, turned it on and focused the lens. 
Then I took the lens cap off the film projector (a big black Simplex 70), threaded the strip of 
film and switched it on. Light poured onto the screen.

PLAY "Feelings."
Love,
Aunt Hildegarde.

The strip of film ran out onto the floor. From somewhere I heard the eerie whistling of wind coming 
down the chimney.


IV. THE PARLOR

The parlor, east of the foyer, was where Uncle Buddy used to bring potential investors. He'd pour 
his finest scotch, hand round his Cuban cigars, and once the "guests" were tight he'd pitch his 
next movie. Having seen his movies, I knew anyone who invested had to have been drinking.

Everything in the room was bolted to the floor except the baby grand piano in the middle. Aunt 
Hildegarde had bought it for Herman and me. Neither of us had practiced as we should have. When I 
lifted the lid there was a violet punch card inside, and I took that too.

I sat down and, a little rusty, played "Feelings." Just as I began to seriously consider lessons, a 
door in the floor opened.

I pushed the piano north against the wall (the hearty breakfast I'd eaten that morning paid off) 
and climbed down into an uncomfortably warm crawl space. A beam ran across the ceiling, splitting 
it in two, and each half had a pillar holding up the ceiling in a niche: a dusty one in the north 
half and a dirty one in the south. In the north wall, about a foot off the floor, there was the 
entrance to a passage, mostly blocked by the floor of the room above.

I took the dirty pillar out of its niche and climbed back up. Then I pushed the piano south, into 
the middle of the room and a couple of feet more, and the whole parlor tipped. The south side went 
down, the piano rolled and slammed against the south wall, and the north side of the floor rose. 
Down in the crawl space, the passage was open now. It ran a little way and stopped, unfinished, and 
in it stood a parking meter.

Two treasures. I left the dirty pillar in the parlor and carried the meter and the ring to the 
living room, where I meant to keep everything for the lawyer. I dropped the yellow and violet cards 
in the foyer, and left the letter and photo in the game room. As I came back into the foyer I heard 
something heavy thud onto a floor somewhere in the house.


V. UP THE CHIMNEY

The living room was where Uncle Buddy and Aunt Hildegarde used to spend their evenings: a long 
sofa, cushy chairs, a telephone, a monstrous Persian rug, and a grand stone fireplace across most 
of the west wall. On the mantle stood three wax statuettes of a Hindu god, red, white and blue, 
each with a wick in its head.

The fireplace mortar was crumbling, and one brick about waist high was loose. When I pulled it out 
an indigo punch card came with it and spiraled to the floor. The hole made a good foothold, and so 
did the holes above it where other bricks were missing, so I climbed. Up and up the chimney, until 
I came out on the west end of the roof. On a stormy night, I remembered, you could watch Linda 
Ronstadt's house wash into the ocean from up here.

There was a second chimney at the east end. I edged along the roof and climbed down into it. It 
didn't go far, only a few feet, and at the bottom sat a stuffed penguin. I climbed back up, crossed 
the roof, and went down the first chimney to the fireplace, where I picked up the indigo card.

Three treasures.

I looked more closely at the statuettes. The red god held up its right hand showing three fingers. 
The white god held up its left hand showing seven. The blue god held up its right hand showing 
five. Red, white and blue. Right, left, right.

In the long hallway east of the foyer hung a portrait of Aunt Hildegarde and Uncle Buddy. I swung 
it away from the wall. Behind it was a safe, and a green punch card fluttered to the floor. The 
dial was set to 6. I turned it right to 3, left to 7 and right to 5, heard a faint click, and 
opened it. Inside was Mama Maggio's cheese grater.

Four.


VI. THE CLOSET THAT GOES UP

The coat closet by the front door was small, with three coat pegs set at an angle on the back wall. 
Left of the first peg was an empty hole about the size of a peg, and right of the third was a peg 
that had been sawed off flush with the wall. A rusty bucket hung from the second peg, and a pair of 
skis leaned in the corner.

I pulled the third peg. The closet shook and rattled, the door swung shut, and my stomach dropped 
to my knees as the whole thing rose and stopped. When I opened the door I was in the middle of the 
upstairs hall.

At the top of the stairs was a newel carved into a life-size wooden head of Roger Corman, one of 
Uncle Buddy's rivals. I turned it and heard a click from the staircase. I didn't know what it did 
yet, but in this house a click was always worth having.

At the east end of the hall a blue velvet sack was poking out from under a closed window. When I 
tugged at it, the rest of it stayed stuck. So I lifted the window with one hand and pulled the sack 
in with the other. Inside was a Maltese finch.

Five.

In the upstairs bathroom at the west end, everything was overdone, from the temperature-controlled 
marble toilet seat to the Oscars that served as hot and cold taps. There was a bath mat with Jack 
Valenti's picture on it. Under the mat lay a red punch card.

I rode the closet back down with the second peg and put the finch with the other treasures. Then I 
took the rusty bucket off its peg.


VII. THE BUCKET

North of the game room, the patio door was locked. I unlocked it and stepped out onto a wide 
fieldstone patio. Standing there I remembered swiping one of Uncle Buddy's big Hollywood cigars at 
a party and then being sick on a rose bush in the garden. I snickered a little, thinking about that 
poor rose bush. An orange punch card was lying on the patio. Somewhere nearby several of Morgan 
Fairchild's cats began to cry.

I followed the walkway around the garden, east and north, to the cannon emplacement. There was a 
replica Civil War cannon from "Dracula Meets the Confederacy," pointing out to sea, with a fuse 
sticking up from it and a pyramid of cannon balls beside it. One of its wheels sat on top of a 
small compartment. Uncle Buddy said he always kept it loaded in case those big Hollywood studio 
types came around.

I filled the bucket with water. It leaked, naturally.

I hurried back, south and west to the patio, through the game room and the foyer and into the 
closet, with water dribbling out the whole way, and hung the bucket on the third peg. The peg sank 
under the weight and the closet began to vibrate. I stepped out quickly, ran up the stairs (thanks 
to the newel, they stayed stairs) and opened the closet door at the top. The closet was waiting at 
floor level and the shaft went on up above it. I stepped out onto the closet's roof.

I waited. Then the water had leaked out far enough. The closet shook, the door swung shut, my 
stomach dropped again, and I rode on the roof up to the top of the shaft.

The door there opened into a dusty attic full of cobwebs, with a dusty trunk and a folding ladder 
fixed to a panel in the floor. When I opened the panel the ladder swung down into the hall below, 
and at the same moment there was a click under the trunk's lid. Inside the trunk was a fire hydrant.

Six. I climbed down the ladder and carried it to the living room.


VIII. ROY G. BIV

I gathered up the punch cards from the foyer floor and went through the dining room, where a thin 
piece of paper lay on the long walnut table, to the kitchen. Aunt Hildegarde's kitchen was big and 
sterile, built for feeding Hollywood parties, nothing like the homey kind most people's aunts have. 
The whole family remembers the Christmas everyone got fruit cakes with serial numbers on them. Next 
to the sink was a matchbox.

Stairs led down to the cellar, and there stood one of Uncle Buddy's old props: the huge, 
over-teched computer that saved the earth from the marauding marketeers in "Plan Nine from 
Marketing." It had a closet door beside it, and behind the door was the bottom of the closet shaft, 
far below the closet, with a blue punch card lying on the floor. That made seven.

I turned the computer on. Its machinery roared, and seven rows of lights across its front flashed 
on and off as it came to life. Beneath the lights there was a slot and a button, and at one end a 
hopper.

I fed it the cards. Red, yellow, orange, green, blue, indigo, violet. Each one went in with a 
slurp; the machine rumbled as if something inside were trying to get out, paused as if to think, 
and the lights changed. Somewhere in the house something thudded onto the floor again. When the 
last card was in, the lights spelled out a number:

576-3190

Up in the kitchen I opened the matchbox, took out the red match inside it, and left the box by the 
sink. Then I went out to the cannon again. I took a cannon ball off the pyramid, loaded it, lit the 
match and touched it to the fuse. It sizzled, the cannon fired, and the recoil pushed it back a 
couple of feet. The cannon ball sailed out to sea. The wheel had rolled off the compartment, and 
inside was a catcher's mask.

Seven.

On the front porch I finally opened the mailbox. There was a copy of The Status Line, a yellowed 
piece of paper and a business card:

For fast service, call
ROY G. BIV
COMPUTER SERVICE & REPAIR
576-1851

Roy G. Biv. Red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet. I'd fed the cards in the wrong order. 
Still, the number I'd got was so close to Roy's that I didn't think the order made much difference, 
so I kept it, and I took the yellowed paper with me and picked up the thin paper from the dining 
room.


IX. THE HEDGE MAZE

The garden had been off limits to me as a kid, ever since the summer I climbed a tree and screeched 
like a baboon while Cousin Herman ran off toward the roses shouting that he was a rhinoceros. When 
I found him, he'd pulled every thorn off every one of Aunt Hildegarde's rose bushes, looking for 
the biggest one to be his horn. When she saw her naked rose bushes, Herman blamed it all on me.

In the tulip beds lay a small shovel. I took it and went on past the orchids and the little pond to 
the entrance of the hedge maze. Aunt Hildegarde had always told me never to go in without the map, 
and I always went in anyway and she always had to come and find me. Guests were forever getting 
lost in it at parties. Sometimes I'd wished Herman would go in and never come out. I'd always had 
the feeling he wished the same about me, only more seriously.

I laid the thin paper over the yellowed one, and the lines on each fit together into a map of the 
maze, with an X near the middle.

The hedges were tall and thick. Every turn looked the same: a hedge maze of astonishing complexity, 
paths leading this way and that, forty feet west, twenty feet north, sixty feet east. I followed 
the map turn by turn. Partway in I heard footsteps on the other side of the hedge, and they weren't 
mine.

At last I came to a dead end with a single path leading out to the north. I dug, and the shovel 
uncovered Buck Palace's large rubber stamp.

Eight.

The way out was as long as the way in, and once more I heard footsteps beyond the hedge. I came out 
at the entrance, walked around by the western junction (from the ocean came the distant ringing of 
warning buoys), and put the stamp with the rest. I left the shovel and the papers in the foyer, 
took the red wax statuette down from the mantle, and rode the closet upstairs to fetch the skis.


X. DOWN THE STAIRS ON SKIS

North of the cannon a steep path led down. I tried to walk it and ended up sliding most of the way 
to a cliff. Far below, the beach stretched east, white in the moonlight. Set in the ground was a 
closed hatch, and an unusual ladder lay beside it. I left the flashlight there, since I'd soon be 
in water, and scrambled back up to the cannon.

East of the northeast junction a worn path led out to the top landing of a long, rickety flight of 
wooden stairs down to the beach. As I walked out to it I heard footsteps running south along the 
stone walkway behind me. Herman and I used to race down these stairs in the summer to see who'd 
reach the water first. Usually Herman tripped me and won.

Somewhere along the way the stairs had a gap in them. I put on the skis and shuffled over the edge.

I gained speed fast, and the gap came up ahead. My skis bowed as I hit it, but my momentum carried 
me over. I hit the landing at the bottom, kept going down a short last flight, and sailed out onto 
the sand, stopping with the drag of the beach and a feeble attempt at a snowplow.

Near a small, smoldering fire lay a green match. I took it, lit the red statuette at the fire, and 
as the wax began to dribble down the god I coated the match head in it. Then I took off the skis 
and waded into the chilly water. The candle went out as I swam into the inlet, but the match was 
safe in its coat of wax.

South of the inlet was a dark cave, and inside it a grotto pool with a boat dock and light coming 
in from the north. I took a deep breath and dove. Down, and down again into a passage with a 
current flowing east. West through a narrow, jagged gap, and then up, with the pressure building in 
my chest until I thought my lungs would rupture, and up again until I broke the surface and gasped 
for air.

It was completely dark. I swam north and climbed out, north again and up, feeling my way. Then I 
struck the green match. The wax melted away and it lit.


XI. THE BOMB SHELTER

I was in a concrete room that looked like a bomb shelter. A heavy plank lay across a sawhorse like 
a seesaw. Above its left end a safe hung from a rope that ran up through a pulley and down to a 
pipe on the wall. Above the right end was a closed hatch in the ceiling with a long chain hanging 
from it, and below the hatch two metal hooks stuck out of the wall.

I lit the statuette from the match, quickly, because the match was nearly gone. I lifted the left 
end of the plank so the right end went down, and the match burned to ashes. I pulled the chain and 
the hatch popped open. I touched the candle flame to the rope until it caught. Then I stood on the 
right end of the plank and waited.

The rope burned through and snapped. The safe crashed down onto the left end, and the right end 
threw me straight up through the hatch. I had a brief, thrilling moment of flight, and then I 
landed on my feet on the cliff, right beside my flashlight and the ladder. Grey clouds swept past 
the full moon.

I blew out the statuette and left it with the skis. Then I took the ladder and the flashlight, 
dropped back through the hatch and hung the ladder on the hooks.

The safe was sitting on the end of the plank, its dial set to 3, with a small plaque on the front:

LEVY, REGAN, LEBLING
SAFE COMPANY
UPPER SANDUSKY, OHIO
1936

The first wall safe had taken its directions from the hands of the gods and its numbers from their 
fingers. This one, I guessed, took them from the names. Levy: left, four. Regan: right, five. 
Lebling: left, seven. There was a faint click, and inside was a copy of the film "A Corpse Line."

Uncle Buddy's lost masterwork. Nine.


XII. THE TENTH TREASURE

I climbed the ladder, went back up to the cannon and around to the house, and laid the film with 
the other treasures in the living room. Then I picked up the telephone and dialed 576-3190.

An answering machine picked up. It was Aunt Hildegarde's voice. "I can't come to the phone right 
now. I'm dead. Don't forget to look in the hopper." Then the line went dead.

I went down to the cellar. In the computer's hopper sat Uncle Buddy's toupee.

Ten.

Under it lay a chipped wooden peg and a note:

Congratulations Pumpkin! You've found all the "treasures." Now come on down for a big surprise.
Aunt Hildegarde

I looked at the peg and thought of the empty hole beside the first coat peg in the closet. I 
climbed the stairs to the upstairs hall, stepped into the closet, and put the peg in the hole.

The door slammed shut and the floor dropped out from under me. I fell for several seconds, landed 
with a bone-crunching thud, and dropped everything I was holding. Then I slid down a twisting, 
slippery slide and was dumped into a room full of props.

And there was Aunt Hildegarde.

She was alive, and she was tied to the conveyor belt of a whirling buzz saw. A man was standing 
over her. She saw me and screamed, and the man turned around, and I knew him at once: my childhood 
nemesis, Cousin Herman.


XIII. THE PROP VAULT

The vault was piled with props: an elvish sword of great antiquity, salivating hedge shears, giant 
nail clippers, a boomerang mail bag, a glowing mop, a gun, a big stick and a hairy club. A chute 
led out to the east.

I grabbed the sword. Herman grabbed the mail bag. Aunt Hildegarde, inching toward the blade, said, 
"Herman, stop this silly game this instant and untie me."

I threw the sword at him. It hit him and broke into a hundred pieces; it was only a prop. He threw 
the mail bag at me, and it crumbled; that was only a prop too. "Herman, that's no way to treat your 
cousin," said Aunt Hildegarde.

I took the big stick, and Herman took the hairy club. She was half a foot from the blade now. "I'm 
just glad your Uncle Buddy isn't alive to see this," she said. I hit him with the stick and it 
shattered. He swung the club at me and it crumbled. "Herman, enough is enough," said Aunt 
Hildegarde. "You are in big trouble, buster. Pumpkin, untie me then run and get your Uncle Buddy's 
belt."

I took the glowing mop. Herman took the nail clippers. The saw was three or four inches from her 
head. "Now you two stop that horseplay and get me off this contraption," she demanded.

I threw the mop, and this time Herman dropped to the ground. I guess that one wasn't a prop after 
all. He was already starting to get up, and the blade was less than an inch from the blue-gray 
hairs of Aunt Hildegarde's head.

I untied her. The conveyor belt stopped, and the saw began to slow. Herman raced to the chute and 
jumped in, and I heard his squeaky laugh trail off into the distance.

Aunt Hildegarde got up, rubbing the back of her head. She was a bit shaken. She told me she had 
been watching me all night while I hunted for the treasures, and that as she followed my progress 
she began to realize we weren't the only ones on the estate. Then someone rapped her on the skull, 
and the next thing she knew, Herman was tying her to the buzz saw.

"I guess he couldn't stand to see you inherit the family fortune," she said. "Well, it's all yours 
now. I knew you could do it."

"I'm sorry I put you through all this, Pumpkin," she said, "but your Uncle Buddy and I had to be 
sure that whoever inherited the estate and the studio would be clever enough to handle it all." The 
only way she could be sure her will would be carried out was to oversee it herself, so she had 
faked her death. She hugged me so tight she squeezed the air out of my lungs.

Tomorrow we'd go and see her lawyer, and he'd take care of the paperwork. She knew I'd take good 
care of Hildebud and the studio. As for her, she was sure it wouldn't be long before the press 
found out she was alive, so she planned to rest in the south of France while the story leaked out. 
It would be great publicity for the studio, she said.

Then she added, "And let's hope we've seen the last of your Cousin Herman."
