MOONMIST

Four Cases at Tresyllian Castle


PROLOGUE: THE GATE

People who hear me tell this story always ask the same thing: what colour did you say at the gate?
I can never give a straight answer. I remember saying red. I also remember saying blue, and green,
and yellow. I remember the same castle, the same guests and the same ghost, and four different
nights, each ending with a different person in Bolitho's grip and a different treasure in my hands.
So I tell it the only honest way I can: the beginning once, and the endings four times.

It was a misty night in Cornwall when my little sports car stopped at the gate of Tresyllian
Castle. In the moonlight I could just make out the ornament on the ironwork: a wyvern, the winged
two-legged dragon of the Tresyllian arms, shown in profile, with one eye glinting. I pressed it.
The eye glowed red, and a voice from a hidden speaker asked me to state my title and my first and
last name.

"Sir Charles Penrose," I said. The Yanks call me Sir Sherlock; I've never been sure whether that's
a compliment.

"And what is your favorite color, Sir Charles?"

I told it. The voice said, "Jolly good! The spare bedroom is decorated in" whatever I'd said, and
the red eye turned green, and the gate creaked open. Floodlights blazed on in the courtyard. The
dark stone turrets rose into the mist, and the castle looked even lovelier than it does in the
tourist brochure. My car, I noticed later, had somehow become the same colour as my answer.

A beautiful red-haired young woman came running out to meet me. It was my friend Tamara Lynd, who
had written to me from England. "Charles!" she cried. "You sweet thing, to answer my letter in
person!" She asked whether I'd read the letter properly. I had: her engagement, the White Lady, and
the fact that someone was trying to kill her.


THE GUESTS

In the foyer her fiancé came striding to meet us: Lord Jack Tresyllian, tall and handsome,
dark-browed, in a dinner jacket. "So you're that famous young sleuth whom the Yanks call Sir
Sherlock!" he said, shaking my hand. His blue eyes sized me up with a friendly twinkle that stayed,
I thought, on the surface.

In the drawing room a tall, graceful older couple were sipping sherry: Montague Hyde, who bowed
stiffly, and Vivien Pentreath, the sculptor, who murmured "How do you do" in a low, attractive
voice. Both stiffened when they heard I was a detective, and Tamara had to explain that I wasn't
the police kind.

In the new great hall, on a floor of black and white marble worn smooth over the centuries, a young
couple were dancing to a portable radio. She was the Honourable Iris Vane, a London deb with
boyishly short dark hair and flirtatious green eyes. "Tell me, Sir Charles," she chirped, "are you
one of those brutally fascinating American private eyes?" I admitted that I was. He was Lieutenant
Ian Fordyce of the Coldstream Guards, tall and blond, with a scarlet cummerbund and a Guards
officer's swagger.

Then Dr. Nicholas Wendish came down the stairs: middle-aged, grizzled mustache, rumpled evening
clothes, and hawk eyes behind gold-rimmed spectacles. He had read about one of my cases in New
York. "Is it a case that brings you to Cornwall?" he asked. I said it was. He said only, "I see."

Tamara took me up to the gallery and along to my bedroom, where Queen Victoria had once slept, done
out entirely in my favourite colour. There she told me about the ghost. She had seen it three
times. The last time, leaving the office late at night, she had opened the door to the first-floor
corridor and found a figure with long blonde hair and a dead-white face, holding a sword and about
to attack. She had screamed and slammed the door. "I don't know whether this ghost is real, or
someone just play-acting," she said. "It can sneak around anywhere it pleases in the whole castle."

The ghost, I gathered, was the White Lady of family legend. And its long blonde hair was the hair
of Deirdre, the girl who had hoped to marry Jack before Tamara came along, and who had fallen down
the castle's ancient well.

Bolitho, the elderly butler, brought up my luggage. When Tamara had gone, he coughed diffidently
and told me what he had seen the night before. After the guests had retired, he had come into the
new great hall to turn off the lights and seen the ghost on the far side of the room, bending over,
groping for something on the floor. It had fled into the darkness of the drawing room, and when he
followed with the lights on, it had vanished. The front door was still locked from the inside.

Then Bolitho handed me a small aerosol labelled JUST PRESS THE BUTTON, "should you find yourself in
any danger from our ghost." And on his way out he added, with no explanation, that if I wished to
view myself from all angles while dressing, I could do so by adjusting the wall mirror.

I took a bath, changed into evening clothes, and went down.


DINNER

At 7:54 Bolitho came in to set out the buffet, left a note on Jack's napkin, and went out. While
everyone was admiring the punchbowl, I read it. The servants, by the terms of the late Lord
Lionel's will, were leaving the castle after the dinner gong and would not be back until morning.
Gladys, the upstairs maid, would not be coming back at all. She had left a note of her own on the
writing desk in the sitting room. I put Jack's note back where I found it.

The gong sounded. Iris proposed a self-serve line at the buffet. Jack picked up his note, read it
with a troubled face, and then announced his engagement to Tamara, to mixed reactions. The food was
excellent and the conversation subdued.

Then a voice said, "Good evening, all," and chuckled.

"Good Lord!" gasped Jack. "That's Uncle Lionel's voice!"

It came from the bronze bust of Lionel in the corner, sculpted by Vivien. The bust was hollow.
Under it sat a small tape recorder on a clockwork timer, set to play on this date at this hour.
Lionel reminded us that his will required the seven of us to dine together at the castle on his
birthday. He reminded us of a valuable artifact he had brought back from his travels and lost. It
was not lost, he said, but hidden. "I suggest you look under the punchbowl."

The first clue was there. "This first clue is merely to sharpen your wits," said the dead man. He
addressed a word to Deirdre, too: "your one goal in life, I believe, is to become Jack's wife,
heaven knows why!" He had given the second clue to one of the people at the table, and invited
them, with a sardonic chuckle, to share it. "So now, my friends," he finished, "let the game begin!"

That is where the night forks.


THE RED CASE

The first clue showed the King of Clubs beside an African chief with a war club, and the King of
Diamonds beside a crowned vulture clutching a diamond. Lionel's second clue had gone not to Jack
but to Vivien, which struck me as odd. She handed it over.

"Forbidden fruit tempted the very first lass. 'Twas once in a garden but now in a glass."

The chapel's great stained-glass window showed Eve offering Adam the apple. Stuck to the apple was
the third clue: "Despite its appearance, the fruit was quite sour. One bite of the apple drove Eve
from her bower." So, a garden. Beyond the courtyard lay a hedge maze, and at its heart a garden
with a goldfish pond, where I dug and found the fourth clue: "Out of the sunshine, into the rain...
The end of the story is... Abel and CAIN." CAIN was underlined.

Rain meant umbrellas, and the umbrella stand stood in the foyer. Among the umbrellas was an odd
cane shaped like a baseball bat, covered with hard, faceted bumps under a new coat of paint. I
scraped the paint off, and there was the war club, its faceted bumps glittering like the King of
Diamonds. I gave it to Tamara. "That's super!" she said. "We can't thank you enough!"

The maid's note was on the desk in the sitting room, in Gladys's best spelling. In cleaning a
certain room she had found SUMMING DREDFUL, and maybe there was more reason than ANYONE SUSPECKS
why the ghost prowled about. "I am not the type who peeks through keyholes, either," she wrote,
"but maybe it is high time someone did!"

I collected a brass lantern from the kitchen and went to my room. Running my fingers round the
frame of the wall mirror, I found a hidden switch. A section of wall creaked open onto a narrow,
cobwebbed passage that ran between the rooms and the castle's outer wall. I waited there, in the
dust and lantern light, until well past midnight.

At 12:41 a figure came out of the dark: long blonde hair, silvery white, glowing with an almost
unearthly light. She saw me and froze. Then she spoke, quickly. "Please don't speak, just listen!
I'm really Deirdre, and I'm alive." Jack had pushed her down the well, she said. An underground
stream had carried her out to sea, where a yacht picked her up. She had come back in disguise to
frighten him, to find proof that he had murdered his uncle, and to frame him for her own murder by
planting a tiny red jewel from her necklace in his clothes. But she had lost the jewel in the
drawing room. Now that I was on the case, she would leave England with the yacht's captain. Then
she ran off towards the priest hole.

I found the jewel deep in the pile of the drawing room carpet. Then I took the secret passage down
a ladder and up some stone steps to a hidden door into the master bedroom, where Lord Jack lay
asleep. In a drawer of his tallboy was Deirdre's necklace, with one empty socket. The jewel fitted
it.

I unlocked his door and put my eye to the keyhole, as Gladys had advised. I saw a microphone, its
wires running towards the family crest on the outside of the door. The bronze crest was loose.
Behind it was a tape recorder built into the door. I pressed play.

I heard Lionel testing it. I heard him tell Deirdre he suspected Jack of wanting him dead. Then
Jack's voice, with a cold-blooded chuckle, telling his uncle that his time had come, then a
muffled, smothered cry: "Jack! Stop!" And then silence.

I woke Jack and arrested him. He denied everything until I told him about the tape, and then he
rushed at me. Bolitho appeared from nowhere, caught him from behind, and said calmly, "I'll guard
this villain for you." Jack confessed. He had murdered Lionel for the title and the castle.
Deirdre, who knew what he was planning, had been blackmailing him into marrying her, so he had
thrown her down the well. He had just never known that the well had given her back.


THE BLUE CASE

This time the first clue showed a skeleton in a Chinese mandarin's costume, and Jack had the
second. It was three verses with a word missing from each: go with a song; a ship laden with
innocent maidens; nothing to equal good wine.

The sheet music on the sitting room piano was Beethoven's "Suite No. 9," with a red star over the
first four letters: SUIT. The tapestry in the drawing room showed a maiden with a unicorn, and
someone had stitched a red star onto her ARM. In the basement wine rack, under the old tower, was a
bottle from a Cornish winery called Our Own Vintage, with a red star drawn over OUR. SUIT, ARM,
OUR: suit of armour. In the helmet of the armour in the new great hall I found the third clue. "My
al___ has no glamour; Its '____e' tones do clam___. Can you find me?"

Alarm, base, clamour: a bell. There was a huge one on the fighting deck at the top of the tower
keep. Up three flights I climbed, into the moonlight and the sound of the sea, and inside the bell
was a fossil skull. I gave it to Tamara.

Bolitho had told me the ghost must need reading glasses. The hall had been ablaze with light, yet
it had groped blindly on the marble floor. So I searched the marble, and found something small,
smooth and slippery: a contact lens, so clear that no wonder the ghost had never found it.

Gladys's note this time ended differently. The first sign of a nut case, her Dad always said, was
talking to yourself, "and some does it on paper." In Vivien's untidy room, in her paint-smeared
wooden box, was a small plastic case holding one contact lens. It was made for two. Deeper in the
box was Vivien's diary, which fell open at a tear-stained page. "O Deirdre, sweet Deirdre! Jack
will pay dearly for your cruel death by losing his new sweetheart..."

The door burst open, and there was Vivien. "Well! I didn't expect MY room to be searched!"

I went back to my room, fetched Bolitho's aerosol from the bathroom, lit my lantern, and stepped
into the passage behind the mirror. I waited by the secret door into Vivien's room. At thirteen
minutes past midnight it burst open, and the ghost came out, glowing, long-haired, cold-eyed, and
pointed a blowgun at my chest.

I pressed the button. She dropped the blowgun and clapped her hands over her mouth and nose. Her
face went greenish, and she collapsed. Under the heavy white makeup, the gown and the blonde wig
was Vivien.

When she came to, I arrested her, and Bolitho once again appeared to hold her. Vivien had loved
Deirdre with a jealous, consuming love, and hated Jack for coming between them. When Deirdre fell
down the well, which was an accident, Vivien was certain she had killed herself because Jack had
abandoned her. So Vivien put on Deirdre's face to hunt for proof, to prick Jack's conscience, and
to terrorise the woman who had taken Deirdre's place. The lens she had dropped had been her own.


THE GREEN CASE

The first clue showed an Amazon hunter aiming a blowgun into the treetops. Jack's clue read: "My
first is an 'I,' but find an 'eye' that sees not."

The game room had a rhino's head on the wall, and one of its glass eyes was in backwards. Behind it
was the third clue: "My second is in never but not in ever, and lies in a hidden 'end'." The letter
N. And a hidden end could only be a dead end in the secret passages. I found the switch behind my
mirror, lit the lantern, and followed the passage north, west and south until it ended at a
peephole. There lay the fourth clue: "My third is the silent side of knight. All together I am what
you could use for poison-pen letters." K. I, N, K: ink.

In the little office, on a tall old-fashioned desk, was an inkwell, and inside it was the treasure:
Moonmist. Lionel's journal of his Amazon expedition lay beside it. Moonmist was a drug taken from a
rare plant called the Moonflower. The natives tipped their poison darts with it, though it might
also make a valuable medicine, and they insisted it must be gathered only when the full moon was
misted over.

Bolitho had said this ghost was groping on the marble floor, and, he added, groping with its left
hand, which he and the other servants agreed meant it was left-handed. On the marble I found a
contact lens. In Dr. Wendish's precise, orderly bedroom, under the things in his medical kit, was a
small plastic case holding a second lens. Under that were a shimmering white gown and a blonde wig.
Inside the wig were several greyish hairs, the colour of the doctor's own.

Gladys had written that some people talk to themselves on paper. In the library, in a bookcase, I
found Dr. Wendish's lab notebook, a record of fiendish experiments on the patients at his clinic.
Near the end was one line: "Finally took care of Poldark's granddau. (comely wench), pity she
disc'd facts of his end."

I went back to his room and waited. The door burst open. "Well! I didn't expect MY room to be
searched!" When I told him about the notebook he rushed at me, and Bolitho caught him, and he
confessed to murder.

Deirdre had suspected that her grandfather died of the doctor's experiments, and she had written to
Lionel begging him to investigate. Lionel did. Wendish found out, and silenced them both. The ghost
had been his cover for midnight searches for Lionel's treasure, and his attacks on Tamara were
meant to make everyone wonder whether Deirdre might still be alive.


THE YELLOW CASE

The first clue was a photograph of the singer Pearl Bailey. Jack's clue was a scrap of Poe: "How
the danger sinks and swells, By the sinking or the swelling in the anger of the ____s..."

Bells. On the fighting deck, inside the great bell, was the third clue, more Poe: "And so, all the
night-tide, I lie down by the side Of my darling, my darling, my life and my bride, In her tomb by
the sounding sea."

I fetched the lantern from the kitchen and went down to the dungeon, where an iron maiden stood in
the corner, its inside covered with spikes. I stepped into it. The floor sank beneath me, down into
a secret tomb with a stone coffin. Inside the coffin was the fourth clue. "If you search for 'A
Cask of Amontillado,' don't get trapped!"

In Poe's story, a man is walled up alive in a wine cellar. I rode the iron maiden back up, crossed
into the basement where the wine was kept, and pulled down the damp, mossy bricks of the wall.
Behind them was a hidden crypt, and in it a skeleton. Around its neck hung a strand of black
pearls, the rarest kind in the world. Whoever she had been, I thought of the White Lady, and of the
legend of a woman walled up in the castle, and I took the necklace very gently.

Then the ghost. In the passage behind my mirror, outside the secret door to Iris's room, someone
had left a blowgun with a poison dart in it and the ghost costume. Inside the blonde wig were
several red hairs, the colour of Tamara's.

I didn't want to believe it. I searched the drawing room carpet, as Bolitho's story suggested, and
found a tiny red jewel. In Tamara's bedroom, which was neat as a pin, there was a journal under the
bed: Lionel's South Pacific journal, describing a black pearl necklace. Under the bed, too, was a
receipt from a pet shop in Frobzance for the purchase of an adder. Someone had written "Iris" on it
and then viciously crossed it out. In her jewellery case was a delicate earring with an empty
setting, and the red jewel fitted it perfectly.

The door burst open, and Tamara stood there. "Well! I didn't expect MY room to be searched!"

I arrested my friend. She denied it all until I told her about the receipt, and then she rushed at
me, and Bolitho held her, and she confessed to fraud. She had never been sure she could keep Jack's
love. She had feared Iris as the rival who might one day take him from her, so she had set out to
make Iris look like a vengeful ghost bent on killing Jack's new love. That meant playing the ghost
herself, and writing to me of attacks on her own life. Deirdre's death, it turned out, had been
nothing but an accident.

Of the four endings, that is the one I tell least often.


EPILOGUE

Every version of that night ends with the same words in my head: Congratulations, Sir Charles. And
every version ends with Bolitho, who seems to appear wherever he is needed, standing behind someone
I had shaken hands with that evening.

I still have the brochure from the chest of drawers in my room. When people ask me what colour I
chose at the gate, I tell them to go to Cornwall and press the dragon's eye, and find out for
themselves.
