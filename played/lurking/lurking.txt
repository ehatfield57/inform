THE LURKING HORROR
One night at G.U.E. Tech

I'd waited until the last minute again. End of term, a twenty-page Classics paper due in the
morning, and every TechNet terminal in the dorm taken. So I slogged across campus through the worst
blizzard of the winter to the old Comp Center, jumping at every shadow, because everyone had heard
about the disappearances by then. Nobody talked about them. Everybody locked their doors.

The Terminal Room was nearly empty. Pizza boxes, Coke cans, posters, the hum of machines. One
hacker sat at a terminal in the corner, someone I half knew, the kind of guy who has lived in this
building since before I was born. I took the whiz-bang pc by the door, sat down in one of those ugly
molded plastic chairs, and logged in.

"Good evening," the computer said. "You're here awfully late."

The task list had my paper on it in blinking red letters: DUE TOMORROW. I clicked on it, and the
box grew until it filled the screen. The title was mine. Nothing after it was.

It looked like a facsimile of something very old: a kind of Olde English I'd never seen, Latin that
wasn't quite Latin, scraps of Hebrew and Arabic script. Here and there a phrase in English. I knew I
should look away, and I couldn't. My finger found the MORE box on its own.

The second page talked about a "summoning," or a "visitor." The third was a poem, with woodcuts that
made my stomach turn, and someone had typed a translation between the lines:

    He returns, he is called back.
    The loyal ones make a sacrifice.
    Those who survive will meet him.
    They will live, yet die.

The fourth page was a photograph. I tried to pull back from the screen and couldn't. Was that a
mouth? And what was in it?

I touched MORE one last time, and fainted.

---

There was a place. That's the only word I have for it. A broken, rocky surface where things moved,
where everything had been drained of color. Harsh sounds. Something sticky at my feet. A noise began
far below and pulled me down into a bowl of black basalt, and a crowd of dim shapes pushed me toward
a low pile of rocks.

One stone in the pile was glowing, smooth and bright as a coal. I picked it up. I don't know why.

The dark in front of me folded in on itself and became a creature. It towered over the crowd,
spraying ichor, its palps pounding on the rock. It turned toward the stone in my hand, and the crowd
shoved me forward, and its mandibles closed around me, and the last thing I remember is its mouth.

I woke up with my forehead on the keyboard. A nightmare. Falling asleep over a term paper, how
embarrassing. Except that the stone was in my hand.

"Losing, huh?" said the hacker, and slid into my chair. He typed for a while, swearing softly, and
told me my directory had gotten mixed up on the file server with some files from the Department of
Alchemy. "People's names for their nodes are getting weird," he said. "This one's called
'Lovecraft.'" My paper was gone. "Sorry. Maybe they could help you down there."

---

The hacker had keys. Everyone knew it. "I'm a licensed locksmith," he told me, a little proud, and
showed me a master key on his ring. He wasn't going to just hand it over. But there was cold Chinese
food in the fridge in the filthy little kitchen down the hall, and the microwave there has more
buttons than my pc. Five minutes on high. "Ah! Serious food!" he said, and ate it like a shark at a
feeding frenzy, and then he loaned me the master key. "Just don't get into trouble, okay? I'll find
you later and get it back."

I wish I'd listened.

There was a flashlight hidden behind the access panel in the elevator. In a temporary building
basement, the kind they put up in the Second World War and never tore down, someone had left a pair
of electrician's gloves and a crowbar. I took them both. I was starting to think like someone who
expected to need things.

The basement of the Aero building had a rusty old forklift. I drove it east into Dead Storage, where
junk is stacked to the ceiling on rotting pallets, and shoved junk around until I'd cleared a path
one forklift wide. Beyond that was Ancient Storage, which is what's deader than dead storage, and in
the floor a manhole nobody had opened in a very long time. The crowbar moved it.

Below was a tunnel of old brick, and at the end of it a cave somebody had reinforced with wood and
iron and steel, and at the bottom of the cave, an altar.

It was a slab of New England granite, carved all over with symbols. The biggest one I recognized
from the screen upstairs. The top was stained brown, shading to red at the edges. There was an iron
plate set in the floor beside it that I very carefully did not touch. Lying next to the stone was a
thin, sharp knife.

I took the knife.

---

Upstairs, in the Infinite Corridor, a maintenance man was waxing the floor in the middle of the
night. Every time I tried to pass him, he swung the floor waxer into my way with a deft little turn,
and he never once looked at me. I took the container of wax he'd left by the doors and waited, and
followed him east one room at a time until I reached a glass-fronted fire cabinet. I broke the glass
with the crowbar and took the axe.

When I cut his power cord, the waxer whined down and he finally noticed me. He climbed off growling
things I didn't want to understand and came at me much faster than a man that size should move. I
poured the wax across the floor. His hand went past my throat by inches as he went down, and then he
just kept slipping and falling and getting up, like a badly made windup toy.

I got past him. At the far end of the corridor he caught up with me, and convulsed, and burst into a
crowd of small squealing creatures that scattered into the dark. I stood there for a long time after
that.

---

The Brown Building is eighteen stories of meteorology. The elevator was out of order. I climbed. The
master key opened the roof door, and the blizzard came in to meet me, and above the little roof
there was a weather dome with a peach tree growing inside it.

Something hit the dome from outside while I was in there. A dark shape, clinging to the glass,
keening.

There was a tub of potting earth under the tree. I dug in it, and my fingers found something hard: a
human hand, dried and chewed, with a tattoo on the back and the marks of teeth where it had been
bitten off.

I heard claws on the ladder outside. I didn't wait for it to come in. I went out onto the roof with
the hand in one pocket, and when the thing rose up at me on its wings, baring its needle teeth, I
threw the stone at it. The stone went right through it, as if it were made of air, and sailed over
the edge of the building, and the creature went screaming after it into the storm.

I found the stone later in the courtyard at the foot of the building, sitting in the snow, perfectly
calm. I put it back in my pocket. I was beginning to understand that it was on my side.

---

Up on the walkway around the base of the Great Dome there was supposed to be a ladder. There was a
shiny, wet rope hanging there instead. I climbed it in the gloves, and only when I was up on the
catwalk did I see where it went: across the catwalk to something large and squishy squatting at the
far end. A bright blue eye opened in it. The rope pulled back into it, because it wasn't a rope. The
whole thing poured through the railing and was gone before I could breathe.

It left the ladder behind.

Outside, on the very top of the Great Dome, where fraternities put cows and Volkswagens, there was a
bronze plug, and under the plug a rolled-up note in shaky handwriting:

    I can no longer face what I've been doing. I refuse to participate in what he is doing any more.
    Either he is insane, or I am insane, or the universe itself is insane. I am the only suicide,
    but I will not be the final death.

I knew the name. It took me a minute to remember why. He was a graduate student who had gone
missing, until they found him at the bottom of the tallest building on campus.

---

The Department of Alchemy is at the end of a dark hall in the Chemistry Building, behind the only
lit door. I knocked. The professor who answered was smiling. "Good evening! I don't get many visitors
this late. You're not one of my students, are you?" He brought me in before I could answer.

I showed him the note. "What drivel," he said. "He had clearly gone over the edge." He was looking
at me the whole time with a sort of interest I didn't like.

"Ah! You'd like to see the lab?"

There was a pentagram chalked on the lab floor. He cut one of its lines with a small knife, pushed
me into the middle, redrew the line, and went to his bench. He talked while he worked. Alchemy was
like chemistry, he said, except that some natural laws are enforced by persons, not physics. Some of
them grant power, if you placate them. Or bribe them. "When I'm done, they won't laugh anymore."

He stepped into a second pentagram and began to chant, and pointed at me now and then, and I
understood exactly what he meant by bribe.

I still had the altar knife. I waited until he was deep in the chant, and then I cut my lines.

He saw. "Stop, don't move!" he said between verses, and the chant went from commanding to pleading.
The room got colder. A black mist was gathering, and parts of it were darker than others, and the
darker parts had a shape. I pushed out of the pentagram and shoved the lab bench aside, and there was
a trapdoor under it. When I pulled it open, the mist went wild and a voice gibbered out of the air,
and the professor jumped out of his own pentagram toward me. I dropped through the trapdoor.

The noise from above was enormous. A scream. Equipment smashing. Light around the edges of the
trapdoor, and then a whimper, and then nothing at all.

When I climbed back up, the lab was covered in something red and sticky. All that was left of him
was a ring on the floor: a G.U.E. Tech class ring, a brass hyrax.

---

There was a vat of tarry liquid on the bench that had survived. I put the hand in it. The liquid
bubbled furiously, and the hand bobbed up, and a finger moved. Then all of them. It tried to crawl
out over the rim. When I reached in for it, it ran up my arm and settled on my shoulder, quiet as a
cat. The class ring fit it perfectly.

I don't know whose hand it was. I think it was on my side, too.

There was a flask in the temporary lab, the kind they keep liquid nitrogen in. I took that. In the
hall outside the elevator I met the urchin, a teenager in a ski hat who'd been stealing from me all
night. He saw the hand twitch on my shoulder and screamed. "I heard what happens around here! I'm
not gettin' fed to a monster!" Something dropped out of his parka as he ran: a bolt cutter.

---

Under the Aero building, past a crack in the subbasement wall, is a coffin-shaped little room people
call the Tomb of the Unknown Tool. The graffiti there goes back decades. In the floor is a rusty
hatch with a huge padlock that the master key opened, screaming. Warm, rotten air came up out of it.

The steam tunnels are the tropics after the blizzard. Mold on the ceiling. The floor squishes. And
from far off, a chittering, scratching sound that got louder.

There was a pressure valve on the steam pipe. I loosened it with the crowbar and waited. When the
rats came, there were more of them than I have ever seen, and I opened the valve full in their faces.
They ran screaming. One stayed behind, dead, and on its neck was a brand: the same symbol as the
altar.

At the end of the tunnel the brick was rotten, and through a hole I pried in it I could see a steel
rod and, beyond it, the bottom of the elevator shaft. I won't describe everything I did with the
padlock and a greasy chain and the elevator machinery. I'll just say that when I called the elevator
from the second floor and let its doors close, I heard a tearing, rending crash from the basement,
and when I forced the doors open again, the wall at the bottom of the shaft was gone.

The rats were waiting on the other side. I gave them the steam again.

---

Past the end of the steam tunnels the walls stop being walls. The passage is mud, pushed into shape
by something, and it slopes down into a wide chamber full of slots in the walls, and in every slot
something moved. Wires ran into each of them from a hole in the floor, thin and ropelike.

They were urchins. All of them. Or things made to look like urchins, with wire in their heads.

The main wire was as thick as my wrist. I cut it with the bolt cutter. It whipped away down the hole
like a snake, and the urchins burst out of their slots, and twitched, and fell, and didn't get up.

Below that were the wet tunnels, a maze of mud with oily water on the floor. I'd have died in there.
But the hand on my shoulder pointed with its mutilated ring finger, north, then down, then south, and
gripped me tight, and I followed until I came to a wall hung with a curtain of moldy slime.

I opened the flask. A white mist boiled out, and I poured the liquid over the curtain, and it froze,
and cracked, and fell away in pieces. Behind it was an ancient wooden door. The hacker's master key
fit that too. Three out of five doors at Tech, he'd said.

It locked behind me.

---

The Inner Lair is a pool of stagnant water six inches deep, and most of it is taken up by a whitish
mass that pulses in time with a noise I could almost understand. Wires and cables ran down into it
from everywhere. On the wall, absurdly, was a metal junction box, with coaxial cable coming in one
side and a living cable running out the other side into the mass.

The hand leaped off my shoulder into the water and dove, again and again, in one spot. I reached in
after it and found a line under the surface, thick and cold. A power line.

Then the hacker came in. He'd gotten suspicious about my problems with the net, he said, and traced
coax through bridges and repeaters that weren't on any chart, and here he was. "That thing is
interfaced to the whole campus net. Commercial, government, even military." He took out a little
pair of wire strippers and walked toward it, as if it were a badly wired closet.

The noise stopped. The wires began to dance. The mass flowed toward him as fast as he walked toward
it, and they met, and he screamed for a long time, and then he was inside it.

I swung the fire axe at the power line. Insulation. Copper. The line parted, and the live ends fell
into the water in a storm of sparks. I tore the cover off the junction box, unscrewed the coax, and
shoved the live line into the socket.

The mass seized. It made the sound of a huge machine running dry. It compacted and darkened, and for
a moment I could see human outlines inside it, all around something that was not human and not
animal and not anything. Then it solidified, and out of the smoking debris rose a thing with scaly
wings and baleful yellow eyes and a mouth that wasn't a mouth, just a hole into nothing.

The stone in my pocket was hot. It was glowing red, bright red, and it didn't burn my hand.

The thing tensed to leap, and I threw the stone.

It stuck to the creature's hide and burned brighter and brighter, and small hands came out from under
its scales and tugged at it, uselessly. Holes of black nothing opened around the stone. The thing
screamed, almost like a person, and its own mouth swallowed it, wings and all, until there was only
a cloud of greasy smoke around a glowing stone hanging in the air.

Then the smoke was gone too, and the stone dropped into the water, no longer glowing.

Something moved in the debris. It was the hacker, slimed and dazed, blinking at me like a man who
has just woken up in the wrong dorm room. He didn't ask for his key back. He didn't say anything at
all for a long time.

---

I'm writing this in the Terminal Room. It's nearly morning. The storm has stopped.

My paper is still gone. I'll have to explain that, somehow, to a Classics professor. I'm going to
leave out the altar, and the professor of alchemy, and the urchins with wires in their heads, and the
hand, which is sitting on the desk next to me right now, very still, wearing a dead man's class ring.

I have the stone in my pocket. Every now and then I take it out and look at it, just to make sure
it's only a stone.
