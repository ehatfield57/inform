THE RIDDLE OF THE CROWN JEWELS
Being a reprint from the reminiscences of John H. Watson, M.D.

I. A SUMMONS IN THE FOG

It was five o'clock on a Saturday morning in June of 1887, the week of the Queen's Golden Jubilee, 
when I found myself once more in Baker Street. The fog was so thick that the street vanished a few 
yards to the north and south of me, and I stood in the spill of the gas lamp outside number 221-B, 
huddled into my coat and shivering in the predawn chill. My old landlady, Mrs Hudson, had sent me 
an urgent summons, and I had come.

I knocked. A bolt slid back and Mrs Hudson's worried face appeared at the door.

"Oh Doctor Watson, I'm so glad to see you," she said. "It's Mr Holmes, sir. Three days have come 
and gone since he's been out of his rooms. He won't eat and I know he hasn't slept. Now there's a 
visitor who says he's here about some important government business. I tried to send him away, but 
he insisted on waiting in the parlour. That's when I sent for you. Thank God you've come."

The entry hall was as I remembered it: small, underheated, and lit by a feeble gas light. I climbed 
the well-worn stairs, paused on the landing outside the study with some dread of what I might find, 
and went in.

Nothing had changed. A pile of letters was still transfixed to the mantelpiece by a jackknife. Next 
to them lay Holmes's revolver, the Persian slipper in which he keeps his tobacco, and his favourite 
pipe. The violin had been tossed onto a stack of newspapers that had plainly never been read. And 
on the sofa lay Holmes himself, pale and emaciated, his sharp eyes burning with fever. He was 
staring at a phial in one hand, and his other hand rested near a hypodermic syringe in a neat 
morocco case. I knew the signs. He was fighting one of the black depressions brought on by what he 
calls "the dull routine of existence."

I did not argue with him. I took the slipper and the pipe from the mantelpiece, took a newspaper 
from the pile, and handed it to him.

His eye fell on a notice about the Tower of London, and he was on his feet in an instant. He hurled 
the phial and the case into the fireplace, where they shattered, and he did not even notice. 
"They've closed the Tower?" he exclaimed. "Most singular." Then he strode to the door, called down 
to Mrs Hudson for a tray of food, dropped back onto the sofa, made a steeple of his fingertips, and 
fixed his gaze on me. My friend had returned.

Presently Mrs Hudson showed up the visitor, a worried-looking gentleman whom I recognised at once. 
Holmes ushered him to the armchair. "I presume, sir, that you have come about the Jewels."

The visitor half rose in protest. "But... but, how...?"

"Come, sir. What else could it be? The Tower is suddenly closed, and within hours I receive a call 
from the highest official of Her Majesty's government. A simple deduction. Now then, tell me about 
the theft."

They knew very little. The Crown Jewels had been stolen from the Tower some time on Thursday night. 
The theft was found on Friday morning, the Tower was closed and searched, and nothing was found but 
a verse left behind in the empty jewel case. The gentleman handed it to Holmes, who glanced at it 
and passed it to me.

It was a string of riddles. The first spoke of royal bones that "sleep within this heap of stones." 
The second was spoken by a virgin queen who reigned two-score years, whose navy shamed the Armada, 
and with whom the Tudor name died. The third told of an apple that showed someone "the mighty pull 
of gravity." The fourth was spoken by a man whose father was said to die "beneath the Holy City's 
sky," and who was named after him. And the last was a taunt:

     O stately Holmes of England,
     In Baker Street abide.
     For even you must surely fail,
     To save your country's pride.

Before he left, the visitor looked at his watch. "Remember," he said, "this calamity can only be 
hidden from the public until Monday morning at nine o'clock. If the Regalia is not at Buckingham 
Palace in time for the Coronation Day festivities, then all is lost."

When he had gone, Holmes unlocked his bedroom, went in, and came out wearing his Inverness cape and 
deerstalker. "Come when you're ready, Watson. I'll wait for you outside."

II. THE MAN OF ACTION

I took my time. Holmes's bedroom was sparsely furnished, its walls covered with pictures of famous 
criminals, and a jumble of laboratory equipment on the workbench showed that he was in the middle 
of one of his chemical experiments. From the bedside table I took his lamp and his favourite 
magnifying glass, and from the workbench a single ampoule with a neatly lettered label. Downstairs 
in the dreary parlour I found a matchbook. Then I went out into the fog.

Holmes was waiting, and he had been thinking. "Obviously the person who stole the Crown Jewels 
wanted the authorities to turn to me for help," he said, "and it is equally obvious that the thief 
has laid down a trail for me to follow, in hopes of somehow entrapping me. So let's play his game, 
Doctor. But let's play it by our rules. The thief will have tried to anticipate the sequence of my 
actions. But if you were to guide the course of our investigations, he will certainly be thrown off 
the scent." He stepped to one side. "Lead on, good Doctor. I will follow."

I showed him the ampoule. He glanced around quickly and whispered that it was "etherium," a 
knockout gas he had made in secret for the government at his brother Mycroft's request. One ampoule 
would put a whole room of people to sleep for several hours, with no harm done, but it was highly 
explosive near an open flame. "You may keep it if you wish," he said, "but please carry it in a 
secure place, safe from prying eyes." I took off my hat. Inside it was my stethoscope. I took out 
the stethoscope, put the ampoule in its place, and put the hat back on. Then I turned up the wick 
of the lamp, and we set off.

III. THE ABBEY

The first riddle could only mean Westminster Abbey. We walked south through the fog by Orchard 
Street and Audley Street to Hyde Park Corner and the Wellington Arch, then down Grosvenor Place to 
Victoria Square, where a hansom cab stood waiting for a fare, and along Victoria Street. Big Ben 
struck six somewhere in the distance. By the time we reached Broad Sanctuary and the great west 
door of the Abbey, a crowd of tourists had already gathered, but the door was shut. We waited. At 
half past six the light began to grow, and at seven a verger came, unlocked the huge door, and 
pushed it open. The sun came up, as much as it ever does in England.

Inside it was dark, cold and gloomy, and when my eyes adjusted I could see one tomb in particular 
at the end of the Nave. It was a white marble sarcophagus with "ISAACVS NEWTON" on the front: the 
apple and the gravity of the third riddle. Off the South Aisle I found the North Cloister, the 
brass-rubbing centre of the Abbey, where someone had left a pacquet of coloured paper and a rubbing 
crayon. I took both.

In the North Chapel Aisle I found the tomb of Elizabeth and her half-sister Mary Tudor, ringed by 
twelve black marble pillars, with an inscription on the base. I laid a white sheet of paper on it 
and rubbed it with the crayon until the inscription came through. When I tried to lift the sheet it 
would not come at first. It seemed to be stuck to the stone, and it took me a few seconds to peel 
it off. I did the same with a brown sheet on Newton's tomb, and it stuck in just the same way.

The fourth riddle took a moment's thought. The father who was to die beneath the Holy City's sky 
was Henry IV, who died in the Jerusalem Chamber of the Abbey, and the son named after him was Henry 
V. We looked in at the Jerusalem Chamber, a small room panelled in oak and cedar and much warmer 
than the stone elsewhere, and then made our way round to the Confessor Chapel. There, among the 
burial places of kings, Henry V lay in an old wooden coffin on a raised platform with his effigy on 
top. I took a rubbing on green paper. It too stuck to the stone.

IV. INVISIBLE INK

That stickiness bothered me. We walked up to the British Museum in Great Russell Street, and while 
the tourists crowded the exhibit halls I followed the one deserted corridor to the manuscript room. 
An old librarian, as dusty as his books, sat under a sign that read "Quiet, please." He greeted us 
loudly and at length about the Jubilee, and about how only Elizabeth had come anywhere near fifty 
years on the throne, and went bustling about the shelves for a book I had not asked for. I asked 
him to be quiet. He was mortified, apologised, and left the room, mumbling something about the 
third joint of the hind leg of an extinct dung beetle.

On the table lay a book called "Secret Writing and Invisible Inks." A few sentences in its 
introduction were underlined. "Most invisible inks are somewhat sticky to the touch," they read, 
"and virtually all of them become visible when exposed to moderate heat."

We went back to the Abbey. In the Evangelist Chapel a bank of candles lit by worshippers made the 
room so warm that it was uncomfortable to stand in. I held each of my three sheets over the flames, 
and on the back of each, writing slowly appeared.

The white sheet from Elizabeth's tomb read:

     Hickory Dickory Dock.
     Your enemy is the clock.
     When it strikes nine,
     The victory's mine.

and under it, "Here comes a candle to light you to bed. Here comes a chopper to chop off your head."

The brown sheet from Newton's tomb began, "London Bridge is falling down. All that's under it will 
drown," and went on:

     When a number you require,
     And you're banking all thereon.
     Subtract the conquest from the fire,
     Half a league, half a league. Half a league on.

The green sheet from Henry V's tomb told of a "son of Mars" who fought at sea and now stood "above 
the world so high, looking down with watchful eye," and of a pussycat who had been to the Tower to 
look at the Queen: "I left something for you. Go back, if you dare."

Holmes leaned forward, his hawk-like face alight. "There is only one man in all London clever 
enough to plan this, Watson: Professor Moriarty. See how boldly he hides clues in public places, 
knowing all the while that they will remain hidden from the casual eye. Onward, Doctor."

V. A TELESCOPE, AN OAR AND A FLOWER GIRL

The son of Mars on high could only be Nelson on his column in Trafalgar Square. In Birdcage Walk a 
salesman with a souvenir stand saw me coming and offered me a telescope for the parade. I haggled. 
He came down to his "special Jubilee price" of nineteen and six, and then, with a sigh about his 
poor wife's heart, to nineteen shillings. I paid him with my pound note, and he gave me the 
telescope and a shilling in change.

If I was going to be under London Bridge, I would want a boat, and I remembered something I had 
seen in the Black Museum at Scotland Yard, where the Yard keeps relics of famous crimes. Along one 
wall stood a replica of the rowboat in which Captain Bligh had been set adrift. I took its oar. I 
also left my rubbings and the rest of the paper there, since my arms were full.

From Whitehall we walked north to Covent Garden, where the market was packed with housewives. A 
crowd of women had gathered around a girl who had collapsed on the ground, a bunch of flowers in 
her lifeless hand. I put my stethoscope in my ears and listened. Her heart was racing. In my black 
bag were two bottles. The brown one held belladonna, "for bradycardia and other decelerated 
arrhythmias." The blue one held digitalis, "for tachycardia and other accelerated arrhythmias," 
together with an orange pill and some cotton balls. I gave her the orange pill. After a moment her 
eyelids fluttered and she opened her eyes, and the crowd broke into applause. When she was on her 
feet she smiled at me shyly and put a lovely carnation in my lapel. Then a distinguished-looking 
gentleman took her by the arm. "Come, Eliza. We mustn't be late for the ball." She looked back at 
me as she went.

VI. THE PIGEON AND THE RUBY

Nelson's column is a hundred and sixty-seven feet high, and I could not climb it. But old Sherman, 
the animal trainer in Pinchin Lane, has helped Holmes before. Toby was ill, he told us, and all his 
other animals were out on loan. All he had left was a half-trained pigeon. "All you have to do is 
show him something and tell him to get it, and away he goes. The only catch is, he only seems to 
like things that are red." He gave me the bird and asked me to take good care of him.

In Trafalgar Square I turned the telescope on the statue. In Nelson's blind eye was a ruby. I 
showed it to the pigeon, told him to get it, and let him go. He flew up, took the ruby, and circled 
high over our heads, and then he was gone. We walked back to Pinchin Lane. "He came back with a 
ruby," said Sherman. "I don't know where he found it." He handed it to me.

"Bravo, Watson," said Holmes. "Well done, old chap."

VII. THE CHAMBER OF HORRORS

A candle to light you to bed, a chopper to chop off your head: that meant the Chamber of Horrors at 
Madame Tussaud's. The museum in Marylebone Road was closed for renovations, and a guard and a dog 
were patrolling outside. The guard tipped his hat to Holmes, and the dog growled at me.

I knew I would need fire inside. So I filled Holmes's pipe with tobacco from the Persian slipper, 
struck the matchbook's only match, and lit the pipe. Then I put the lamp down with the dead match 
beside it and walked in, smoking.

In the lobby Holmes dropped to his hands and knees and, after a minute, pointed to a tiny speck of 
ash on the floor. In the Chamber of Horrors stood waxworks of Guy Fawkes, Thomas Becket, and 
Charles I kneeling with his head on the block under the executioner's raised axe. Guy Fawkes held a 
torch. I asked Holmes for the newspaper, lit it from my pipe, and lit the torch from the newspaper. 
"An ingenious solution, Watson," said Holmes. "My compliments, old chap." I lifted the wax head of 
Charles I from the block and held the torch to it, and as it melted into a pool on the floor I saw 
a gem glittering in the wax. It was an emerald.

Back in the lobby, Holmes studied the ash. "It is the ash of a Trichinopoly cigar, which is made 
from the dark tobacco that grows only in the Madras district of India. Unless I miss my guess, 
Watson, our mastermind friend has joined forces with an Indian accomplice who does his dirty work 
for him." At the door the guard politely took the torch back, and I collected my lamp.

VIII. BIG BEN

"Your enemy is the clock." We pushed through the crowds at the Houses of Parliament and climbed an 
old stone stair that wound up into the darkness, and climbed, and kept climbing, until we came out 
inside the clock tower. Big Ben hung over a shaft so deep it might as well have had no bottom, with 
a wooden railing around it. Glued to the enormous clapper was a deep-blue sapphire. Before going 
up, I had put the cotton balls from the blue bottle in my ears.

At one o'clock the bell swung towards me, ringing loudly, and the clapper came within reach. Holmes 
clapped his hands over his ears. I tugged at the sapphire, but the bell swung away before I could 
get it free. We waited an hour. At two o'clock the clapper swung up to me again and this time I 
pulled the sapphire loose. "Good show, old fellow," said Holmes, beaming.

IX. WIGGINS, AND A RING

East along Cheapside, in Threadneedle Street, stands the Bank of England. A bank guard was lounging 
against the wall, jangling his keys and watching a group of urchins at play, and one of them looked 
familiar. It was Wiggins, the leader of the Baker Street Irregulars. He would not lift a finger for 
less than his price. "One shilling's me price. Nothing more. Nothing less." I gave him the 
salesman's shilling. He strolled past the guard, said, "Cor blimey. That bird isn't 'alf built, is 
she?", and while the man looked, his hand went in and out of the guard's pocket. He came back and 
slipped a key into my hand. "Sorry I couldn't get everything, guv. I 'ope this is enough."

When I showed Holmes the ruby, his face grew grave. "It's obviously not one of the Crown Jewels, 
Watson. In fact, this gem belongs to the Agra treasure, which was stolen in India many years ago 
and has never been recovered. The case grows more complex. We are in more danger than I had at 
first suspected." He took a signet ring from his finger and gave it to me. "If anything should 
happen to me, take it to my brother Mycroft at the Diogenes Club. He'll tell you what to do."

Under the glass, each gem bore a scratched message. The ruby said "Wear a carnation." The emerald 
said "20/6/87." The sapphire said "2:00 a.m." That was a meeting, I thought, at two in the morning 
on the twentieth, which was Monday. But where, and with whom?

X. UNDER LONDON BRIDGE

On the Embankment an incredibly old rowboat lay by the river, with one empty oarlock. It probably 
leaked, but it looked as though it would float. The river runs high and low with the tide, though, 
and a man in a rowboat cannot reach the underside of a bridge at low water. I waited on the 
Embankment through the long afternoon. Daylight faded, and at a quarter past eight darkness fell 
and the mists came in. I lit the lamp, and Holmes and I got into the boat. I pulled up its anchor, 
set Bligh's oar in the oarlock, and pushed off.

The current carried us downstream. I rowed under London Bridge and dropped the anchor. Looking up, 
I saw a clump of moss lodged among the supports, a slightly different colour from the lichen around 
it. At twenty to nine, with the tide at its height, I reached up and touched it, and it crumbled 
away and left me holding a beautiful opal. Holmes clapped me on the shoulder. "Capital, Watson. 
Capital!"

Scratched on the opal were the words "Password: Swordfish."

We rowed back upstream against the current to the Embankment, and as we climbed out a man stormed 
up and demanded to know what in bloody 'ell we were doing with his boat. He did not wait for an 
answer. He jumped in and rowed off, muttering that he would have to find a safer place to keep it.

XI. THE VAULT

At the bank the guard stopped me at the door. "You can't go in there. And don't even THINK about 
trying to bribe me." I took this as the invitation it was. I offered him the ruby, and he said he 
would need more than one. The emerald was more beautiful, but not worth the risk for only two. The 
opal: "One more and you're in." The sapphire did it. He looked up and down the street and 
whispered, "Good enough. Go on in," and as soon as I was inside, he left.

The vault door had a big shiny dial in the middle. I put the stethoscope to my ears and turned the 
dial slowly, listening to the tumblers: whirr, click, click, whirr, and then, at last, click, 
kerchunk. The door swung open. "Splendid, Watson," said Holmes. "The net is drawing ever tighter 
around our quarry." Inside were rows of safety deposit boxes. Holmes, it seems, cannot abide a 
stethoscope. "Watson," he said, "take that ridiculous thing out of your ears."

The brown sheet had told me which box. You subtract the conquest from the fire: the Great Fire of 
London, which the Monument down the road commemorates, came in 1666, and the Conquest in 1066. The 
number was six hundred. Wiggins's key opened box 600, and inside was a topaz. Holmes reached past 
me and drew out an almost invisible thread. "Muslin. Of Indian origin, and of the type used almost 
exclusively in the manufacture of turbans. Our case is almost complete, Doctor. The Agra gems, the 
Trichinopoly cigar, and now this strand of muslin. Moriarty, for reasons as yet unknown to us, has 
stolen the Crown Jewels. The Agra gems have somehow passed into his hands by way of this Indian 
accomplice, and he is using small pieces of it to lure me into a rendezvous with him."

The topaz said "Bar of Gold." Now I had the place as well.

We stepped out into Threadneedle Street. At once someone seized me from behind, pinned my arms and 
covered my head. I fought, but it was no use. A voice shouted, "Let him go, we've got the other 
one." I was struck on the head and thrown to the ground, and when I came round I was alone. 
Sherlock Holmes had been kidnapped.

Wiggins had seen it all. "It was over before I could lift a finger. Mr 'Olmes was a friend to me, 
'e was. And I'd do anyfing to 'elp you get 'im back."

XII. MYCROFT, AND THE TOWER

At the Diogenes Club an ancient butler tottered up to me, heard my request, and came back to say 
that Mr Mycroft Holmes needed some token to prove who I was. I gave him the signet ring. Mycroft 
came down himself, looking very worried. "Sherlock warned me that you might come, and the fact that 
you are here indicates that he is indeed in trouble. I believe that in order to solve this mystery, 
you are going to have to go to the Tower of London. It will be closed, but you can give the 
password 'Howard' to the guard at the Byward Tower. He will let you in." He studied me for a moment 
as if deciding whether to trust me with his brother's life, gave a small nod, and went back into 
the club.

With Wiggins at my side I went east through the fog, past the roaring night presses of Fleet 
Street, along the river, and over the drawbridge to the Byward Tower. A Yeoman of the Guard stepped 
out to challenge me. I said "Howard," and he stood aside.

In the Jewel Room of the Wakefield Tower the case that had held the Regalia lay twisted and 
smashed, and the mace the thief had used was on the floor beside it. I took it. The green sheet had 
said the thief left something for me at the Tower, and I thought I knew where. In the Bowyer Tower, 
where the Duke of Clarence was drowned in a butt of Malmsey in 1478, the keg still stood in the 
corner, brimming with wine. I knocked out the bung with the mace, and the malmsey poured out across 
the floor. At the bottom of the empty butt lay a garnet. My shoulders were too broad to reach it, 
but Wiggins climbed in and brought it out. Scratched on it were the words "Give me to Akbar."

I did not mean to leave the way I had come in. I meant to leave by water. In the Dungeon under the 
White Tower, which William the Conqueror began, stood a man-size suit of armour. I put it on and 
clanked my way back down to Traitors Gate. A paddle lay there. Beside the lowered portcullis hung a 
chain, and when I grabbed it and lifted my armoured feet off the ground, the old mechanism creaked 
and the gate rose up and locked in place. I took off the armour. Waiting outside the gate, as if it 
had followed me, was the same old boat.

Wiggins and I paddled upstream under London Bridge to the Embankment. As we got out, the boat's 
red-faced owner ran up and yelled, "You again?!!" and gave me a swift kick. He jumped in and 
launched it, and only then found he had nothing to steer with. The last I saw of him, he was 
drifting down the Thames, screaming and shaking his fist at us.

XIII. THE BAR OF GOLD

Down a set of slovenly stairs off Lower Thames Street runs Swan Lane, the seediest stretch of the 
waterfront, and there, under a faded sign, is the Bar of Gold. It was nearly midnight on Saturday. 
The meeting was not until two o'clock on Monday morning, so I waited in Swan Lane. I waited through 
all of Sunday while Big Ben struck the hours, through the dawn and the long day and the dusk, and 
at last the darkness came down again and the clock reached two.

Wiggins would not come in. "You must be stark raving bonkers. That's the most dangerous place in 
London. I'll wait out here, thank you very much!"

Inside was the most infamous opium den in London. The proprietor gave me a sidelong look and 
ignored me. I said "Swordfish." He disappeared into a back room and came back with a tall, silent 
man with dark skin and a white turban. The Indian glanced at my head and said, "I am Akbar, 
honourable sir. What is it that you wish?" I gave him the garnet.

He looked at it closely. "I must ask you to come with me, sir. But first, I ask that you give me 
all that you carry." He took everything, apologised, and put a blindfold over my eyes, and led me 
down a long series of winding passages. A door closed behind me. When the blindfold came off I was 
standing in Moriarty's lair. Akbar locked the door, dropped the key on the desk, and piled my 
things on the floor. He had not taken my hat.

The Professor sat behind his desk and looked at me as if I were a botanical specimen. Holmes was 
tied to a chair a few inches from the Crown Jewels, which Moriarty had spread across the desk to 
taunt him. "Ah, there you are Watson," said Holmes. "You see, Professor, you grossly underestimate 
the intelligence and determination of your fellow countrymen."

I asked Moriarty about the jewels. "Pretty, are they not? I purloined them at the request of a 
foreign government whose identity need not concern you. My clients care nothing about the Crown 
Jewels themselves. They wish only to undermine confidence in the British monarchy as a first step 
towards weakening and ultimately destroying the British Empire."

I took off my hat. Akbar, disconcerted by such sacrilege, looked away, and I took out the ampoule. 
Moriarty's face dissolved in rage. "Why didn't you take that from him when you brought him in?" 
Akbar came at me with murder in his eyes. I took a deep breath, and Holmes, seeing me do it, 
quietly did the same. Then I broke the ampoule.

Everyone but Holmes and I dropped. I untied Holmes and kept the rope, and tied up Moriarty, and 
then Akbar, while my lungs screamed for air. At last I had to breathe, and the etherium took me.

XIV. NINE O'CLOCK

I woke slowly and staggered to my feet. Moriarty and Akbar had already come round and were 
struggling hard against the ropes. I had been out a long time. It was half past eight on Monday 
morning.

I took my whistle, Moriarty's key, and the Crown Jewels from the desk, unlocked the door and 
stepped out, blinking in the bright sun. I was standing on London Bridge with no idea which way I 
had come. I blew the whistle, and again, and a hansom cab pulled up beside me. "Where to, guv'nor?"

"Buckingham Palace."

"Right-o."

The cab weaved through the Jubilee traffic, and a few minutes later we stopped outside the gate in 
Queens Gardens. It was six minutes to nine. I gave the Regalia to the Palace guard. He looked at it 
closely and went inside, and a moment later he came back with the Prime Minister, who hurried us 
into the presence of the Queen.

For a few moments she said nothing. Then she smiled. "Once again, Mr Holmes, we find ourselves in 
your debt. Until now you have always refused our offers of gratitude. Do you intend to refuse your 
Queen yet again?"

Holmes smiled back. "I do, Your Majesty. But only because your thanks would be misdirected. It is 
Doctor Watson here who deserves your gratitude."

"Indeed? And what reward would you ask of us, Doctor?"

I stammered something about the service being its own reward, and before I knew it I had been 
royally thanked and shown back out into Queens Gardens. There Holmes and I stood among the cheering 
crowds when, at nine o'clock exactly, the Queen stepped onto the balcony, wearing the full Regalia, 
to begin the Coronation Day festivities.

Moriarty had said the clock was my enemy. It had been a near thing, but this time the clock was on 
my side.
