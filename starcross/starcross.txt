STARCROSS
A prospector's log, more or less

The alarm got me out of a dream I'd been enjoying, which is how alarms always seem to work. Not the
fire alarm, not the hull breach alarm: the mass detector. On a ship called the Starcross, out of
Ceres, with a hold full of nothing and a repair bill I couldn't pay, that bell is the sweetest sound
there is.

I rolled out of the bunk, went starboard to the Bridge, and slapped the red button to shut it up.
The screen said "mass UM28." I looked up the numbers in the registry and sat down in the control
couch like a man who'd done this a thousand times. Strap in first. You learn that early, or you
don't learn anything after.

"Computer, range is 250." "Computer, theta is 45." "Computer, phi is 178." "Computer, confirm."

The burn pressed me into the couch. Then there was nothing to do but wait, so I waited.

The computer said "Program completed" in its flat little voice, and I very nearly unbuckled. I'm
glad I didn't. Out the viewport something rose over the horizon that was not a black hole and not
a rock. It was a cylinder, kilometers long, studded with domes: a blue one sliding past, then a
yellow, then a green. Then a red dome, and out of the red dome came a tentacle as thick as my ship,
and it took hold of the Starcross the way you'd pick up a cup.

The ship shuddered and went still. The word "up" didn't mean what it used to. It meant the center
of that thing now.

I've never been so careful putting on a space suit. I got it and the safety line out of Storage,
clipped the line to my suit, cycled the airlock and stepped out onto the Red Dock. My magnetic boots
caught the hull of the artifact and held.

There was a relief carved into the metal beside the airlock door: a row of bumps, one big one and a
string of smaller ones running away from it. It took me longer than I'd like to admit to see it.
The big one was the Sun. The fourth bump, small and blue-ish in my imagination, was Earth. I pushed
it. I pushed the tiny column beside it. The whole sculpture flattened, and where Earth had been, a
hexagonal rod of black crystal slid out into my glove. When I took it, the airlock door opened.

Somebody had built a lock that only opened for people who knew where they lived. I thought about
that all the way up the ladder.

---

Inside, the artifact was dim and tired. The Red Hall had planters full of wilted plants and lights
that seemed to be worn out rather than off. Corridors ran off in every direction, some of them
curving away in rings, and the names I'd been raised with (north, south, port, starboard) only
half applied. I got lost twice in the first hour.

I found a computer room with a mainframe thirty meters high and a slot in its access panel, empty,
waiting for a card I didn't have. I found a zoo where most of the cages had gone dark, their
occupants dried to husks behind bars of shimmering force. One sign, chewed and ruined, marked the
cage of a grue. I did not go far inside that cage.

And I found the rat-ants.

They're about the size of rats, with shells like ants, pincers, little tufts of hair, and spears.
Tiny, serious spears. Their nest filled a corner of the cage, built from every scrap of junk they
could drag in. Stuck in the mud by one of the entrances was a red crystal rod, the same shape as my
black one.

Here is the part of this log I would rather leave out. I thought they might trade. I held out my
black rod, very politely, and a rat-ant took it and built it into the nest. I tried my space suit.
They took that too. My safety line. Same. Every time I reached for anything, a head popped out of
the mud and snapped at my fingers with needle-sharp mandibles. In ten minutes I had turned myself
from a prospector into an exhibit.

The answer, when it came, wasn't clever. In a wrecked armory off a corridor whose walls had melted
like candle wax, I found a ray gun: the heavy-duty model, with a long ugly barrel and a charge
indicator nobody could read. I carried it back to the Nesting Cage, and I threw it at the nest.

The nest smashed into fragments. The rat-ants froze, and then all of them at once began carrying
their babies across the cage to start again. For a few seconds everything I owned lay in the
rubble: suit, line, black rod, red rod, the gun. I have never picked things up so fast. Behind me
the rat-ants finished their new nest and settled down to rest, leaving a few guards to glare at me.
I didn't blame them.

---

Out in the cylinder's great open interior, where grassland curves up and over your head and a herd
of something like unicorns grazes on the ceiling, I found a hatch hidden among the shrubs. Below it
was a Repair Room with two machines. One had a picture of rays and a yellow slot. The other had
three diagrams with a red slot under each: four dots around six, two clusters of eight, three dots
around seven.

I'm a prospector, not a chemist, but I had enough school to know oxygen when I saw it. I put the
red rod in the second slot, and somewhere machinery hummed back to life. Half an hour later I
cracked my helmet and breathed air that was, as I wrote down at the time, "quite breathable and
slightly sweet." I'm told the other two slots would have given me methane or ammonia. I'm glad I
paid attention in class.

The metal and ceramic square from the repair room turned out to be the missing card. The computer's
switch was on, and I very nearly fed the card in like that. I'd smelled burning electronics before,
so I turned the power off first, slid the card home, and threw the switch. FOOOM. The main display
blinked twice, a bell rang, and a gold rod dropped out of the output hopper at my feet, as if the
whole artifact had been waiting for someone to do this one thing right.

A little maintenance robot shaped like a mouse hummed past me, polishing the floor and twitching
its dish-antenna ears, looking for garbage. It was the happiest thing I'd seen in days.

---

The gun rattled when I shook it. Down the barrel sat a silver rod. I don't know who keeps a key
inside a weapon, but I took it out before I ever pulled the trigger, and I'm glad. I found out later
the first shot would have turned it to dust.

At the aft end of the cylinder a tree grows that would make a sequoia blush. The bark is rough
enough to climb, but not in a space suit, so I left mine at its roots. The higher I climbed the less
I weighed, until at the top I simply jumped and floated up to the hatch of the drive bubble. The
silver rod opened it. A white rod hung in the air inside, next to a slot.

There was also a black slot in that room, ringed with a dead-black circle that seemed to drink the
light. I had a black rod in my pocket. I didn't put it in. Some doors you can tell from across the
room are marked "off."

From the drive bubble, the control bubble at the far end of the axis looked close enough to touch.
There's no gravity on the axis and nothing to push against, so I pushed off from the bubble and
fired the ray gun behind me. The recoil sent me sailing.

I fired again. I fired a third time, carelessly, and the shot pushed me off the axis. Air resistance
and spin took hold, and I fell in what was probably a lovely arc onto the ground.

I remember the impact. Then I remember a voice that wasn't a voice, inside my head: "This is not
promising. The candidate does not deserve another chance, but the instructions are explicit." A
pallet. Metallic threads. A feeling of being taken apart and put back together. I woke up in the
Red Hall with a mouse polishing the floor beside me.

So that's what this place is. It isn't a ship that lost its crew. It's a test, and I am the
candidate, and somebody very old and very patient is keeping score.

The second time I aimed. Every shot went at the drive bubble behind me, and every shot pushed me
straight down the axis toward the fore end, until I caught the knobs of the control bubble and
pulled myself down to its hatch. The gold rod fit the gold slot. The hatch opened.

---

I'm writing this inside the control bubble. Through the transparent walls I can see the whole
interior of the artifact: the grassland, the tree, the tiny drive bubble at the far end. The walls
in here are gray and blank, except for one small slot surrounded by clear crystal.

There's a clear rod somewhere, in an observatory, they say, inside a projector of blinding light.
There are more colors still out there: brown, green, blue, violet, pink, yellow. Some are hanging
around a weasel chieftain's neck, some are in the hands of a giant spider bored nearly out of his
mind, and one is locked in a silver globe I haven't figured out yet.

My repair bill is still waiting back on Ceres. I find I don't much care.

The candidate is going to need another rod.
