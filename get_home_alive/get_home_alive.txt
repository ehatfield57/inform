GET HOME ALIVE
Four days in a lifeboat built for two

I wasn't supposed to be on Apollo 13 at all.

I was the backup. Ken Mattingly had trained for two years to fly the command module, and three days
before launch the doctors found out he'd been exposed to German measles, and they grounded him. So
they put me in his seat. Ken took it like a gentleman. I have never felt so lucky and so guilty at
the same time.

That's how I came to be floating in the center couch of the command module Odyssey on the evening
of April 13, 1970, two hundred thousand miles from home, on our way to land Jim Lovell and Fred
Haise on the Moon at a place called Fra Mauro.

We had just finished a television broadcast. Jim gave the tour, Fred played some music, and I did
my best to look like I'd been there all along. We found out later that none of the networks had
bothered to show it. Going to the Moon had become routine. People had seen it before.

"Thirteen, Houston," said the radio. "We've got one more item for you, when you get a chance. We'd
like you to stir up your cryo tanks."

The cryo tanks hold the supercold oxygen in the service module, the big cylinder behind us with our
engine and our fuel cells and nearly everything that keeps us alive. Every so often you switch on
little fans inside the tanks to stir the oxygen, so the gauges read true. I'd done it a dozen
times. The switches were right by my right hand.

I flipped them.

---

There was a bang. Not loud, the way a bang is on Earth, but deep, a thud that went through the
whole spacecraft and into my bones. The cabin lurched. Then every warning light on the panel came
on at once, and the master alarm howled in my headset.

Jim came diving down the tunnel from Aquarius, the lunar module, and Fred swung into the right-hand
couch, and for a second the three of us just looked at the panel.

MAIN BUS B UNDERVOLT.

Somebody had to tell Houston. It was me.

"Okay, Houston," I said. "We've had a problem here." And then, because it was the only thing I was
sure of: "We've had a Main B Bus undervolt."

"Roger," said Houston. "Main B undervolt." His voice was perfectly calm. I could hear, behind it, a
whole room full of people starting to look at their screens at once.

Out of the corner of my eye, through the hatch window, I saw something strange. I floated over and
looked out. Something was streaming away from the back of the spacecraft: a glittering white cloud,
spreading slowly out against the black like the tail of a comet.

"We are venting something out into the..." Jim said, "into space. It's a gas of some sort."

There was a long silence on the radio. We all knew what it was. It was our oxygen. Oxygen tank 2
read zero. Tank 1 was falling. Two of our three fuel cells were already dead, and the fuel cells
needed that oxygen to make our electricity and our water.

When Houston came back, the voice was still calm. "Thirteen, it looks like you're losing oxygen
from tank 1 too. Odyssey's got maybe an hour left. We want you to power up Aquarius and use her as
a lifeboat."

The lunar module. The little spidery lander that was supposed to take Jim and Fred down to the Moon
and back while I waited in orbit. It was built to keep two men alive for two days. There were three
of us, and we were four days from home.

"And before you power down Odyssey," said Houston, "read the gimbal angles off her computer so you
can type them into Aquarius."

---

This is the part nobody thinks about. A spacecraft's guidance platform knows which way the
spacecraft is pointing, and in empty space there isn't any other way to know. If Odyssey went dark
with that knowledge still inside her, it would be gone, and Aquarius would wake up blind.

I looked at the DSKY, the computer's keypad and display, and read off the three gimbal angles. 312.
47. 5.

"Write those down," said Jim, "or remember them."

I said them over and over, like a phone number you're afraid of forgetting on the way to the
telephone. Three hundred twelve. Forty-seven. Five.

Then up through the hatch and the narrow docking tunnel to Aquarius. There are no seats in a lunar
module. You stand at the controls, held in by straps, in front of two triangular windows. Fred came
floating up behind me, and we went through the power-up checklist as fast as either of us has ever
done anything. Breakers in. Switches up. One by one the lights came on, the fans began to whirr,
and fresh oxygen hissed into the cabin. The lifeboat was awake.

I keyed in the angles on Aquarius's DSKY. 312, 47, 5. ENTER. The computer thought about it for a
moment, and then the attitude ball swung round to match Odyssey's.

"She's aligned," said Fred. "Now get down there and power Odyssey off, before her batteries are
gone."

I went back down the tunnel into Odyssey and threw her breakers one at a time. The fans spun down.
The lights faded. The cabin went silent and dark around me. Her batteries were all we would have to
bring us through the atmosphere, four days from now, and we couldn't spend one amp of them until
then. It felt like turning off life support on a friend.

The cabin was already starting to get cold.

---

We were not, at that point, on our way home. We were on our way to the Moon, on a course that would
have looped us past it and off into space forever. We needed a burn of Aquarius's descent engine,
the one that was supposed to land Jim and Fred on Fra Mauro, to put us on what the flight people
call a free-return trajectory: around the back of the Moon and home again.

I armed the engine. I pressed START. Aquarius shuddered and roared, and Jim held her steady, and
thirty-four seconds later the engine shut down.

"Burn complete," said Houston. "Thirteen, you're on a free return. You're coming home."

Hours later, Aquarius swung round behind the Moon, and for half an hour we were out of radio
contact with every other human being in the universe. The far side went by underneath us, grey and
cratered and close enough to touch. We were higher above the Earth than any people had ever been
before, and that record stood for more than fifty years. Jim and Fred took pictures. I didn't feel
much like it. I had been about to orbit that Moon.

When we came round, Houston wanted another burn, a long one, four minutes and twenty-three seconds,
to get us home a day sooner. They call it PC+2: two hours after pericynthion, the closest point to
the Moon. I armed it and pressed START, and that engine burned for the longest four minutes of my
life.

"Good burn, Aquarius," said Houston. "You'll be home a day sooner."

---

Then a warning light came on, and a horn sounded in our headsets.

"CO2," said Fred, tapping the gauge. "Carbon dioxide's climbing."

Every breath you breathe out is carbon dioxide. In a spacecraft, canisters of a chemical called
lithium hydroxide soak it out of the air. Aquarius's canisters were made to keep two men alive for
two days, not three men for four. Odyssey had plenty of spare canisters, stacked in her lockers.
But Odyssey's canisters were square, and Aquarius's sockets were round.

"Houston," said Jim, "we've got a square peg and a round hole."

"We're working on it, Aquarius."

They were. Somewhere in Houston, a room full of engineers had dumped out on a table everything we
had on board, and they were building something out of it. A few minutes later, or a few hours, I've
never been sure, the radio came back.

"Okay, Aquarius, copy this down. You'll need one of Odyssey's square canisters, a plastic bag, the
cardboard cover off your flight plan, the hose from a suit, and the duct tape."

The duct tape. Every spacecraft should carry a roll.

I went down the tunnel into Odyssey's lower equipment bay, which was dark and freezing now, with
water beading on the walls. I took a square canister and the big gray roll of duct tape and a
plastic stowage bag, and I slid the canister into the bag. I tore the stiff cardboard cover off my
flight plan, hundreds of pages about landing at Fra Mauro that none of us would ever use, bent it
into a curve and pushed it into the bag so the plastic couldn't collapse against the canister. I
wound duct tape around and around the whole thing until it was sealed. Then I took the hose off my
spacesuit in the sleep station, cut a slit in the bag, pushed the hose in, and taped it tight.

It looked like a lumpy gray mailbox. That's what we called it: the mailbox. It was the ugliest
thing I've ever seen.

Back in Aquarius, I taped the end of the hose onto the air outlet, and the fans began to pull the
cabin air through the mailbox, through Odyssey's square canister, and back out again. We all
watched the gauge. For a long minute nothing happened at all.

Then the needle started to fall.

"Houston," said Jim, "it's working." We could hear them cheering, all the way from Texas.

---

After that it got cold. To save power, almost everything on board was switched off, and Aquarius
got colder and colder, and Odyssey, sitting dark below us, got colder than that. Thirty-eight
degrees. We couldn't sleep for the cold. Fred came down with a fever and lay shivering in his
sleep. Water ran down the windows. We ate cold food and rationed our water, and at some point in
all of this I remembered that I hadn't filed my income tax, which was due on April 15th, and told
Houston so. They laughed for a long time. Later they told me I'd been given an extension.

On the third day Houston called again. We were drifting off course. If we came in too steep we'd
burn up; too shallow, and we'd skip off the atmosphere like a stone across a pond and never come
back. We needed a short correction burn, fourteen seconds. But Aquarius's computer was switched off
to save power, so there was nothing to aim it.

"You'll have to do it by hand," said Houston. "Keep the Earth's terminator, the line between day
and night, on the crosshairs of the alignment sight. Time the burn with a watch."

So that's what we did, three men in a freezing tin can a hundred thousand miles from home, steering
by eye like sailors. I looked through the sight while Jim worked the hand controller. The Earth
swam into view, half in sunlight and half in shadow, and slowly Jim nudged Aquarius round until the
terminator sat right on the crosshairs.

"Got it," said Jim. "Hold it there. Jack, you time it."

I started the stopwatch on my Omega. NASA had tested those watches to destruction, and in that
moment it was the best clock on board. Fred armed the engine. I pressed START, and the engine
kicked, and I stared at the second hand while Jim held the Earth on the line.

Four seconds. Six. Eight. Ten. Twelve.

Fourteen.

"Stop!" I hit the button. The engine cut out.

There were long seconds of silence while Houston checked the tracking. Then: "Aquarius, that was a
good burn. You're right in the corridor."

Jim let out a breath I don't think he knew he'd been holding.

---

The Earth grew in the window every hour, until it filled it.

Odyssey had to come back to life for reentry. She had been dark and frozen for three days, and her
batteries were low, and nobody had ever powered up a command module like this. Back in Houston, Ken
Mattingly, the man whose seat I was sitting in, had been in the simulator for days working out a
power-up procedure, to the last amp. He still hadn't come down with measles. He never did.

"Okay, Odyssey," said Houston. "First, plug in the power cable in the tunnel, so Aquarius can
charge her batteries. Then switch on the main bus, and then the guidance computer. Nothing else,
Jack. I know it's cold. No heaters. If you go over the amps, there won't be enough left for the
parachutes."

I plugged in the cable, and the lifeboat fed the mother ship a trickle of power. When the batteries
were charged I went down into Odyssey's dark cabin, with my breath steaming, and switched on the
main bus. The panel flickered to life for the first time in three days. Water droplets fizzed on
the switches, and every one of us was waiting for something to short. Nothing did.

The heater switch was right there. I was so cold. I didn't touch it.

I switched on the guidance computer, and it hummed awake, and the DSKY lit up green. Odyssey was
alive.

"Odyssey, Houston," said the radio. "Power-up looks good. Beautiful work, Jack."

---

Then it was time to let go of the service module, the great cylinder that had carried us all this
way. I took the camera, because the engineers wanted pictures, and pressed SM JETTISON, and with a
thud the service module drifted slowly away from us, turning.

When it turned, we saw it.

One whole side was gone. A panel the full length of the spacecraft had been blown clean off, and
inside there was nothing but a tangle of scorched wires and twisted metal. I took the pictures with
my hands shaking.

"There's one whole side of that spacecraft missing," said Jim, very quietly.

The heat shield was right next to that damage. None of us said it, but all of us were thinking it.
If it was cracked, we would know in about an hour.

Then we said goodbye to Aquarius. Everybody into Odyssey. I closed the hatch and checked it twice.
Then I pressed LM JETTISON, and with a soft bang Aquarius drifted away from us, her job done, a
little lander that never landed, shrinking against the black.

"Farewell, Aquarius," said Jim, "and we thank you."

---

Odyssey fell toward the Earth. Through the window, the sky turned from black to pink to blazing
orange as the heat shield bit into the atmosphere at twenty-five thousand miles an hour. The radio
dissolved into static.

"Blackout," said Jim. "Three minutes, normally."

Three minutes passed. Four. Nobody spoke. Five minutes. Six. In Mission Control, they told me
later, nobody moved. Nobody even breathed. I sat there in my couch and listened to the static and
thought about the heat shield next to that blown-out panel.

"Odyssey, Houston, standing by, over," said the radio.

And through the static I heard a voice say, "Okay, Joe."

It was mine. I didn't even remember deciding to say it.

They tell me Mission Control erupted.

Bang! The drogue chutes snapped open, and then the three great orange and white main parachutes
blossomed above us, and Odyssey swung gently beneath them like a cradle. We hit the Pacific with a
thump, rolled over, and bobbed upright. Through the window I saw blue sky, real blue sky, and the
helicopters coming out from the USS Iwo Jima.

Jim grinned at me. Fred, still shivering, started to laugh, and then we were all laughing, three
men who smelled terrible and were very, very cold.

We never got to the Moon. NASA calls Apollo 13 a successful failure. But a whole room of people in
Houston, and a man in a simulator who should have been in my seat, and a roll of duct tape, and an
Omega watch, and a little spidery lander named Aquarius all did their jobs, and we got home alive.
