ZORK II
The Wizard of Frobozz

The last thing I remember of the white house is the trophy case, full at last, and a voice that
said I had earned the right to go further. Then a path opened in the forest that had never been
there before, and at the end of it was a barrow, an ancient burial mound, with its door standing
open.

Inside, on the floor, lay an elvish sword, and a brass lantern that looked strangely familiar. Both
of them, I swear, were mine. I had left them in a trophy case a moment before. I didn't ask. In the
Great Underground Empire you learn not to ask. I picked them up, and walked south into the dark,
and that is how I came to be the guest of the Wizard of Frobozz.

---

The tunnel opened into a great cavern lit by glowing moss, with a ravine and a footbridge, and a
ford across a stream, and beyond that, in the dark, I lit my lamp and found the strangest thing
yet: a garden. A formal garden, under the ground, with clipped hedges and rosebeds and gravel
paths, lit by moss on the cavern roof so that if you didn't look up you'd think it was a cloudy
afternoon in some lord's country house. In the middle of the roses stood a little white gazebo, and
in the gazebo was a tea table, set for tea: a china teapot, a place mat, a newspaper, a letter
opener and a matchbook that said "Visit ZORK I".

And at the north end of the garden, peacefully cropping the grass, was a unicorn, with something
hanging around its neck. It looked at me, and bounded lightly away.

I took the teapot. I filled it at the stream. It was the first sensible thing I did in that place,
and I didn't know yet why.

---

South of the garden, past a hedge clipped into the shapes of animals, was the Carousel Room.

I don't know how to describe the Carousel Room except to say that the whole room was spinning. You
couldn't see it. You could hear it, a loud whirring from everywhere at once, and you could feel it,
a seasick sense that north was not quite where north had been a moment ago. Eight passages led out
of it, and none of them went where you meant. I tried for the southeast passage and walked out into
a marble hall. I came back and tried again and found myself in a cool, misty room. I came back. A
path by a stream. I came back. On my fourth try I walked out into a bare room with a great stone
door, and above the door was carved a riddle.

"No man shall pass this door without solving this riddle:

What is tall as a house, round as a cup, and all the king's horses can't draw it up?"

I knew that one. My grandmother used to say it. "A well," I said, and there was a clap of thunder
like the end of the world, and the door swung quietly open.

Beyond it, in a former broom closet, lay a pearl necklace with hundreds of large pearls. Beyond
that was a circular room of brick, with a big wooden bucket in it, three feet across, at the bottom
of what was plainly a well. I got into the bucket and poured my teapot of water into it, and the
bucket rose, slowly, all the way to the top.

And that is where I first met the Wizard.

---

He appeared beside me in midair, floating nonchalantly: a strange little man in a long cloak, with
a high pointed hat embroidered with astrological signs and a long, stringy, unkempt beard. He
grinned at me. He drew out his wand. He waved it in my direction, and it began to glow, and in a
deep and resonant voice he spoke one word.

"Freeze!"

My limbs turned to stone. I couldn't move a finger. The Wizard chuckled and vanished, and I stood
frozen in a bucket at the top of a well for what felt like a week, until my little finger began to
twitch, and then the rest of me, and I climbed out and went on.

That was how it went, all the way through. The Wizard of Frobozz had once been the greatest wizard
of the Empire, and was now, I gathered, mostly retired, and what he did with his retirement was pop
up at the worst possible moments and cast spells that began with F. "Fierce!", and I found myself
rushing at him to tear him limb from limb, and he stepped politely aside. "Fireproof!", which did
nothing I could notice. He seemed to forget, half the time, what spell he'd meant to cast. Once he
started to say something, thought better of it, and peered at me instead. He was annoying, and he
was dangerous, and I am sorry to say that I came to rather like him.

---

At the top of the well was a tea room, and on the table were four cakes, iced with letters in red,
orange, blue and green. I'd read the same book you have. I took the red, the blue and the green,
and left the orange alone, and ate the green one.

The room grew enormous around me. I was standing among four wooden posts, under a vast table. To
the east was a pool of tears, salty water leaking from somewhere, and floating in it was a glass
flask marked with a skull and crossbones, which I let be. I threw the red cake into the pool, and
the pool evaporated, revealing a slightly damp but still valuable package of candied goodies. I
took them. Then I ate the blue cake, and grew back to my right size with a feeling I'd rather not
describe, and went northwest into a low room.

There was a robot in the low room. It said "Whirr, buzz, click!" when I spoke to it, which I took
to mean yes. There were instructions for it on a green piece of paper. I sent it east into a
machine room, where a triangular button sat on a panel, and told it to push the button, and
somewhere in the distance there was a dull thump, and the whirring of the Carousel Room stopped
forever.

In a dingy closet to the south, on a pedestal, sat a red crystal sphere, and a sign on the wall
said "Protected by FROBOZZ Magic Alarm Company. (Hello, footpad!)". I reached for the sphere and a
steel cage dropped from the ceiling over me. I had expected that, more or less. "Robot," I said,
"lift the cage." And the robot picked it up and hurled it across the room, and I took the sphere. I
think the robot was showing off.

---

I'll spare you some of it. A fancy violin in a dented steel box, in the carousel room, now that it
held still. A key, behind a locked door in a tiny room above the ravine: I slid the place mat from
the tea table under the door, and pushed the key out of its keyhole with the letter opener so that
it dropped onto the mat, and pulled the mat back with the key on it, a trick I'd once read in a
mystery novel. Behind the door, in a dreary room lit by a red glow, a blue crystal sphere.

And a dragon.

He was lying across the entrance to his lair, a huge red dragon, and he looked at me with smoky
yellow eyes. I hit him with my elvish sword, which did no damage at all but got his attention. Then
I walked away, south, over the stone bridge, and he followed me, out of mingled curiosity and
anger. I hit him again. He followed me again. I led him into a hall of ancient lava whose western
half was filled with a mountain of ice, and as he came in he saw his own reflection in the glacier,
and roared at it, and breathed fire at it.

The ice melted all at once. A torrent of water came down from the glacier and swept everything
away. When the flood was over, there was no trace of the dragon anywhere, except, later, a huge
dead body lying across a stream far below. Beyond the melted ice lay a moby ruby the size of my
fist.

---

In the heart of an old volcano there was a balloon: a huge wicker basket and an enormous cloth bag,
and under the bag a little receptacle for fuel. I put the newspaper from the tea table in it and
lit it with a match from the Zork I matchbook, and the hot air filled the bag, and we rose up
through the volcano, past ledges and caves, toward a small circle of sky.

At a narrow ledge I tied the balloon to a hook in the rock. There was a gold zorkmid on the ledge,
a priceless coin, and beyond it a ruined royal library where, inside a moldy purple book, someone
had pressed a Flathead stamp. At a wide ledge higher up I tied up again, and in a dusty room there
was a rusty old safe set into the wall. I had a brick of clay that I'd picked up in the marble
hall, and a coil of black string I'd found in a cobwebby corridor, and you will see where this is
going. I stuck the string into the brick for a fuse, put the brick in the safe's keyhole, lit the
string, and stepped outside. There was an explosion. Inside the safe was the excessively gaudy
crown of Lord Dimwit Flathead, which I put on, because I could.

The first time I went up in that balloon I let it rise one minute too long, and it floated out of
the top of the volcano, and I had a breathtaking view of the mountains for a moment, just before I
died. I prefer not to talk about that. You get another chance in the Empire, sometimes. I took it,
and I counted the minutes more carefully.

---

In the dragon's lair, among the debris, was a rotten wooden chest, and a young woman in a dirty and
bedraggled gown, sitting on a rock as if she'd been asleep for years. She was a princess. They
always are. I kissed her, which seemed to be expected, and she shook herself awake and noticed me,
and then she noticed that the dragon was gone.

I went back to the gazebo, and a little while later the princess came and joined me there. We
waited. And shyly, out of the hedges, the unicorn peeked out. It saw the princess and came to her,
and she took the red satin ribbon from around its neck, and the delicate gold key that hung on it,
and she tied up her hair with the ribbon, and then she smiled at me and handed me the key and a
rose that she plucked from the arbor. "You may have use of such a thing," she said. "It is the
least I can do for one who rescued me." Then she climbed onto the unicorn, side-saddle, of course,
and rode off into the gloom.

The rotten chest, when I forced it open, held a golden statuette of a dragon. I hope he would have
liked that.

---

The gold key opened a door guarded by a lizard. Not a lizard, exactly: a lizard's head, set into
the door, with sharp teeth and beady eyes, that snapped at anyone who came near. I gave it the
candied goodies from the pool of tears. It wolfed them down, package and all, and fell asleep, and
I unlocked the door.

It was the Wizard's Workshop. His home. Dark corridors, a workroom with a bench covered in arcane
junk, and three empty stands in a triangle, each made to hold a sphere: one ruby, one sapphire, one
diamond.

The diamond sphere was in an aquarium in the next room, inside a baby sea serpent's tank. I broke
the glass with a wooden club I'd found in the oddly-angled rooms below the menhir, rooms whose
walls were never at the angles they should be, where you had to walk around them in a particular
pattern, like running the bases, before a floor swung open somewhere. The aquarium shattered. The
serpent died mere inches from my foot. I took the clear sphere and put all three in their stands,
white, red and blue. They began to hum, and vibrate, faster and faster, and then there was only one
sphere on the bench, and it was black.

I carried the black sphere into the next room, where a great pentagram was drawn on the floor in
black, and set it in the circle. A cold wind blew out from it. The candles flickered. And a large
and rather formidable demon rose up and floated in midair before me.

---

The demon would do me a great service, he said. And great services are bought at a great price.

I had robbed a bank for him already, as it happens. The Bank of Zork, deep in the Empire, where the
walls of the safety depository were curtains of shimmering light that you could step through, and
where they still kept two hundred neatly stacked zorkmid bills in a vault with no doors, and a
portrait of J. Pierpont Flathead in the chairman's office. The trick was to walk in and out through
the light in exactly the right order, so that you came out in a viewing room with the money instead
of locked in a vault forever. I had done it. I am not proud of it.

So I gave the demon everything. The pearls and the golden dragon. The gold key. The portrait. The
bills. "Almost halfway there, oh worthy one!" Then I walked all the way back to the gazebo, past
the topiary and the carousel that no longer spun, and fetched the rest: the violin, the ruby, the
gold zorkmid, the stamp, and Lord Dimwit's crown off my own head, and gave him all of those too.

The Wizard had appeared somewhere in the middle of this, and was standing in the corner watching
me, eyeing me warily, tearing at his beard.

"Demon," I said, "give me the wand."

"I hear and obey!" said the demon. He stretched out an enormous hand toward the Wizard. The poor
old man didn't know what to do. He pointed his wand at the demon, then at me, then back at the
demon, and cried "Fudge!", and there was a strong smell of chocolate in the air, and nothing else
at all. The demon plucked the wand out of his fingers, and handed it to me, and vanished.

I have thought since that I owed the Wizard an apology. He had never done me any real harm. He had
frozen me, and made me fierce, and made me fireproof, and in the end he had made the air smell of
chocolate. I hope wherever he is now he has found another wand.

---

In the menhir room, a single huge standing stone blocked a passage to the southwest. I waved the
wand at it, and the wand grew warm, and I said "Float." The menhir rose majestically into the air,
about ten feet, and hung there. Beyond it was an old kennel, and in the kennel was a dog collar
large enough for three rhinoceros-sized dogs.

I found out why down the stairs, below the oddly-angled rooms, at the entrance to a crypt. There
was a dog guarding it. It was more or less the size of a house, and it had three heads, and each of
them was looking at me. Cerberus. I am told that people in the old stories sing to him, or drug him
with honey cakes. I put the collar on him. And the creature whined happily, and the center head
licked my face, and the other two looked a little jealous.

Beyond him was the crypt of the Flatheads, twelve kings in their tombs, and it was empty of
treasure, of course, because someone had already been there and taken everything. Someone like me.

I put out my lamp. In the dark, on the south wall, there was the faint outline of a rectangle, as
though light were coming through the cracks of a door. I opened it. Beyond it, a rough staircase
led down into the darkness, and at the bottom of it, far below, a vast red-lit hall stretched away
into the distance, and I could feel something waiting down there for me.

The Wizard of Frobozz was behind me. Ahead was the Dungeon Master.

I went down the stairs.
