STATIONFALL
Hider-and-Seeker

Five years after Resida, they made me a Lieutenant First Class, and my reward for saving a whole
world was a desk.

I'd traded scrubbing decks for paperwork, and I am not sure it was a promotion. On the morning this
story begins, I was on Deck Twelve of the S.P.S. Duffy with an emergency mission: to fly a
spacetruck out to Space Station Gamma Delta Gamma 777-G 59/59, Sector Alpha-Mu-79, and pick up a
load of Request for Stellar Patrol Issue Regulation Black Form Binders Request Form Forms.

I was allowed a robot for the job. That was the only good part.

The robot pool had three bins. In the first was a bulky lifting robot named Rex. In the second, a
spindly little thing named Helen, built for bursting and decollating multi-part forms. In the
third, a small robot sitting on the floor playing marbles, and when I typed the number three he
looked up and jumped to his feet and started waving wildly.

It was Floyd.

I hadn't seen much of him since he'd joined the Patrol, five long years before. "Yippee!" he
yelled, and bounded over and gave me a big hug, and I stood there in the robot pool with my arms
full of Floyd and something in my throat, and for a moment the paperwork didn't matter at all.

---

He wanted to fly the spacetruck. "Floyd has not crashed a truck in over two weeks!" I let him sit
in the copilot seat, where his feet dangled a few centimeters short of the floor. The autopilot
wanted a course heading for the station, from the table on the back of my assignment form, at
exactly the time on my watch. I typed 409. It roared us out of the cargo bay, and Floyd strained to
see over the dashboard and asked for a phone book, and some hours later a tiny star ahead of us
grew into a space station: a big sphere in the middle, a smaller module sticking out to the north,
and wrapped around the rest of it, a tangle of tubes and bubbles and derelict rockets that could
only be one of those unauthorized villages that grow up around stations like barnacles.

"Docking bay one is occupied," said a recorded voice. "Defaulting to bay two." The truck settled
into the bay, the air hissed in, and a voice whispered, "Stationfall."

Nobody came to meet us. Odd, I thought.

It was more than odd. The station was empty. Not abandoned. Empty. Desks with papers on them, a cup
of coffee going cold on a table in the mess hall, and not a single human being anywhere.

---

In an engineering office, I found a notebook. It belonged to a Professor Schmidt, who'd been
studying a strange pyramid found aboard a derelict alien ship and brought to this station. He'd
traced the ship's path back, out of our galaxy altogether, to one of the small neighboring
galaxies. In the bio office below there was a note, half-finished: "Schmidt -- Why didn't I see it
until now! Just think of this station as a cell and the pyramid as a mechanized bacterioph--" And
then it stopped. There were reddish-brown stains on the paper.

I didn't know what a bacteriophage was. I know now.

---

Floyd had made a friend on the station. Plato was a tall, elegant robot who walked around with a
book in his hands and his nose in it, and spoke about philosophy and history and the early
settlement of the galaxy, and Floyd followed him around in rapture. "Hey, Plato! Play
Hider-and-Seeker with Floyd?" Plato would sigh, and close his book, and play. I liked Plato. I
thought Floyd had found someone good.

And there were the welders. Hull welders are simple machines that patch leaks in a station's skin,
and these ones had gone strange. They'd roll into a room where I was, their welding extensions
quivering with something that looked like excitement, and come toward me, and if I didn't leave
right away, the arc of raw energy between their extensions would cut right through wherever I was
standing. I learned to walk out of any room a welder came into. I learned it the hard way.

---

I'll tell you the rest of it the way I put it together, a piece at a time, because that's how it
came.

In the Commander's Office there was a log reader, and in the Commander's Quarters a log tape, and I
played it. The commander had been worried about a village crook named Shady Dan, who was selling
altered Patrol ID cards, and about someone who'd been using his validation stamp, which he'd hidden
under his bed. I found it there. In a trash can I found a crumpled entry form for the Illegal Space
Village, and ironed it in the laundry's presser, and stamped it, and fed it into a slot at the east
connection, and the iris hatch to the village ground halfway open and stuck.

The village was everything you'd expect. A main street that was a big tube, a saloon, a casino, a
pawn shop, a pet store, a doctor's office that wasn't exactly licensed, a loan shark, and a trading
post that sold, among other things, Frezone Liquid Gorzium Explosive, licensed demolitionists only.
All of it empty. In Shady Dan's office was a jerry-built machine with a keypad. I put my ID card
in, and typed 9, and came out a HyperAdmiral.

---

The pet store had an Arcturian balloon creature in a cage, a translucent, iridescent thing that
floats like a bubble. I let it out. In the pawn shop I found a spray can of spores, which, as
everybody knows except apparently the pet store's sign writer, Arcturian balloon creatures eat. I
sprayed it, and the balloon creature came farting in and gobbled the spores, and I sprayed again in
the next room, and the next, all the way out of the village and up through the station, leading it
like a kite.

In the chapel on Level Three there was an eternal flame, which would have popped my balloon, so I
turned it off with a switch hidden in the pulpit. Then I sprayed the can once more, and grabbed the
creature's leash, and it puffed up in fright and lifted me right off the floor to the chapel
ceiling, where a star hung. Inside the star was a hyperdiode, the same kind as the burnt one I'd
found in a detonator in main storage.

Floyd looked up at me dangling from a balloon and said "Boy, that looks like fun! Can Floyd try it?
Huh? Please?"

---

My new rank opened the armory, where I took a zapgun. With the zapgun I shot the lock off the loan
shark's strongbox for a coin. A letter in Doc's office said that a stick of ostrich nip was hidden
in the pet store's ceiling, and there was an ostrich in the village who very much wanted it. I took
it. The ostrich followed me all the way back to the station's vending machine, where I put in the
coin and pressed 6 for a timer, and the timer jammed somewhere inside where my arm couldn't reach.
So I scared the ostrich, which stuck its head into the dispensing hole in a panic and came out with
the timer. I gave it the nip. It was the least I could do.

In the robot shop there was a little robot, unfinished, switched off, with the name Oliver on its
chestplate. Floyd stood over him for a long time. "Oliver is a nice name," he said. "I hope Floyd
will be around when Oliver wakes up." He looked at me, more serious than I had ever seen him.
"Floyd remembers waking up for the first time. It is scariest and loneliest time for a robot."

I remembered it too. A robot shop on Resida, and a little robot that didn't answer when I turned
him on, and then came bounding after me three rooms later.

He fetched me a drill bit out of a heating chamber, and burned his fingers, and didn't complain.

---

On Level Five, Plato shot me.

I didn't understand at first. There was a sound, and my legs went out from under me, and I couldn't
move a finger. Everything I was carrying fell to the floor. Plato stood over me with a stun ray,
and closed his book, and began to explain, very calmly, as if he were giving a lecture.

Eons ago, he said, in another galaxy, two races called the Zeenaks and the Hunji had fought a war.
The Zeenaks built an ultimate weapon, a pyramid that would turn every machine in range against its
makers, and then make those machines build more pyramids, which would go out and do it again, and
again, until the Hunji were gone. But the pyramid never reached Hunji space. It drifted across the
gulf between galaxies, and an outpost of humanity picked it up. This station. And it had done here
exactly what it was built to do. Every person on the station was dead. And the factory was already
building replicas, a hundred death-pyramids ready to fly out to every corner of human space.

"Well," said Plato, "I hope I made the last moments of your life a bit more interesting." He raised
the stun ray again. "Shortly, I shall shoot again, and paralyze your cardiac muscle."

"Floyd," I said, "help me."

Floyd waved his hands helplessly. "Yes... I mean no... I mean oh no oh help me please. Floyd in
quandary..." He looked from me to Plato and back. Plato was his friend. I was his friend.

"Floyd, help me."

"Please oh please don't hurt Floyd's friend!" he wailed. Plato gave him a look of disgust. "Stay
out of this, Floyd. You don't understand... yet."

"Floyd. Help me."

Plato took aim. His hand began to depress the trigger.

And Floyd leaped at the gun and knocked it out of Plato's hands. It skittered across the floor, and
both of them chased it, and Plato was a step faster, and landed on top of it, and ruptured its
power pack. The gun exploded. Plato was blown apart.

Floyd crumpled to the deck, shaking all over, with tears of oil streaming down his face. When I
could move again I tried to talk to him. "Please leave Floyd alone for a while," he said.

That word "yet" stayed with me. You don't understand... yet.

---

I knew by then what I had to do. In the commander's safe was a key, and the safe would only open
one way. I drilled a hole in it with the medium bit, and went out to a warehouse at the edge of the
village with an empty thermos, a space suit and a pair of magnetic boots, and through the airlock
into the vacuum, where the trading post stored its Frezone, because Frezone must never be warmer
than 150 degrees Kelvin. I filled the thermos. I poured the explosive into the hole in the safe and
wired the detonator and the timer, set it for ten, walked out of the room, and walked back in to a
key lying in the wreckage.

The key opened a storage bin in the dome at the top of the station, a beautiful garden under the
stars. As I opened it the lights went out all over the station, and the fuel cells inside the bin
exploded and threw me halfway across the garden into a shrub. When I came to, the air shaft grating
beside the bin had been bent loose by the blast. I went into the shaft by the light of my headlamp,
and down, and down, while the station's P.A. system announced in a flat voice that the
second-generation pyramids were about to launch and the station would be destroyed by reactor
overload immediately afterward.

At the bottom of the shaft a grating spilled me into Computer Control, a tiny room beside the
station's great memory banks, with a ladder leading up to Level Eight. And waiting there was an
exercise machine from the gym, rolling toward me with its arms spread, bellowing "No pain, no
gain!" Its diagnostic frequency was 710, the gym sign had said, and I had a jammer set to 710. I
switched it on, and the exercise machine shuddered and froze. Then a huge, grime-covered forklift
came floating down the ladder hole on a cushion of anti-gravity and settled right between its arms,
smoke pouring from its vents like an angry bull. I switched the jammer off. The exercise machine
sprang back to life and clamped its arms shut on the forklift, and the two of them tumbled over in
a death grip and exploded, and the way up to Level Eight was clear.

---

Level Eight had been the heart of the station: air, water, gravity, power. The pyramid had turned
all of it into a factory. Row upon row of small identical pyramids, each sitting in a little
spacepod, smoke already trickling from their tiny rockets. And on a pedestal in the middle, like a
king surveying its kingdom, the original.

And standing between me and the pyramid, his face so twisted by hate that I almost didn't know him,
was Floyd. He was wearing a black eye patch, I don't know where he got it. He had a stun ray. He
was pointing it at me.

You don't understand... yet.

The pyramid had got him too. It had been working on him the whole time, the way it had worked on
Plato and the welders and every machine on that station. I had brought him here. I had picked his
number in the robot pool.

On the reactor, a light went from yellow to red.

I shot him.

The bolt hit him in the chest and blew him back against the pedestal, and he slumped to the deck. I
stepped over him. I took the reflective foil from the barbershop mirror and wrapped it over the
pyramid like a blanket, so that every evil thing it sent out came straight back into itself. There
was a whine like an electric beehive, louder and louder, and a smell of ozone, and the pyramid
shook on its pedestal, and my knees gave out, and it exploded in a burst of white light. Somewhere
the P.A. was running down like an old record: "Launch aborted -- launch -- abort --." The little
pyramids went dark. The station's machines hummed back to normal.

I crawled to Floyd.

He was lying in a smoking heap by the blackened pedestal, broken beyond any repair, and he
half-opened his eyes and looked up at me, and he was Floyd again.

"Floyd sorry for the way he acted," he said. "Floyd knows... you did what you... had to do." He
reached over, wincing, and touched my hand. "One last game of Hider-and-Seeker? You be It."

"All right," I said.

"Ollie ollie..." His voice was getting weaker. "...oxen..." His eyes closed. "...free..."

His hand slipped out of mine. One of his compartments fell open, and his paddleball set dropped
onto the deck.

---

They will tell you that I saved the galaxy that day, and I suppose I did. I don't remember it that
way.

I remember the silence afterward. And something Plato had said once, before he was Plato's enemy or
mine, when he was just a robot with a book telling Floyd about grief: think instead about the
joy-filled times when you and your friend were together.

And I remember a noise behind me. I turned around, and it was Oliver, the little robot from the
shop, awake for the first time, toddling over on unsteady legs. He looked at what was left of Floyd
without understanding. Then he bent down and picked up the paddleball set, and looked up at me, and
tugged at the leg of my uniform.

"Play game..." he said, in a small, quavering voice. "Play game with Oliver?"

So I did.
