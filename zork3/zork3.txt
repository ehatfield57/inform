ZORK III
The Dungeon Master

At the bottom of the endless stair, the brass lantern was waiting for me again, at my feet, like an
old dog that had found its own way home. I had carried it out of a white house, through the realm
of the thief and the troll, through the Wizard's garden and his workshop. I didn't ask how it had
got down the stairs ahead of me. I picked it up, lit it, and went south into the dark, into the
last and deepest part of the Great Underground Empire.

I didn't know then that I was being watched, the whole way, by an old man. I want to tell you about
him first, because he is the whole story.

---

I met him in a room whose north wall was carved all over with runes in a language I didn't know. He
was huddled asleep in the corner, old and wizened and frail, snoring loudly. I had a loaf of fresh
bread, which I'd found lying under a tree at the edge of a cliff where somebody, it seemed, had
recently been. I woke him up.

He peered at me with eyes that looked much younger and stronger than the rest of him, and waited,
as if he expected something to happen. So I gave him the bread. He ate it slowly, and when he'd
finished, he said, "Perhaps what you seek is through there!" and pointed at the carved wall, where
I now saw the bare outline of a door. When I turned back to thank him, he was gone.

---

South of there lay a lake, ice cold, glowing faintly from above. I had to leave my lantern on the
shore. I said goodbye to it. I didn't see it again. Then I jumped in.

I can't tell you how cold that water was. It took the breath out of me and wouldn't give it back. I
dived, and on the sandy bottom, among a few hardy plants, something sparkled: a simple golden
amulet. I took it and came up gasping, with the voice in my head saying the icy waters were taking
their toll and I would not be able to hold out much longer, and I swam for the western shore.

There, in a little chamber, was the Scenic Vista, which was not scenic at all. It was a bare stone
room with a table fixed to the wall, angled toward you like the ones in parks that point out the
mountains, and above the table, an indicator with a Roman numeral that changed every few minutes:
I, II, III, IV. On the wall burned a torch. I took it, and waited for the numeral to read II, and
touched the table.

I was somewhere else. A small chamber with a number 8 chiseled on the wall, and a loud whirring
through the rock: the room off the Wizard's carousel, back in his domain, years away and miles
away. In the corner lay a can of Frobozz Magic Grue Repellent. I took it, and the moment I tried to
leave, a fierce wind blew me back into the Scenic Vista. When the numeral read III I touched the
table again and found myself in a damp passage I didn't know yet. I put down the torch there. Then
I was pulled back.

I want you to notice that. I left a torch in a place I had never been, for a version of myself who
would get there later. It was the first time in that dungeon that I did something without knowing
why, but it was not the last.

---

I jumped back into the lake to cross it, and of course I dropped the grue repellent in the shock of
the cold, and of course I had to dive for it. On the south shore a passage climbed into blackness.
I sprayed the repellent on myself. It smelled like a mixture of old socks and burning rubber. In
the dark, all around me, there were sinister gurgling noises, and nothing came near.

The passage led up to a dim room with a manhole cover in the floor and a single key lying in a beam
of light, a strange key, which I took. Under the manhole was an aqueduct that had carried water for
the Empire once, and at the end of it was a water slide, and at the bottom of the slide was the
damp passage, and on the floor of the damp passage was my torch, exactly where I'd left it.

---

West of there, in a junction, a great rock stood with an elvish sword set into it. I couldn't pull
it free. I didn't try very hard. I had the feeling it was not meant for me yet.

West again, at the edge of a cliff where sunlight came pouring down through a hole in the earth two
hundred feet above, giant trees had grown, an oasis in the middle of the Underground Empire. A rope
hung over the edge. I climbed down to a ledge where a big chest lay locked among the boulders, and
nothing I had would open it, and I couldn't climb back up.

So I waited, and a man appeared at the top of the cliff and looked down at me. "Hello, down there!
You seem to have a problem. Maybe I can help you." He chuckled in an unsettling sort of way. If I
tied the chest to the rope, he said, he'd drag it up, and then he'd be happy to help me up too. He
laughed again.

I didn't trust him. I tied the chest to the rope anyway. He hauled it up and was gone a long time,
long enough that I began to think I'd been robbed and left to die, and then the rope came down
again and a voice called "Are you still there?" with a coarse laugh, and he pulled me up. He'd
taken every valuable out of the chest. "You've been a good sport!" he said. "Here, take this, for
whatever good it is! I can't see that I'll be needing one!" And he handed me a plain wooden staff
from the bottom of the chest, and walked off admiring his loot.

I kept the empty chest. You'll see why.

---

At the foot of the cliff, the path ran south to the shore of the Flathead Ocean, the great
underground sea that few adventurers ever reach and fewer return from. A heavy surf, a thick mist.
I had been standing there only a minute when the whole dungeon shook with a great tremor, as if
something deep in the earth had turned over in its sleep, and debris came pattering down all around
me.

And out of the mist, passing slowly along the shore, came an old boat like an ancient Viking ship,
and on its prow stood an old and crusty sailor, peering out over the water.

Every adventurer in the Empire has said "Hello, sailor" at some point, to some wall or some empty
room, as a joke. Nothing ever happens. I said it anyway.

The seaman looked up and steered toward the shore. "I have waited three ages for someone to say
those words," he cried, "and save me from sailing this endless ocean. Please accept this gift!" He
threw something onto the sand, and sailed away west singing a lively but somewhat uncouth sailor
song. It was a little vial.

---

East of the shore lay the Land of Shadow, low hills under no light at all, where I heard quiet
footsteps that didn't belong to me. Then a cloaked and hooded figure stepped out of the shadows in
front of me with a brightly glowing sword, and at that same instant, from nowhere, the elvish sword
from the rock at the junction appeared in my hand, blazing.

We fought. It was the only real fight I had in all that dungeon, and I don't like to remember it. I
was hit, more than once. But the figure was hit too, and at last it was badly hurt and couldn't
defend itself, and it stood there swaying in front of me.

I pulled back its hood.

It was my own face. Weary and wounded, with a faint smile coming to its lips. And then, as I
watched, the face began to change, very slowly, into the face of an old, wizened person, and then
the image faded, and the body with it, and only the cloak lay on the ground.

I put down the sword. I picked up the cloak and wore it. I didn't know whose it was any more.

---

The earthquake had changed things. An arch had collapsed, and a hole had opened at the Great Door,
and through it I came to the Royal Museum of the Empire. In the Technology Museum stood a golden
machine with a seat and a dial and a button. A Temporizer, the plaque said. The Royal Jewel
Collection was next door, and its plaque said that Lord Dimwit Flathead had presented the crown
jewels in 777, Great Underground Empire time. The dial on the machine said 948, which is now.

So I pushed the machine, with some effort, through the door into the jewel room, and set the dial
to 776, a year before the collection opened, and got in, and pressed the button.

Everything I carried was gone. The machine was gone. The jewel room was half finished, and through
the door I could hear voices, and one loud and grating voice above the rest. "Very nice! Very nice!
Not enough security, but very nice! Now, Lord Feepness, pay attention! What we need is a dam, a
tremendous dam to control the Frigid River, with thousands of gates. We shall call it ... Flood
Control Dam #2. No, not quite right. Aha! It will be Flood Control Dam #3!" "Pardon me, my Lord,
but wouldn't that be just a tad excessive?" "Nonsense! Now, let me tell you my idea for hollowing
out volcanoes..."

Lord Dimwit Flathead. I had climbed through one of his hollowed-out volcanoes in a hot-air balloon.
I had stood on his dam. And there he was, through a door, talking.

The guards marched away. I took a golden ring from the unfinished collection, the only thing there
not yet guarded, and walked into the unfinished museum next door, where the golden machine sat in
its own time, a non-working model donated by Frobozzco. I lifted its seat and found a hollow under
it, and hid the ring there. Then I set the dial to 948 and rode it home. And when I lifted the seat
in my own time, the ring was still there, where it had been waiting for one hundred and seventy-two
years.

---

South of the museum was the Royal Puzzle.

I am not going to describe the Royal Puzzle, because I can't. It is a square of small rooms whose
walls are blocks of sandstone you can push, sliding them along, so that every push closes one way
and opens another, and somewhere in the middle of it lies a book, and the only way out is a ladder
that has to be got under the hole you came in by. I pushed walls for what felt like a whole day.
South wall, east, south. West wall, twice. Once I pushed the north wall three times until it
wouldn't move any more. When I finally climbed out with the book, my hands were raw and I did not
ever want to see sandstone again.

---

Then I went back to the carved room where the old man had pointed, and through the secret door to a
long hall where a beam of light ran across the corridor. I set the empty chest down in it, and the
beam was blocked. In the next room I pushed a button, and at the end of the hall a great mirror
swung open on a panel, and I stepped inside it.

It was a wooden box, built inside the mirror, with poles and panels of different woods, and a
compass rose carved on a T-bar. I raised the short pole and pushed the white panel, and the whole
structure turned, counterclockwise, southwest, south. I lowered the pole. I pushed the pine panel,
and it opened onto the last hallway.

Two stone statues stood on either side of that hall, holding bludgeons. The Guardians of Zork.
Everyone who has ever tried to walk between them has been smashed flat. I opened the sailor's vial,
and drank it in one gulp, and nothing seemed to happen. Then I walked between the Guardians, and
they did not see me, because there was nothing there to see.

At the end of the hall was a great wooden door with a barred panel. I knocked. For a long time
nothing happened. Then the little panel opened, and through the bars I saw the wrinkled face of an
old man.

He smiled broadly. The door opened without a sound. "I am the Master of the Dungeon!" he said.

I knew him. I had given him bread.

---

He followed me through his dungeon, leaning on his staff, to a parapet over a great circle of fire,
where a sundial with a dial and a button stood at the wall. The book from the Royal Puzzle opened
itself to a page with a picture of eight small rooms around a circle of flame, all identical save
one, which had a bronze door leading to a magnificent room bathed in golden light. "The Dungeon and
Treasury of Zork," it said.

I turned the dial to 4 and pressed the button, and asked the Dungeon Master to wait for me there.
"I will stay," he said. I went into the prison cell across the corridor, and behind me, in the
south wall, was a bronze door.

"Dungeon Master," I called, "turn the dial to 8 and push the button."

"If you wish," he said.

The cell moved. It is hard to describe a whole room moving around you. The door behind me was
closed now, and the bronze door was still there in front of me, and when I put the strange key from
the lake shore into its lock, the key seemed to mold itself to the lock's shape, and turned.

---

The Treasury of Zork. Chests of precious jewels. Mountains of zorkmids. Rare paintings and ancient
statues. On the wall a map of the whole Empire, with every trove of treasure marked on it, and
several superior scenic views. On a desk at the far end, stock certificates for a controlling
interest in FrobozzCo International.

The Dungeon Master appeared beside me as I stood there gaping.

"Now that you have solved all the mysteries of the Dungeon," he said, "it is time for you to assume
your rightly-earned place in the scheme of things. Long have I waited for one capable of releasing
me from my burden."

He tapped me lightly on the head with his staff, and mumbled a few well-chosen spells, and I felt
myself changing, growing older, more stooped. For a moment there were two identical mages standing
among the treasure. Then the other one dissolved into a mist and disappeared, with a sardonic grin
on his face.

I understand it now. The old man asleep in the carved room. The man at the top of the cliff who
took the treasure and left me the staff. The figure in the shadows with my face, which turned into
an old man's. The face behind the barred door. They were all him, testing me, every one of them. Or
they were all me. I am honestly not sure there is a difference any more.

I have the staff now. I have his cloak. I have the vast lore of the Empire at my command, and a
thirst to use it, and a dungeon full of puzzles and a great many years to wait.

If you come down the endless stairs one day with a brass lantern in your hand, look for an old man
asleep in a corner. Wake him up. Be kind to him. Bring him some bread.
