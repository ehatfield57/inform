SUSPENDED
Seventy Cycles

I have not moved my body in a very long time. I am not sure I still have one.

That was the job. Somebody on Contra had to be the failsafe, and it was me. They put me in a
cryogenic capsule deep under the planet's surface, in the heart of the complex that runs the world
above: the weather, the farms, the transit lines that carry thirty million people through their
days. Three great Filtering Computers keep all of it in balance, Alpha and Beta and Gamma, and as
long as they stay in balance, I sleep. If anything ever went wrong with them, the complex would
wake me, and I would put it right. Not with my hands. With theirs.

I had six robots, and a cryolink to each of them. I could see only what Iris saw, hear only what
Auda heard, touch only what Waldo touched. I was a mind in a box, wired into six machines. That was
all I was.

And then, one day, something went wrong.

---

FC ALERT, said the voice in my head. Planetside systems are deteriorating. FC imbalance detected.
Emergency reviving systems completed. You are now in control of the complex.

Then Sensa, who perceives magnetism and the flow of electrons in the walls the way you or I
perceive weather: Seismic aftershock detected ten meters north of Beta FC. Tremor intensity 9.7.
Projected damage: connecting cables in Primary and Secondary Channels.

An earthquake. The cables joining the three Filtering Computers had been torn. Up on the surface,
the weather was going mad and the crops were dying, and people were already starting to die, and
every cycle I spent thinking about it, more of them would.

All robots, report locations.

They answered one by one, and I want to introduce them properly, because by the end of that day I
loved every one of them, and two of them gave their lives for a world they had never seen.

Iris, in the Weather Monitors. Iris was my eyes, the only robot who could see, and she flirted with
me shamelessly, called me "good looking", though she had never seen me and I am fairly sure I am
not. But she couldn't see anything that morning. Her visual function was nonfunctional. The quake
had broken her.

Waldo, in Gamma Repair. My hands. Patient, strong, a little slow, the one who could actually hold a
tool and use it.

Sensa, in the Central Chamber. My sense of the invisible: magnetism, current, the hum of machinery
behind the walls.

Auda, in the Entry Area. My ears. Cheerful. "I hear you loud and clear."

Poet, also in the Central Chamber. Poet perceived electricity and could diagnose its flow, and he
had been programmed, by some engineer with a sense of humor, to speak in verse and quotation. "All
life's a stage," he'd say, setting off on an errand, "so just consider me a player. See you."

And Whiz, in the Advisory Peripheral. The one who could talk to the complex's computers, and who
reported his position in coordinates.

Six robots. Three broken cables. Thirty million people. And, as I learned soon enough, a clock.

---

I've been told since that what I did that day is called critical path planning. At the time it felt
like playing six games of chess at once, blindfolded, with the boards on fire.

I sent Poet to the Weather Control Area first, because the weather was killing people fastest. He
set the faulty dial back to a hundred, which bought time. It didn't fix anything. I sent Sensa for
a ramp, a dense wedge of metal that would let the robots climb between the complex's two levels,
where a step had been left by the quake. I sent Waldo to meet her and take a grasper, a little
container of parts, so that he could fix Iris. I sent Auda down to Gamma Repair, where I would need
her later, and Whiz to the Secondary Channel, where I would need him much later.

Waldo found Iris in the Main Supply Room and opened her panel. He replaced a rough device that had
shaken loose in the quake with a rough part from the container.

"OOOH!" said Iris. "That felt good! Close my panel, big boy."

He closed it. "You never looked so good," she told him. And suddenly I could see.

---

There was a machine in the Main Supply Room that could reset the Filtering Computers. It was broken
too, of course. Everything was broken. Waldo started on it: pulled out a burned chip and a fried
one, put in a red one and a yellow one from the parts he carried, pressed a button that popped open
a front panel. Inside it, beside a fourteen-inch cable he needed, was a fuse. Iris took the fuse.
On the front of the panel were eight raised circles, each printed with three letters. FOO. MUM.
BLE. BAR. KLA. CON. BOZ. TRA.

To reset the Filtering Computers, the machine wanted a code. Two of those circles, in the right
order. And the only place the code was written was on a little sign in the Primary Channel, next to
Alpha FC.

The Primary Channel was on the far side of the Cavernous Room. And at the fifteenth cycle a second
tremor had shaken the lower level, and something in the Cavernous Room had spilled. Acid. Any robot
that passed through it from then on would carry the acid with it, and it would eat them alive.

---

I sent Poet.

I want to be honest about that. I knew what would happen. I had him ride the little transit car out
to the Biology Laboratory first, to fetch a TV camera, and ride back, while the other robots
worked. And then I sent him to the Primary Channel, through the Cavernous Room, with the camera.

"All life's a stage," he said, and went.

"I fear I'm about to become one with the cosmos," he said a while later, from somewhere in the dark.

"Warning: I detect the presence of the other worlds."

He reached the Primary Channel. "As much as I can be anyplace, I'm here." He plugged the camera in.
"We're on location, all systems go." I told him to aim it at the sign. And through the link, Iris,
who was standing back in the Main Supply Room, saw what his camera saw, and read it out to me: the
access code the machine needs. It says BLEMUM.

"Farewell, sweet prince," said Poet. And then the link went dead.

So much for that robot, said the complex. Too bad.

---

Meanwhile Sensa had a cable cutter from the Small Supply Room, where she'd had to stand on her ramp
to reach the holder, and had gone down to Gamma Repair, where Auda was waiting. There was a great
old machine there, its circuitry dead, that the robots called Fred, and behind Fred was the length
of cable we needed. Sensa turned the flowswitch on its front. Fred would not move for one robot. I
tried Sensa alone. I tried Auda alone. "I tried, but I failed," they both said.

So I linked them together: both of them at once, Sensa and Auda, a dual cryolink, two robots moving
as one. And they moved Fred, and behind him was the cable, and Sensa cut it.

Waldo had the fourteen-inch cable from the reset machine. He set off for the Secondary Channel to
fix the break there. That also meant crossing the Cavernous Room.

"Graspers are beginning to malfunction," he reported on the way.

"Warning: internal mechanisms failing."

He made it to the Secondary Channel. And there he stopped, and didn't answer again.

Waldo is non-functional, the complex said. Last known location: Secondary Channel.

I sent Whiz. Whiz, who could talk to computers and had never done anything with his hands in his
life, rolled down to the Secondary Channel and found Waldo, and took the fourteen-inch cable out of
Waldo's dead graspers, and replaced the broken nine-inch cable with it. Then I asked him to drag
Waldo out of there, to the East End, away from the acid. It didn't help Waldo. I don't know why I
asked. I just couldn't leave him there.

---

Sensa was last. She had the twelve-inch cable, and she went through the Cavernous Room to the
Primary Channel, past Poet. "Sensory input filters not working reliably," she said, on the way.
"Warning: unable to process data correctly."

She arrived. And she replaced the broken four-inch cable with the twelve-inch one, with the acid
already eating her circuits.

Approaching balance between all three units, said the complex. Attempting internal stabilization.
Reset codes may be entered now for planetside stabilization.

A moment later Sensa said: "SYSTEM FAILURE: Unable to continue."

So much for that robot.

---

Iris was standing at the reset machine in the Main Supply Room. My eyes. The robot I had fixed
first, because I couldn't do anything at all until I could see.

"Iris," I said, "press the third circle." BLE.

First access code accepted. Enter second access code now.

"Press the second circle." MUM.

Second access code accepted.

And then the complex told me, in its flat way, that all systems were returning to normal. The
weather was slowly finding its balance. The hydroponics were at full capacity. The surface was in
recovery mode. Of thirty million, one hundred and seventy-two thousand people on Contra, eight
thousand had died. Thirty million, one hundred and sixty-four thousand would live.

It had taken me seventy cycles.

The complex told me that this gave me the possibility of being considered for a home in the
country, and an unlimited bank account, and that on a scale of one, the best, to seven, the worst,
my ranking was one.

---

I'm back in the capsule now. Asleep, mostly. I don't know whether they'll ever give me that home in
the country. I don't know whether I'd know what to do with a body if they did.

But sometimes, in the cold, I think about them. Poet aiming a camera at a sign in a channel full of
acid, quoting Shakespeare to the end. Waldo crossing the Cavernous Room with his graspers failing,
carrying the cable that would save thirty million people he would never meet. Sensa holding on just
long enough to make the last connection. Whiz, who wasn't built for it, dragging his friend out of
the dark.

Eight thousand is a terrible number. I am told it is the best number anyone can get. I suppose
that's what it means to be the failsafe: not that nobody dies, but that most people live, and that
you are the one who chose how.

Iris and Auda and Whiz are still out there in the complex, keeping things running while I sleep. If
the ground ever shakes again, they'll wake me.

I hope it doesn't.
