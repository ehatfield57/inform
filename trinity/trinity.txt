TRINITY

All Prams Lead to the Kensington Gardens


I.

The news that morning was bad: sharp words between the superpowers, tanks in East Berlin, and the
BBC muttering about a satellite blackout. It was enough to spoil my continental breakfast. But it
was the last day of my $599 London Getaway Package, and I meant to soak up every penny of it. So I
left the tour bus behind, ditched the camera, and went for a walk through the Kensington Gardens.

An east wind was blowing hard. In the Flower Walk I found a soccer ball half-hidden among the
blossoms, and since nobody seemed to own it, I picked it up. Then I walked north to the Lancaster
Gate.

An old woman stood under a crooked tree there, struggling with an umbrella. A gust tore it out of
her hands and swept it up into the branches. She circled the tree a few times, staring upward, and
a tear ran down her cheek. Nobody but me noticed. After a while she dried her eyes, gave the tree a
vicious little kick, and shuffled away.

I threw the ball. It wedged in the branches beside the umbrella, and the umbrella teetered and
dropped at my feet. When I smoothed it out I saw a touristy slogan printed around the cloth: "All
prams lead to the Kensington Gardens."

At the Round Pond the toy boats, made of paper and sticks, bobbed among the outraged wild birds.
The wind pushed one of them toward me, and I saw that its white sails were wings. It was a folded
paper bird. I leaned over the edge and fished it out.

In a round clearing called the Wabe stood an antique sundial. Seven strange symbols ran around its
edge, with the words TEMPUS EDAX RERUM, time the devourer of all things. The triangular gnomon was
loose. I turned it until it fell off in my hand, and I put it in my pocket. That whole afternoon
felt like following a script I'd never read.

On the Broad Walk a cloud of pigeons circled an old woman selling bags of crumbs. I paid her with
an odd seven-sided coin I found in my pocket, and she pressed a bag and a small coin into my hand.
"Bless yer," she said. When I scattered the crumbs, something glinted red in the dirt: a ruby
bigger than a walnut, cut into sparkling facets. It must have been in the bag.

I bent to pick it up, and a very large bird shot out from behind a tree, snatched the ruby in its
beak, and zigzagged away east through the tourists. If I hadn't known better, I'd have sworn it was
a roadrunner.

"It's time!" shrieked the bird woman.

The east wind died. The trees went still. Then a fresh wind came in from the west.

At the Black Lion Gate a nanny had left her perambulator unattended. I pushed it round past a boy
in headphones blowing soap bubbles, and down to the Lancaster Walk. A notice stuck in the lawn said
Do Not Walk On The Grass. Beyond the grass, between the trees, the Long Water glittered.

The notice said nothing about riding on it. I opened the pram, squeezed myself into it to the great
amusement of everyone passing by, and opened the umbrella.

The west wind took me. I zoomed down the long slope, missing trees and boulders by inches, with the
water coming at me faster and faster. I closed my eyes and pinched my nose.

Crash.

I came to on the shore of the Long Water. The pram was wrecked. The paper bird had been crushed
into a crumpled sheet. I gathered up my things, closed the umbrella, and stood up, and that was
when I saw the missile.

It hung in the sky over the park, almost motionless, and it was falling toward the Long Water very,
very slowly. I waded out into the cool, dark water, and below the missile, just above the surface,
I saw a white door hanging in the air.

As I reached it, the roadrunner fluttered past with the ruby gleaming in its beak and slipped
through ahead of me. Then the colour drained out of the world. The trees, the sky and the sun
flattened into a shell with me at its centre, and the shell came rushing in.

"This way, please," said a voice. "Be quick."

The space around me bent in a direction my mind refused to accept.

"It's a perfectly legitimate direction," said the voice, coolly amused. "Now come along."


II.

I stepped out of a door in a toadstool the size of a house, into a golden meadow full of
dragonflies, and the door faded away behind me.

From a low hill I looked out over forests, marshes and plateaus under a sad, brassy sun, with giant
toadstools growing everywhere by the hundred. In the east, rising thousands of feet above the
trees, stood a vast triangle. Its tip cast a long shadow across the wood. While I watched, a meteor
streaked across the sky, and a moment later the ground shook with a roar like thunder.

A voice followed me through that place. It never showed itself; it only whispered in my ear,
usually with a pun.

In the South Bog I found a rotting log glowing with phosphorescence. It fell to pieces at my touch,
leaving one big splinter that still glowed, and I carried it as a lantern. A narrow stair climbed
the long side of the triangle. At the top, on a tiny freezing platform, was a sundial much like the
one in the Wabe, with a threaded hole in its face and a wide brass ring around its edge. My gnomon
didn't fit. The thread ran the wrong way.

The answer was in a hedge garden to the south: a great pergola of arborvitae with a glass sculpture
inside, signed Felix Klein 1849-1925. I climbed into the pergola, pulled a silver axe out of a
trunk at the top, and came down the other side. Everything was reversed. The inscription now read
5291-9481 nielK xileF. So was the thread on my gnomon. I climbed back up the stairs and screwed it
into the dial. It went in neatly, and a long metal lever rose out of the platform. "The Gnomon
Conquest," remarked the voice.

Then I went back through the pergola once more, so that the world, and I, were the right way round
again.

Turning the brass ring moved the sun: the whole sky swung like a time-lapse film, and I let go with
a cry before I learned to turn it one click at a time. Each of its seven symbols sent the shadow to
a different toadstool and opened the white door in its stem. Slowly I understood that every door
led to a place where a bomb was about to go off.


III.

The sixth door stood on a moor beside a foggy river. I stepped through it into empty air, fourteen
hundred feet above a small city, falling at seventy miles an hour.

I opened the umbrella. It held, and I drifted down past railway lines and streets full of bicycles
and horses, and landed with a crunch in a children's sandpile. There was a long school building
with paper birds and flowers hung in its windows. Children were chasing dragonflies. Teachers, worn
out, were digging an air-raid shelter beside one that was already finished. In that shelter I found
a spade.

A little girl tripped in the sand, laughing, and then saw me and nearly screamed. But her eyes
found the umbrella and stayed on it. I gave her my crumpled paper. "Origami," she said, with a
little bow, and with a few quick folds she made it into a bird again. Then I gave her the umbrella.
Her mouth made a little O. She handed back the paper bird, smiled shyly, and carried her prize down
into the shelter.

The bird began to twitch in my hand. Its wings unfolded like petals and kept unfolding until it was
the size of a car. I climbed onto its crackling back, and it lifted me over the city while, far
overhead, I heard the drone of approaching aircraft. The white door waited in the sky. I didn't
look back.

The fifth door was in the herb garden behind a cottage on a bluff. Inside the cottage an iron
cauldron steamed. On a pedestal lay an enormous book of commands. Its last page was headed "How
Wabewalker happens upon a Book of Hours, and begins to study it," and ended with exactly what I had
just done: E. OPEN DOOR. READ BOOK. "Awk! Read book," croaked a magpie in a birdcage. I let the
magpie go and kept the cage, and I took a rotten clove of garlic from the rubbish pile and dropped
it into the cauldron.

Through the garden door it was bitterly cold. A loudspeaker on a steel enclosure counted down in
Russian, and the frozen tundra crawled with rodents, all racing the same way. I followed them to a
cliff over an Arctic sea, where they poured over the edge by the hundred. Lemmings. One was stuck
in a crack in the rock. I pulled it out, it bit me, and I put it in the cage.

With the axe I chopped down an oak at the edge of a chasm and pushed it over, so that it fell
across to a mesa where another toadstool grew. Its door opened into a tin shed full of pipes and
pressure tanks. A red button opened the doors onto a tropical lagoon at dawn. A grey fin circled
the water. A tiny islet offshore had a single palm tree on it, and the tide was rising. "Gnomon can
tether time or tide," muttered the voice.

The fin burst from the water in a mouthful of teeth, but it was only a dolphin, chattering and
standing on its tail. A coconut dropped from the palm, and the tide carried it off the islet. I
pointed at it, and the dolphin caught it and tossed it at my feet.

Under a cliff there was a hive of giant bees. I reached in for honey and was stung on the hand, and
reached in again and was stung on the forehead, and came out with a fistful of honey. "One more
like that, and you've had it," sniggered the voice. I ran, and the bee followed me straight into
the jaws of a Venus flytrap. Click. At the cottage I washed the honey off in the boiling cauldron,
split the coconut with the axe, and let the milk drip in.

The third door was in an ossuary at the back of a barrow, and a barrow wight guarded the way. It
shied from the light of my splinter, covering its eye with a claw. Among the bones I found a
skeleton key, still clutched in a dead hand.

The door led into a tunnel of cables beside a great buried cylinder, where a technician had left a
lantern and a walkie-talkie. A little lizard, a skink, fled from my splinter into a crevice. I left
the lit lantern in the next tunnel and jammed the splinter into the crevice, and the skink, caught
between two lights, ran in circles at my feet until I pocketed it. On the way back through the
barrow I put the key in a hole in the wall and turned it. The floor opened, and I slid down into an
ice cavern behind the waterfall while the wight howled behind me.

In the ice cavern I brought down an icicle with the axe, froze it hard again at the top of the
triangle, and ran it to the crater where the meteor had fallen. A lump of glowing metal lay there.
The icicle burst into steam against it, and then the lump was cool enough to hold.

On the promontory sat the boy from the Inverness Terrace, giant now, still in his headphones, still
blowing bubbles. I climbed into his dish of soapy water. When he dipped his wand, I went with it,
and when he blew, I floated away in a bubble of rainbow film.

The second door was at the waterfall. I squeezed the bubble through it, and found myself five
hundred miles above an Arctic sea, the film around me freezing solid but holding, black sky and
stars all around. The white door shrank away and vanished between the horns of a crescent moon, and
somehow I knew that what I had to do, I had to do in its light. A satellite drifted into view. The
meteor metal in my arms pulled toward it. I took the skink out of my pocket and squeezed it until
it stopped moving. Then the lump tore loose and fastened itself to the satellite's hull, and the
bubble with it. "Where gnomon has gone before," whispered the voice. Far below, a missile was
climbing out of the clouds, and the satellite's thrusters turned it to face the missile. When the
white door came round again, I broke the bubble with the axe and was blown through it as the
plutonium heart of the satellite erupted behind me.

At the cottage I dropped the skink into the cauldron. It boiled and roared. I ran outside, and the
blast knocked me flat. When I went back in, the book and pedestal were cinders, and at the bottom
of the cauldron lay an emerald.

The seventh door lay across the river. In the cemetery above the waterfall I levered open a granite
crypt with the spade. The corpse inside, some great explorer judging by the crypt, wore a grey
shroud, a red boot and a green boot, and a bandage holding its jaw shut. I took them all and put
them on. When the bandage came away, the mouth fell open, and in it was a silver coin.

The green boot had a little recess in its toe. I pressed the emerald into it, and the leather
swallowed the jewel like melting wax and sprouted a tiny pair of wings at the heel.

At the river a dory came out of the mist with a dark oarsman at the stern. Grey shades rose out of
the sand and boarded, each paying a silver coin. The oarsman looked at my shroud with silent
approval, took my coin, and rowed me across. On a fog-bound islet, the roadrunner sat on top of the
last toadstool with the ruby in its beak. "Gnomon is an island," giggled the voice. The roadrunner
nodded at me and hopped through the open door.


IV.

The door opened into a metal shack on top of a tower in the desert. A five-foot sphere sat on a
bracket in the middle of the floor, studded with bolts and cables that all ran into one box. A
paperback lay in the corner, with a slip of cardboard in it for a bookmark. Men's voices outside
talked about breakers and lines; a searchlight filled the shack with glare; a motor revved below
and faded away. I was alone at the top of the tower, in the grey before dawn, beside the first
atomic bomb.

At the foot of the tower the roadrunner was waiting on a locked wooden box. With a skillful toss it
dropped the ruby onto the box for me, then leapt down and danced around my feet as if it was glad
to see me. I pressed the ruby into the red boot, and that boot grew wings too. After that, every
step carried me across the desert in a rush.

Northwest along the road stood an abandoned jeep with a radio in it. The dial was set to 73. I set
the walkie-talkie's slider to the same number, raised the aerial, and switched both on. "Roger,"
the walkie-talkie hissed. "Displacement, report." From then on, I could hear the whole test.

A rattlesnake waited in the assembly room of the ranch house where the bomb had been put together.
I ducked into a closet, shut the door, and in the dark let my lemming out of its cage. When I
opened the door, it ran straight into the snake's fangs, and the snake dragged its prize away. I'm
not proud of that. In the house I found a screwdriver under the brown paper on the workbench and a
steak knife in the kitchen cupboard. Through the static the walkie-talkie began to play "The
Star-Spangled Banner," and a voice said, "It is now zero minus twenty minutes."

I fished a pair of binoculars out of the reservoir with the lantern. Behind a shed I spied on the
main shelter, crowded with technicians and racks of equipment. A thin man in a porkpie hat stood in
the doorway, smoking his pipe and looking up at the overcast sky. Inside, on a box like the one
under the tower, a steel key sat in an open padlock. I pointed. The roadrunner shot under the
jeeps, into the shelter, past every technician, and came back to lay the key at my feet.

The key opened the box under the tower, and inside was a circuit breaker. I lifted it and listened.
"We have 100% continuity loss towerside," the walkie-talkie cried, and in the confusion someone
asked whether the kid had reconnected the ground line on X. I closed the breaker again. "If it dies
again before the sequencer takes over," a voice said, "we're gonna have to scrub."

Then the searchlight, on a blockhouse where a big German shepherd slept against the wall. I left my
bag of crumbs under its nose. The roadrunner found the bag, the dog woke up barking, and while
everyone was busy chasing that buzzard, the light swung away from the tower and I climbed it.

Back in the shack, the other side of the bookmark had a diagram on it, with a legend scribbled
across the bottom: RD=GND BL=DET ST=POS WH=INF. Red was ground. I unscrewed the panel on the box
with the screwdriver, pulled the light chain, and looked in at four wires: red, blue, striped and
white. Then I waited, with the knife in my hand, while the countdown came through the walkie-talkie
between bursts of music.

"Zero minus one minute. Sequencer will assume control in fifteen seconds."

"Zero minus forty-five seconds. Commence auto-sequence."

"Zero minus fifteen seconds. Fourteen. Thirteen. Twelve. Eleven."

I slid the blade under the red wire and pulled. It stretched, frayed and split, and a shower of
sparks threw me onto my back.

"X-unit just went out again," someone shouted. "Kid's board says it's the ground. We're lettin' it
go, Able. The sequencer's running."

"Congratulations," said the voice in my ear. "You should be proud of yourself. This gadget would've
blown New Mexico right off the map if you hadn't stopped it." It chuckled. "Of course, there's the
problem of causality. If Harry doesn't get his A-bomb, the future that created you cannot occur.
Not to worry, though. Nature doesn't know the word 'paradox.' I wouldn't be surprised to see a
good-sized bang every time they shoot off one of these gizmos. Just enough fireworks to keep the
historians happy."

"Three," said the walkie-talkie, very faint. "Two."

The space around me bent again. It wasn't as frightening the second time.


V.

I was standing at the Palace Gate. Perambulators were rolling up the Broad Walk, and the east wind
was blowing. In the Flower Walk there was a soccer ball half-hidden among the blossoms.

At the Lancaster Gate an old woman was struggling to open an umbrella under the crooked tree. The
wind tore it from her hands and swept it up into the branches. She circled the tree, and cried a
little, and kicked it, and shuffled away.

I stood and stared at the umbrella. When the sirens started, they scattered like leaves, and I
still couldn't take my eyes off the umbrella swinging in the branches.

"It's time," said a gentle voice.

I bent down and petted the roadrunner, waiting impatiently at my feet. Then I hurried off to find a
soccer ball. The slogan kept running through my head as I went, over and over, in time with my
steps.

All prams lead to the Kensington Gardens.
