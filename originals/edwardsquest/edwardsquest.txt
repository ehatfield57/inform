EDWARD'S QUEST
The stable lad and the Raven Knight

It was a crow that woke me. It landed on the stable roof with a thump like a dropped boot, and I
sat up in the hay with my heart going, and then I heard the shouting from the village.

"The crown! The king's crown has been stolen!"

By the time I had picked the straw out of my hair and climbed down the ladder, everybody in
Haverlock knew the story, and most of them were telling it wrong. But the bones of it were these.
In the night, Sir Morvane the Raven Knight had come over the castle wall on black wings, or on a
black horse, or on a black cloud, depending on who you asked, and he had carried off the golden
crown of good King Hal to his tower beyond the river. Every knight in the kingdom had already
ridden out after it. None of them had come back. They said Sir Morvane only had to look at a man to
turn him to stone.

Nobody thought of asking me. I'm Edward. I muck out the king's stable and sleep in the hayloft
above it, and the only thing anybody ever asks me is whether I've done the water buckets yet.

I stood in the stable for a while, in the warm horse-smelling dark, and thought about it.

Clover watched me from her stall. Clover is the oldest horse in the king's stable, grey all over,
with a sway in her back like a hammock. The knights wouldn't be seen dead on her. She's the only
one in the place who has ever been kind to me without wanting something.

"Somebody's got to go," I told her. She blew through her nose, which I decided meant yes.

A brass lantern hung by the door. I took it. There was a spare leather strap on one of the tack
pegs, and I took that too, not because I had a plan for it but because I have learned that a stable
lad with a strap in his pocket is never entirely without hope.

---

The first thing I did on my great quest was climb a tree.

The castle's kitchen garden has an old apple tree in the middle of it, and every apple a person can
reach from the ground was picked weeks ago. But I know that tree. I've been climbing it since I was
six. I went up into the crook of it, in among the leaves and the bees, and there, near my hand,
hung one perfect red apple, the last one, as if it had been waiting for me.

From up there I could see over the castle wall. North, past the cottage roofs, lay the Darkwood,
dark even in the morning. And far beyond the Darkwood, thin as a splinter against the sky, stood a
tall black tower.

I climbed down with the apple and took it to Clover. She ate it in two bites, juice running down
her grey chin, and then she nuzzled my shoulder and followed me out of her stall. After that,
wherever I went outdoors, there was a clopping behind me. I didn't even have to lead her. I have
never had a friend like that before.

---

The village green was full of geese and gossip. Nobody paid me any attention, which was how I liked
it. I went to the well in the middle and turned the crank, because I was thirsty, and up came the
bucket, and in the bottom of the bucket something glinted. A silver coin. Somebody's wish, I
suppose. I fished it out and set it on the rim, and when I picked it up there was a loud POP behind
me, and a cloud of purple smoke, and a voice coughing inside it.

That was the first time I met Donald.

He was a tall, thin old man in a robe covered with stars, most of them stitched on crooked, and he
had a long white beard with what looked like a bird's feather caught in it. He waved the smoke away
and squinted at the sky. "Spell of light!" he cried, and pointed his wand at the sun.

It began to snow. Only on me. For about ten seconds.

"Hmm," said Donald, squinting at the place where I was. "Without my spectacles I can't see what I'm
pointing at." And with another POP he was gone, and I was standing on the green in the sunshine
with snow melting down the back of my neck.

I found out later that he is the king's own wizard, and that everyone in the castle is quite fond
of him and stays well out of his way.

---

The coin went to Mags at the Crooked Tankard. The Tankard has a crooked roof beam and a crooked
landlady, and I mean that kindly, mostly. Mags bit my coin, grinned at me with all four of her
teeth, and tucked it away in her apron.

"What's that for, then? A pie?" She looked at the lantern in my hand. "No. Ah, that old lantern
wants lighting, I'll be bound." She untied the tinderbox from her belt and gave it to me. "Mind the
hay!"

I struck a spark, and the wick caught, and the lantern glowed warm and yellow. I felt, for the
first time, like somebody going somewhere.

I took the lit lantern down into the Tankard's cellar, because I had heard Mags complaining that
there were mice, and in the corner, between the casks, I found a little wicker trap with a grey
mouse inside it, looking extremely sorry for itself. I didn't know yet what I wanted it for. I just
had the feeling you get on a quest, that everything you pick up is going to matter. I put the trap
in my pocket with the strap.

---

The chapel bell has a rope that hangs right down into the nave, and Brother Ambrose was asleep in a
pew underneath it, and I'm afraid I rang it anyway. BONG! The whole village jumped. Brother Ambrose
did not wake up. A great cloud of bats came pouring out of the belfry, and in the middle of all the
flapping something small and heavy dropped down and clanged on the stone beside my foot.

An iron key.

There is an iron grate in the chapel floor, and everyone knows what's under it: the crypt, where
Sir Aldric the Bold is buried, the greatest knight Haverlock ever had. Children dare each other to
touch the grate. The key fitted. I opened it and went down the cold steps with my lantern held high.

Sir Aldric's tomb stood in the middle of the crypt, with his stone likeness lying on the lid,
holding a shield up in front of him as if he were still keeping something off. And there, propped
against the tomb, was the shield itself. It was dented and dull and grey with dust, but it was his.
I said sorry to him, out loud, and took it.

---

Brom the smith was sitting on his anvil, looking at his cold forge the way some men look at an
empty mug. His big bellows had a tear down the side, and a bellows with a tear in it is just a
leather bag.

I knew then what the strap was for. I laid it over the tear and laced it tight the way the old
stableman taught me to mend a girth. Brom watched me without a word. Then he got up, gave a great
laugh, and pumped the bellows, and the forge roared up white-hot.

"That's the job, lad! Now, what can I do for you?"

I gave him Sir Aldric's shield. He whistled, long and low. "Sir Aldric's own," he said, and he put
it in the fire and hammered out the dents and worked it on his grindstone until the sparks flew
like a swarm of bees. When he handed it back to me, it shone like a mirror. I could see my own face
in it, open-mouthed, with a bit of straw still stuck in my hair.

"Whatever you're doing with that," said Brom, "do it well."

---

At the edge of the Darkwood there was another POP and another cloud of purple smoke.

"Ah! Edward, is it?" said Donald, peering at me. "Or a hat stand?" He waved his wand, and my ears
turned bright blue. "Oops. That was meant to be a spell of courage." The blue faded slowly. "Have
you seen my spectacles anywhere, my boy? I think an owl took them."

And he was gone again, and I stood there with my ears tingling, thinking about owls, and about a
grey mouse in a wicker cage.

The path into the Darkwood was dark and winding, with branches meeting overhead, and Clover clopped
along behind me, snorting at the shadows. On the west side of the path stood an oak so old that its
heart had rotted away into a hollow you could have stabled a horse in. High up, far out of reach,
was a big messy nest, and in the nest sat a large tawny owl, glaring down at me with eyes like two
gold coins. Something on the nest beside it glinted.

I want to say that I felt sorry for the mouse. I did. I opened the little cage, and the mouse
darted out across the roots, and the owl came down like a dropped stone. There was a very short,
very sad squeak. The owl flapped back up to its nest in a hurry, and in its hurry it knocked
something over the edge, and it fell, flashing, into the leaves at my feet.

A pair of gold spectacles.

I picked them up and wiped them on my shirt, and waited, because I had a feeling. Sure enough: POP.

Donald squinted at me, and then at the spectacles in my hand, and gasped. "Are those... could those
be... MY SPECTACLES?"

I gave them to him. He put them on, and blinked, and blinked again, and his whole face changed.

"Great galloping gargoyles, I can SEE!" He looked at me properly for the first time. "Oh! You're
not a hat stand at all." He shook my hand, rather too hard. "Thank you, my boy, thank you! If ever
you need me, just call. Call DONALD, and I'll be there in a puff."

---

North of the Darkwood the river runs broad and fast. There's a ford, but the water was deep and
pulling hard, and I could see at once that it would have had me off my feet and halfway to the sea.
On the far side, a road ran north to the black tower.

Clover came up beside me and looked at the water, and then at me.

I climbed onto her broad grey back. She snorted, and waded in, and picked her way across that
roaring water as if she had been doing it every morning of her life. On the far bank I slid down
and put my arms round her neck. The knights wouldn't be seen dead on her. I hope they were watching.

---

The tower was so tall it seemed to lean over me. Ravens circled the top of it. Its great iron-bound
door was shut, and across the door glowed a crimson rune, like something burned into the iron with
a hot finger. I pushed. It didn't move. Of course it didn't.

"DONALD!" I shouted.

POP. He appeared in a neat puff of purple smoke, adjusting his spectacles. "A sealed door, eh?
Let's have a look." He peered at the rune, tutted at it like a schoolmaster with a bad essay, and
tapped it with his wand. "Spell of Unsealing!"

The rune flickered, sputtered and went out. With a long iron groan, the great door swung open.

"There you are," said Donald. Then he stopped smiling. "Mind how you go in there, my boy. Sir
Morvane's gaze turns men to stone. Do keep that shield of yours handy." And he was gone.

I made Clover wait outside. I don't think she minded.

---

The hall inside was black stone, cold and echoing, with a staircase winding up into the dark. At
the far end was a black chair, and in the black chair sat Sir Morvane.

He rose. His armour was the colour of a raven's wing, and it made no sound at all when he moved. He
looked at me for a long moment, a stable lad with straw in his hair and a lantern in one hand, and
I think he almost laughed. Then, slowly, he raised his visor.

Behind it, two eyes began to glow a terrible green.

I didn't decide anything. My arm decided. It came up on its own with Sir Aldric's shield on it, the
shield Brom had polished until I could see my face in it, and the green fire struck the mirror of
it and flashed straight back into the Raven Knight's own eyes.

He gave a terrible cry. His black armour went grey, and then greyer, and the cry stopped short, and
he was a statue of solid stone, frozen with his visor up and his mouth open. Outside, the ravens
screamed and scattered.

I stood there a while, shaking. Then I went up the stairs.

The top of the tower was windy and covered in raven feathers. Far away to the south I could see
Castle Haverlock, as small as a toy. And on a nest of black feathers lay the golden crown of good
King Hal, shining in the sun.

---

Clover carried me back across the ford. We went back through the Darkwood, past the hollow oak,
where the owl was asleep and looked very pleased with itself, and out across the green, where the
geese hissed at us as usual, and up the road to the castle gate.

The guard at the portcullis had a halberd and a long face, and he opened his mouth to tell me to
clear off, and then he saw what I was carrying. His eyes went as round as the owl's. "The king's
crown!" he shouted, and the portcullis rattled up so fast it nearly took his hat off.

The courtyard went quiet. Every servant in the castle stopped to stare at me as I walked through to
the throne room, with Clover clopping behind me until somebody very politely took her to the
kitchen for a carrot.

King Hal was sitting on his throne, looking glum, the way kings do without a crown. When he saw
what I held out to him he leaped up, which kings are not supposed to do. "My crown!" He set it on
his head, where it sat slightly crooked, and clapped me on the back so hard I nearly fell over.

"Edward the stable lad," he boomed, so that the whole hall could hear, "you have done what all my
knights could not. Kneel."

I knelt. He touched his sword to one of my shoulders, and then the other.

"Arise, SIR Edward!"

There was a puff of purple smoke, and Donald appeared beside the throne, throwing confetti. The
confetti turned into butterflies, and the butterflies turned into more confetti, and everybody was
cheering. "Splendid!" cried Donald. "Splendid! I knew you could do it." He leaned toward me and
lowered his voice. "Well. I hoped."

---

I still sleep in the hayloft, most nights. Sir Edward or not, somebody has to see to the horses,
and Clover won't let anyone else do it. The knights all came home in the end, when the Raven
Knight's spell broke and they stopped being statues, and some of them were quite cross that a
stable lad had got there first. I don't mind.

Now and then, when the village is quiet, there's a POP in the stable and a cloud of purple smoke,
and Donald comes and sits on a bale of hay and tells me about spells. His spectacles are fixed on
with a strap now, a leather one. I laced it myself.
