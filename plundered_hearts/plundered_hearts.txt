PLUNDERED HEARTS
The Falcon and the Lady

I was on my way to my father when the pirates came.

He was in the West Indies, and I, being his daughter and therefore every bit as stubborn as he, had
taken passage on the Lafond Deux to join him. I had a cabin, a reticule with smelling salts and a
banknote in it, a cotton frock and a pair of kid slippers, and no idea at all what the world was
like.

I learned that night. A mast came crashing down to the deck. Fire glowed through the porthole.
Somewhere above me a rough voice cried, "Cap'n Jamison! We've got 'em!" and another answered, "The
Falcon! The Falcon conquers!", and I knew what that meant, because every lady who had ever read a
newspaper knew it. The Falcon was the most dreaded pirate in the Caribbean.

There was a coffer under my bed that some fleeing officer had left behind. I took it. I didn't know
why. One should always have something heavy to hand.

---

The door burst in, kicked by a hulking man with a face blackened by soot and blood. "Me name's Andy
Crulley," he sniggered, "and ladies learn to love me." His breath was thick with rum. I hit him
with the coffer. I hit him again. It didn't stop him; it only made him angrier, and he dragged me
toward him.

Then he grunted, and stiffened, and slumped into my arms, and collapsed at my feet.

A tall form filled the shattered doorway, one fist still raised. Broad shoulders. A scar along the
jaw. A ragged shirt, patched breeches, high boots. Eyes as blue as the sea. I knew him at once: the
dreaded Falcon, scourge of the sea. My fate was sealed. I met his eyes and waited for the worst.

He bowed.

"Well met, my lady," he said, and his voice was cultured, his smile warm. "I am Captain Nicholas
Jamison, known in these waters as 'The Falcon.' Your father has sent me." He saw my glare and
laughed. "Yes, you are like your sire. You needn't believe me untried." And he handed me a letter.

It was in dear Papa's handwriting. Lafond, the governor of St. Sinistra, was a viper with the smile
of a saint, and his most particular enemy. I was in the gravest danger. Papa had sided with
Nicholas Jamison, a sea captain and a gentleman whom circumstances had driven to piracy, and I
could trust him. And Papa was riding to someone's rescue: "there is another for whom I fear."

"My lady," said the Falcon, "may I offer my protection and my ship until your father is free?"

I said yes. On the deck, among the fires and the screaming, a sailor called Davis seized me and
held a knife to my throat, and Captain Jamison drew his rapier and pulled me free and killed him in
one smooth motion, and I fainted, as I believe I was entitled to, into the Falcon's arms.

---

For two days I was a prisoner aboard his ship, the Helena Louise, in a cabin with a large cupboard
pushed against the door, brought my meals by a peg-legged old cook called Cookie who was deaf as a
post. I saw nothing of my rescuer.

Then he came, all red velvet and white lace, tying a ribbon onto his rapier, dressed to attend a
ball. They had anchored off St. Sinistra. He would go to Lafond's dance that night and find my
father and free him. He told me why he hated Lafond: years before, his brother and his brother's
bride had sailed for a new life in Virginia, and pirates had struck, and taken the women, and a man
of Lafond's description had shot his brother in the back. Lafond had bought himself respectability
since. "But he shall never buy peace," said Nicholas, and bowed, and left.

He came back once more, to give me a jewelled brooch from his share of the coffer. The clasp was
broken. "I'll have it repaired," he said, and folded it into my hand, and looked at me a moment too
long. If the men suddenly left the ship, he said, it was only to help him. Rodney would stay with
me.

I did not trust that at all.

---

I slipped out past the cupboard and went exploring, which a lady certainly should not do. Down in
the crew's quarters I found a little bottle of laudanum and a bit of broken mirror, and I heard
voices. Crulley, awake and vengeful, had a plan. The men were going ashore. And down in the hold,
behind a wire cage, a fuse was burning toward the powder.

In a sleeping cupboard I found a boy's clothes, breeches and a loose shirt. In the captain's
quarters I hurled the coffer through the tall stern window in a shower of glass, and stripped down
to my chemise, and put on the breeches and the shirt. I looked a little fat. I did not care. A rope
ladder hung down outside from the poop deck. I climbed onto the ledge and caught it, and climbed up
the side of a pirate ship, in the dark, in breeches.

On the forecastle I raised the lever on the winch and let the anchor go. In the galley I took
Cookie's dagger. On the main deck stood two open barrels, one of rum and one of water. I cut a rag
from my old frock, soaked it in the water, crept down through the canvas hatch, and threw it over
the gate of the cage, and by the grace of God it landed on the burning end of the fuse and put it
out.

Then I climbed into an empty cask, cut the line that held the stack of them, and rolled off the
deck and into the sea.

There was a slab of salt pork stuck to the bottom of that cask. I peeled it off and kept it. I had
learned by then to keep everything.

---

The cask washed ashore on St. Sinistra in the night, and I found my way through the forest to a
little folly in Lafond's garden. Through a loose bamboo slat I saw Nicholas, and I told him about
Crulley, and he swore, "God's blood!", and then he did something I had dreamed of and dreaded since
the cabin door burst open: he pulled me into his arms. I leaned into him, breathless and eager. I
will not say more about that.

In Lafond's kitchen, a woman looked up from the fire. "You from the village, boy? Dost know Lord
Dimsford?" She sighed at my surprise. "So he sent you. I am his Lucy." Lafond had caught her
eavesdropping, and now she couldn't leave the grounds. She was frightened. She wanted my father to
come and get her. She slid a garter down her leg and gave it to me. "Give this to him. He'll
recognize it." It seemed the other one my father had ridden to rescue was a lady.

I climbed a vine to an upstairs bedroom, and there, laid out as if it had been waiting for me, was
a ball gown of creamy silk. It fitted perfectly. I had an invitation, too, from the coffer, made
out to a "Miss Davis." The butler read it and bowed. "Enjoy the dance, Miss Davis." The sea of
spectators parted to let me into the ballroom.

---

I danced with Nicholas, and then I danced with Lafond, who told me he was still king of these
waters and wore an ancient gold ring carved with gargoyles and curling tongues. I did not like the
way he looked at me. Then I slipped away to the library.

Behind a portrait in Lafond's library there was a secret stair. The trick was a hat on a knob and a
book called a Treatise of Power and a painted island on a globe: when you pressed St. Sinistra, the
portrait slid aside. Down below were Lafond's dungeons. In the guards' bunkroom I found a key and a
horn. In an anteroom lay a crocodile, between me and the cells. I put three drops of laudanum on my
salt pork and gave it to him, and he ate it and went to sleep.

And in a cell, behind a locked door, was a tall bony man who came out of the darkness. "Papa!" I
cried, and ran into his arms.

"Why, hullo, my dear," he said, hugging me tightly. "Nick certainly took his time about fetching
you."

He would rescue Lucy. I was to find Nicholas. The four of us would meet on the beach. "Zounds!" he
muttered, all the way through the maze of passages.

---

But when I came back through the portrait, they had taken Nicholas. Dragoons were dragging the
Falcon out of the foyer in chains. Someone had betrayed him. Crulley, I would have bet my life.

And then the butler came to tell me that Lafond would see me. In his bedroom.

There was a table with a flagon and two goblets, a green one and a blue one. I filled the blue
goblet and, while Lafond's back was turned, squeezed the rest of the laudanum into it. A beam of
moonlight came in at the window, and I rolled my bit of mirror in it until it shone, and from far
out on the dark water the Helena Louise flashed back.

Lafond came to the table smiling. "Which glass was mine? I seem to have forgotten." He pointed to
the green goblet. "Is this it?"

I nodded.

"You must think me very naive to fall for such a trick," he said. "I saw you pour something into
one of these." And he switched the goblets, and put the green one into my hand, and kept the blue
one for himself, smiling most evilly. "Now you will drink from the cup intended for me."

I drank. It was only wine. He didn't trust his own cleverness either, so he called in the butler to
taste the blue goblet first, and the butler, poor man, reported nothing odd and went back to his
post outside the door, where a little while later I heard a sound like someone slumping against a
wall.

I won't tell you what Lafond did next, except that it was what such men do, and that he had put a
dish of cold chicken and a bowl of fiery spices on the table, and I took a pinch of those spices,
and blew them straight into his face. He sneezed, and wept, and knocked over the table, and
stumbled cursing out of the room. I stepped over the sleeping butler and ran.

---

Nicholas's men were pouring into the house. Cookie found me in the foyer. "Foller you? Anywheres!"
Back through the library, the hat, the book, the globe, the portrait; down the secret stair, past
the sleeping crocodile, into the dungeon.

Crulley was there, with a whip, and Nicholas hung in chains, half conscious. "Flogs me, will ye,
Cap'n? Taste it yourself, har." A rapier leaned against the wall. I took it. "Be careful," Crulley
snorted, "ye might hurt yer dearie." I had never held a sword in my life. I flicked it across his
chest and barely scratched him, and he stepped back in surprise, and there was a trapdoor in the
floor behind him, and down he went. I closed it on top of him.

I waved my smelling salts under Nicholas's nose. He sneezed, and opened his eyes, and saw me. And I
picked the locks of his chains with the pin of the brooch he had given me, the one with the broken
clasp.

"For my brother," he said, "his wife, and your honor, I will kill Jean Lafond."

---

When we came up into the house, Nicholas went looking for Lafond, and found him. By the time I
reached the gallery above the ballroom they were fighting in the middle of the dance floor, with
the dancers pressed back in a wide circle and my father and Lucy and Cookie by the veranda doors.
Nicholas had lost a great deal of blood. Lafond was cool and smiling and in no hurry at all.
Someone cried out "We've got the Dimsford girl, Governor!" which was a lie, but Nicholas looked up,
and slipped in his own blood, and fell, and his rapier went skittering away.

A chandelier hung over the dance floor on a rope, the spare end tied around the gallery railing. I
untied it. I took hold of the rope. And with a cry that came uncalled to my lips, I swooped down
from the balcony, petticoats flying, and slammed into Lafond just as he lunged. His rapier went
into Nicholas's shoulder instead of his heart, and Lafond and I rolled across the floor in a
snowball of petticoats and brocade.

"A gentleman can't kill an unconscious man!" That was Papa, hauling Nicholas away. The dragoons
were coming. We ran for the beach and the ship's boat.

It ended on the beach anyway, as Papa had planned, though not as he'd planned it.

Lafond appeared at the top of the cliff with a pistol in each hand. He gave one to Crulley, who had
somehow climbed back out of the dungeon, and fired the other at Nicholas, and Cookie, dear deaf
Cookie, threw himself in front of his captain and took the ball in the shoulder. "Fight like a man,
Lafond!" Nicholas shouted up the cliff. "You have drawn my blood once tonight. Let me draw yours."

"On one condition," Lafond sneered. "If I win, your lady comes to me. You will have no need of her
when you are dead."

Nicholas looked at the dragoons on the cliff, and turned slowly to me. He said nothing, his face
hard, but his eyes told me everything. "Answer, my darling. Yes or no?"

I nodded once, and never took my eyes off him.

"I accept," said Nicholas, "so long as my people go free if I win." Lafond leaped down off the
cliff like a huge brocaded bat to land in front of him, and his pistol spun out of his hand and
into the sand near me, and I picked it up. They fought in the surf. Lafond drove Nicholas back
toward the foaming water, and Nicholas, bleeding, set his jaw and drove him back again, into the
shadow of the cliff, and then leaped forward, and his rapier slipped into Lafond's ribs like a
snake into its burrow. The governor of St. Sinistra died on the sword. Nicholas took the gargoyle
ring off the dead man's finger, his brother's ring, I understood then, and threw his own cheap copy
into the sea.

High on the cliff stairs, Crulley was loading a pistol.

I poured a lumpy stream of powder into the barrel of mine and tamped it as best I could. Crulley
braced himself against the cliff and levelled his gun at Nicholas. Trembling, I fired. The heavy
pistol roared over the surf. Crulley staggered, and fell from the cliff, and the gun fell from my
nerveless hands.

And time stopped, as Nicholas and I looked at each other. Then he bowed, and offered me his hand,
and I took it, and he pulled me into his arms, and his mouth was on mine.

---

At dawn I stood with him on the forecastle of the Helena Louise, looking north.

"I have nothing left in England," he said. "There I am but a destitute gentleman, wanted for
piracy. This isle holds too many memories; your father may have it. Mayhap my brother's wife yet
lives. I must try to find her, either way." He took me into his arms. "Come with me, my love? To
America. They tell of endless fertile fields, and strange beasts in that wild land. It won't be
easy, but we will have each other. Together, shall we carve a kingdom blessed with fair children
and freedom?"

I put my arms around his waist, and leaned into him, and smiled at the rising sun.

I left England a lady who had never been anywhere. I suppose I am still a lady. But I have climbed
a pirate ship in breeches, and put out a fuse, and outwitted a governor, and shot a man to save the
one I love. My father says I am just like him. Nicholas says that is precisely the trouble.
